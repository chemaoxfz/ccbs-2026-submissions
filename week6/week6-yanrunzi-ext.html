An antenna that spends its coherence on timing

Author: Yan Runzi · Week 6 (2026-10-23) · CCBS 2026

Lecture 12 builds a computer out of binding and then counts what it costs. A molecular NAND needs four dedicated catalytic channels, so a cell with a thousand catalytic types buys about two hundred and fifty gates — roughly a twentieth of the Apollo Block II guidance computer, and a rounding error against a modern chip.1 The lecture then turns to a different question: how a cell holds a decision once it has made one. It never asks whether the cell already contains a device that is quantum-mechanical in the strong sense — one whose function cannot be written down without a density matrix. There is such a device, and this course has met it before: the Fenna–Matthews–Olson complex, the antenna protein of green sulfur bacteria.

The temptation is to call it a quantum computer. It has the ingredients people point at: an excitonic system, room-temperature operation, and long-lived coherences that two-dimensional spectroscopy has actually observed.5, 6 The review I am working from ends its introduction by hoping the complex might serve as "a biological resource of quantum processing."2 This essay takes that hope seriously enough to test it, and argues the opposite. The FMO complex is a quantum device. It is not a quantum computer. What its coherence buys is speed — it sets when the excitation arrives — and not an answer, which is set by the energy landscape and the temperature and barely notices which state the system started in. The evidence for that is a single table in the source, read one way rather than another.

The assignment · what to hand in

One point taken beyond the lecture and pushed. Lecture 12 supplies the frame — build the computing unit, then ask what it costs — and stops at chemical gates. Here the frame is aimed at a candidate the lecture does not discuss at all: a protein whose operation is genuinely quantum-mechanical. The point pushed is that being quantum and computing are different claims, and the parameter sweep in González-Soria, Delgado and Anaya-Morales2 contains the numbers that separate them. Structure follows the previous weeks: the claim, then the object, then the one number, then what would break it.

Part I

The claim, and the budget it runs into

A real quantum device, holding far too little coherence to be a computer.

1A candidate device, and what it is

Eight pigments in a bag, 8.3 nm across, running at room temperature.

The FMO complex is a water-soluble protein of about 150,000 Da that sits between the chlorosome and the reaction centre of green sulfur bacteria.7 It is a homotrimer with a maximum diameter near 8.3 nm; each monomer folds into a bag holding eight bacteriochlorophyll a molecules, spaced about 12 Å apart — close enough that their transition dipoles couple strongly and the excitation is not localised on any one of them. Light absorbed by the chlorosome enters at pigment 8 and its excitonically coupled partner pigment 1, is funnelled through two branches — one through pigment 2, one through pigment 4 and then 7 — and lands on pigment 3, the sink from which it leaves for the reaction centre. Absorption peaks at 830–840 nm. The two best-studied species, Chlorobaculum tepidum and Prosthecochloris aestuarii, have crystal structures at 3BSD and 3EOJ and are 78 % identical over the mature sequence.8, 2

The first thing to get right is that the usual comparison to gate budgets is the wrong comparison, and it is worth saying why, because the wrong version of the argument is the one that is easy to make. Lecture 12's figure of merit is

(1) Gcell  ~  Ncat/4  ~  103/4  =  250 gates,

set against Apollo's 5,600 counted logic gates, a factor of about twenty-two. One is tempted to run the same arithmetic on FMO: eight pigments per monomer, twenty-four per trimer, and call each pigment a device. That count would come out around twenty-four, which the number above would then be invited to dismiss. But this is a category error in both directions. Gate count is not what makes a computer; programmability is. The Apollo computer can be loaded with a different mission; the FMO complex has one Hamiltonian, fixed by its folded structure, and cannot be reconfigured without re-engineering the protein. So count the pigments and you learn nothing. The real question is architectural, and it is the subject of the next section.

The claim

The FMO complex operates in a genuine quantum regime — its excitation is delocalised, its off-diagonal density-matrix elements are non-zero and oscillate, and its transfer is modelled by a non-Markovian open-system equation rather than by rate theory. But quantum coherence and quantum computation are separate claims, and FMO satisfies only the first. Its coherence acts as a timing signal on a single fixed transport step; it does not carry an input-dependent result. The protein is a quantum clock, not a quantum processor.

2Four things a computer must do

FMO fails three of them, and the failure is measurable rather than interpretive.

A physical system computes when its final state carries information about its initial state, through a sequence of operations that preserve the amplitudes long enough to interfere. Written out plainly, a computer needs four things: a preparable input; operations that act on the state without measuring it; coherence that outlives the sequence of operations; and an output that is a function of the input. Anything short of all four is a physical device that is not computing. Table 1 puts FMO against each requirement, and the verdicts are not equally close — the first is nearly met, the third is met by a factor of ten at best, and the fourth fails by a mechanism rather than by a margin.

Rows one and two are the reasons the question is worth asking at all: the protein does have quantum amplitudes, and it is not a classical rate machine. Nothing above says the coherence is absent or negligible. It says the coherence occupies a different place in the causal order than a computer's coherence does.

Part II

The mechanism

The object, the quantity that measures its coherence, and the quantity that measures its output.

3The funnel, and where the coherence lives

A downhill path through eight coupled pigments, with a coherence transient at the entrance.

The source models the monomer as an eight-site excitonic system coupled to a structured phonon bath, and solves it with the hierarchical equations of motion — a non-Markovian method that keeps the bath's memory rather than assuming it is instantaneous.2 Three parameters are swept: the bath temperature T from 77 to 347 K, the bath cutoff γk from 30 to 50 cm−1, and the site reorganization energy λk from 35 to 75 cm−1. That is 150 combinations, of which 121 converged at hierarchy depth 3. The two pigments that matter for the essay's claim are the entrance and the sink, and Figure 1 draws both the route between them and the timescales on which it happens.

The two quantities

Coherence is measured by the l1 norm of the off-diagonal part of the density matrix, which for a density matrix is exactly twice the sum of the absolute values of its off-diagonal entries, and which decomposes exactly into the pairwise coherences of each pigment pair:

(2) Cl1(ρ)  =  minσ∈Γ ∑|ρij − σij|  =  2 ∑i<j|ρij|  =  ∑k<l Ckl  ≤  d−1 = 6.

The identity on the right is the source's own, and it matters here: an eight-site system can hold at most seven units of coherence, so Cl1 is a bounded resource, and the values in Table 2 sit at 1.3 to 2.3 — between a fifth and a little over a third of the ceiling. The output, meanwhile, is a population, and needs no coherence at all to be defined:

(3) η  =  limt→∞ [ ρ33(t) + ρ44(t) ].

Equation (3) is the fraction of the excitation that ends up on pigments 3 and 4 and leaves for the reaction centre; it is the efficiency of the antenna, read as a diagonal of the density matrix after everything has settled. Equations (2) and (3) are the two ends of the argument. One is off-diagonal and transient; the other is diagonal and final.

4Three states in, one number out

The efficiency barely notices which state the excitation started in. The coherence notices enormously.

The source runs the same model from three initial conditions: an excitation localised on pigment 1 (ρ(0) = |1⟩⟨1|), one localised on pigment 6 (|6⟩⟨6|), and a Förster-like mixed state, ρ8FRET, built from the pigment-8 site energies and weights. These are not three states of one physical apparatus being switched; they are three different ways light could have left the baseplate. But they are the closest thing in the literature to three different inputs, and what the model does with them is the crux. Read down the η columns. At λ = 35 cm−1 the six conditions give efficiencies between 0.536 and 0.564, a spread of five per cent, across two species and three initial states. At λ = 65 cm−1 they lie between 0.596 and 0.656, a spread of ten per cent. Now read down Td50% in the same columns: at λ = 35 it runs from 0.048 to 0.397 ps, a factor of 8.3; at λ = 65 it runs from 0.057 to 1.220 ps, a factor of 21.4. The transient and the output vary by two different orders of magnitude in the same set of runs.

The source sees this and reads it as a signature. Its own gloss is that the differences in decoherence and transfer time "constitut[e] a quantum fingerprint in their structure." Strangely, the same paragraph notes that the efficiencies are "very similar at the end … because in their relatives markovian models final populations depend only on the energy spectrum and temperature." Both sentences are correct, and together they state my thesis more compactly than I can. A fingerprint tells you which protein you are looking at. A computation's output tells you what the input was. The efficiency is nearly a function of (λ, T) alone; the coherence dynamics are a function of the protein and the starting state. That is the profile of a clock, or of an identity tag, not of a processor.

Part III

Consequences

What the coherence does buy, and what would have to change for the computing claim to hold.

5The fingerprint is in the timing

If coherence is a clock rather than a bit, the number that measures it should be a duration, and it is.

The claim can be made into a single ratio. Take the time over which the coherence survives, Td50%, and divide by the time the transfer takes, Tt95%. If coherence were carrying anything through the transfer, that ratio would approach one: the amplitudes would have to persist as long as the process they were supposed to influence. If coherence is a transient trigger, the ratio is small. The ratio is also dimensionless and involves no parameter I chose, so it is the honest place to put the argument's weight.

One number, made and checked

Form Td50%/Tt95% for all twelve conditions in Table 2: 0.020, 0.033, 0.078, 0.021, 0.022, 0.060, 0.154, 0.028, 0.071, 0.011, 0.016, 0.054. The range is 0.011 to 0.154. In the best case for the computing hypothesis — C. tepidum, ρ(0) = |1⟩⟨1|, λ = 65 cm−1 — the coherence lasts 15 % of the transfer. In the worst it lasts one per cent. There is no condition in the entire sweep, at any temperature or bath cutoff the study covers, in which the coherence outlives the transfer it is supposed to be influencing by any margin at all, and none in which it approaches the ratio quantum algorithms require: a circuit of N gates needs coherence over N gate times, and here even N = 1 is not reached.

The comparison that makes this a mechanism rather than a curiosity is the one between the spread in the coherence times and the spread in the output. Fix λ and vary species and initial state — the six-way comparison within each half of Table 2. At λ = 35 cm−1 η moves by a factor of 1.05, Td50% by 8.3 and Tt95% by 2.9; at λ = 65 cm−1 the same three factors are 1.10, 21.4 and 3.0. The transient carries between eight and twenty times more variation than the answer does. Across the twelve rows the maximum coherence and the efficiency are in fact slightly anti-correlated (Pearson r = −0.36), though twelve conditions are not twelve independent draws and I would not lean on that coefficient.

Coherence decay occurs on different timescale as the energy transfer, thus questioning the actual importance of quantum coherence. González-Soria, Delgado & Anaya-Morales, 2020, p. 2

My disagreement with the source is narrow and, I think, worth stating precisely, because it is not about the data. The authors report these numbers accurately; the sentence above is theirs, and so is the caution that follows it — that the efficiency may need both coherent and incoherent transfer, and that the rapid decoherence "might be necessary to assure fast unidirectional transfer towards the reaction centre." Where we part is in the last step, the one from "FMO is a quantum system" to "FMO is a resource for quantum processing." Everything in Table 2 argues that the coherence has a job, and the job is to make the transfer fast and one-way. That is a real function, and it is not computation.

What follows constructively is that the correct technological reading of this protein is not as a processor but as a coherence standard. FMO's off-diagonal elements are readable by two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy, reproducible across a homotrimer and across species, and stably expressed at ambient temperature — a combination that engineered solid-state qubits do not offer. Because the sweep shows the coherence lifetime responding systematically to both λ and γ, the complex also works as a bath spectrometer: a fixed molecular object whose decoherence reports the memory time of whatever environment you put it in. Neither application is a computer. Both are things the protein demonstrably does.

A correction the full text forced

I came to this essay from the source's framing and expected a paper about a photosynthetic complex being groomed for quantum information. The sentence that invited that reading is in the introduction: "This may lead to the hopeful application of this system as a biological resource of quantum processing, particularly due to its room temperature performance."2 Reading the body changed the claim three pages later. The processing sentence is a hope in a discussion paragraph with no result behind it; the results are the parametric sweep, and the sweep's own summary paragraph says the coherence and the transfer run on different timescales and that this "question[s] the actual importance of quantum coherence." A review can hold both sentences because it is a review. An essay cannot, and the numbers decide which one to keep.

Part IV

Situating and testing

What would make me wrong, and the concession the timescale argument has to make.

6Where this could be wrong

A coherence lifetime of one per cent of a picosecond is short for a computer and long for an optical field.

The argument above is a negative one and should be stated so that it can be attacked. Four things would move it.

  • A different readout. Efficiency is a population readout — a diagonal of the density matrix. A device could in principle compute in the phases and never show it in the diagonals, and no measurement in this study would see that. My claim is therefore about what has been measured, not about what the protein might be doing invisibly. The refutation is concrete: exhibit an output observable that is not ρ33 + ρ44, depends on the input state at fixed λ and T, and depends on Td50%. Nothing in the literature I have read does this.
  • An efficiency that tracks the coherence. If the output were a function of the transient at fixed landscape and temperature, the anti-correlation would have to reverse and grow. Table 2 is twelve points against that, but they are twelve points from one model at one temperature and two values of λ; a sweep at fixed λ across T could look different, and the source's own 77 K measurements are where long-lived coherence is best established.5
  • The circuit might be reprogrammable in a way I have dismissed. The coupling matrix is fixed by the fold, but the fold is not fixed across species, and the source shows the dynamics change with the sequence. The two mature sequences are 78 % identical, so more than a fifth of the residues differ,2 and the coherence times differ by up to twentyfold. That is a knob — a slow, evolutionary one, but a knob. What it is not is a knob that can be set per computation.
  • Short is not the same as useless. This is the concession the argument owes. The optical period at 830 nm is 2.77 fs. A coherence half-life of 48 fs to 1.22 ps therefore spans 17 to 440 optical cycles — enough for the excitation to interfere with itself hundreds of times, which is exactly why the oscillations are visible at all.6 Coherence that survives a hundred cycles is a serious physical resource. It is simply not a resource that survives a sequence of operations, because the sequence is the transfer itself and the transfer takes the coherence with it.
One number, made and checked

Convert the timescales into cycles, which is the units quantum information actually uses. At λ = 830 nm the field period is λ/c = 830 nm / 2.998 × 108 m s−1 = 2.77 fs. The twelve coherence half-lives of Table 2 span 0.048 to 1.220 ps, which is 17 to 440 periods. Compare the gate requirement: a quantum circuit of N gates needs the coherence to survive N gate intervals. Even reading the ratio in the most generous way — one optical period per gate — FMO supports of order 102 elementary operations. That is not nothing; it is roughly the coherence budget of an early solid-state qubit. It is also two to three orders of magnitude short of anything that would factor a number, and, unlike a qubit, its operations cannot be addressed.

What I would want to see before changing my mind is a measured quantity that is not a population, that varies with the initial state at fixed λ and T, and that varies with the coherence lifetime rather than against it. Until then the honest summary of the FMO complex is the one Lecture 12's framing suggests but does not say: a cell does contain a quantum-mechanical device, it is excellent at the one computation it was selected for, and that computation is not a computation — it is a transfer that the coherence makes fast.
