Week 6 Shadow prices turn an FBA optimum into a local decision rule

Author: Peng Su week6 (2026-10-25) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: Lecture 13 already shows that only an active metabolic limit changes the optimum; Based on this point, throughout any region with the same active constraints, the exact derivatives λj=∂μ*/∂Uj form a linear decision rule, while the identity of the active constraint is itself a Lecture-12 threshold computation.

From molecular decisions to metabolic decisions

Lecture 12 constructed a linear threshold unit whose decision follows from weighted inputs. Lecture 13 used objectives and constraints to select an FBA optimum, and it showed that relaxing an uptake bound can raise the optimum and then produce a plateau, while Section 15 explained that extra enzyme helps only when its capacity is active. Then based on these results as background, the main extension is to determine whether their slopes and changes of active constraint form an explicit computation.

Attach λj to the nutrient-uptake upper bound Uj. “Shadow price” was used for this uptake-bound dual value while some FBA literature uses the phrase for mass-balance duals instead. In a growth-maximizing model, λj=∂μ*/∂Uj is the return from one extra unit of capacity. While the active constraints remain unchanged,

Δμ* = Σj λjΔUj.

This is exact throughout an active-set region, rather than merely a small-change approximation. It has Lecture 12's form: nutrient changes are inputs, dual values are weights, and a benefit threshold gives a yes-or-no decision. Crossing a boundary selects a different linear rule.

A two-nutrient calculation makes the weights switch

Consider a deliberately small biomass reaction that requires two carbon units and one nitrogen unit per unit biomass. Let UC and UN be the available carbon and nitrogen uptake capacities. The FBA problem is

maximize μ,   subject to   2μ ≤ UC,   μ ≤ UN,   μ ≥ 0.

The answer can be read without a solver:

μ* = min(UC/2, UN).

At UC=10 and UN=8, growth is 5 and carbon is limiting. One extra carbon unit raises optimal growth by 0.5, whereas one extra nitrogen unit does nothing. The local weights are therefore λC=0.5 and λN=0. At UC=20 and UN=8, growth is 8 and nitrogen is limiting. The weights switch to λC=0 and λN=1.

The identity of the limiting nutrient is itself a linear classification. Nitrogen is limiting when UC−2UN>0, carbon is limiting when this expression is below zero. Thus the boundary has weights +1 and −2 with threshold zero. On either side, the dual-value rule is exact. At the boundary, UC=2UN, the ordinary derivative is not unique: the carbon supergradient, equivalently its admissible dual value, can take any value λC∈[0,0.5], with λN=1−2λC.

Where the classifier analogy breaks

FBA should not be called as a literal molecular computer. The dual-value rule is a sensitivity statement about an optimized model, not a biochemical mechanism that measures λ. It remains exact while the same constraints stay active, but a sufficiently large nutrient change can cross a boundary and replace the entire linear rule. At the boundary the supergradient is set-valued rather than a unique derivative. As Lecture 13 already notes, larger networks can also have alternate internal flux optima with the same objective value.

The calculation also inherits FBA's steady-balance assumption and omits the time needed to express enzymes or switch pathways. Objective dependence is background from Lecture 13: survival, ATP maintenance and secretion can value the same uptake bound differently. The extension is narrower: each unchanged active set gives an exact weighted rule, and crossing its threshold selects another.
