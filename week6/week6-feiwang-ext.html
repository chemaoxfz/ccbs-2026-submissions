Week 6 — Extension: Can Enzyme Saturation Destroy Perfect Adaptation?

Author: wangfei · Week 6 (2026-10-15) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: An incoherent feedforward loop may have the correct wiring for perfect adaptation, but saturation of its removal pathway introduces a nonzero steady-state input–output gain and restricts adaptation to a suitable kinetic regime.

The point I am adding

Lecture 11 shows that a stable incoherent feedforward loop (IFFL) can restore its output after a sustained input change when the direct and indirect input effects cancel. I want to distinguish having the right network topology from actually achieving accurate adaptation. The same circuit can lose this function when its molecular reaction law changes.

Consider an input w that both produces an output x and increases a mediator e that removes x. With bilinear removal, the minimal model is

dx/dt = w − xe
de/dt = w − e

At a positive steady state, e* = w and x* = 1 for every w > 0. Thus, an input step can produce a transient response but cannot permanently shift x*. This is the ideal cancellation discussed in class.

Now suppose the removal arm becomes saturated with respect to the mediator. Keeping the same arrows in the network, replace the removal flux by a Michaelis-type effective dependence:

dx/dt = w − (K + 1)xe/(K + e)
de/dt = w − e
K > 0

Here K is a dimensionless saturation scale; the factor K + 1 normalizes the output to x* = 1 at baseline w = 1. The saturating law is a phenomenological model of mediator-limited removal, rather than an assertion that x itself is Michaelis–Menten saturated. It keeps the same IFFL topology while changing only one reaction order.

Work it

The steady-state equations give e* = w and

x*(w) = (K + w)/(K + 1)

In the ideal bilinear model, the logarithmic gain d ln(x*)/d ln(w) is zero. For the saturating model it becomes

G(w) = d ln(x*)/d ln(w)
= w/(K + w)
= 1 − K/(K + w)

The removal flux has order K/(K + e) with respect to e, while the production flux has order one with respect to w. Thus the unmatched fraction is exactly the residual steady-state gain.

At K = 0.5, the mediator-side removal order is 1/3 at baseline; the output gain is 2/3, not zero.

For an input step from w = 1 to w = 2, the fractional change in the settled output is

Δx*/x*(1) = 1/(K + 1)

The following original figure plots the exact steady-state expressions, not fitted data or numerical ODE trajectories.

These are dimensionless model calculations, not experimental measurements. The equilibrium remains locally stable: the Jacobian in the order (x, e) is triangular with diagonal terms −(K + 1)e/(K + e) and −1, both negative at e* > 0.

Hence the problem is imperfect adaptation despite stable dynamics, not the loss of stability.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

It would be too strong to conclude that enzyme saturation always destroys adaptation. The result depends on where saturation occurs, how the mediator couples to output removal, and whether other reactions compensate.

For example, ordinary Michaelis–Menten saturation in the substrate x, rather than in mediator e, need not introduce input dependence if production and enzyme abundance still scale proportionally. Saturating an input-responsive branch can also be offset by matching nonlinearities elsewhere.

My model also assumes a freely adjustable mediator, first-order mediator turnover, a stable positive equilibrium, and an effective saturation law that remains valid throughout the tested input range.

Strong binding-induced sequestration, feedback, or an input-dependent K could change the prediction. Therefore, the experimentally testable claim is narrower: a mismatch between the effective input order of production and the mediator-side removal order creates an adaptation error in this specific IFFL model.

A useful experiment would impose graded input steps, measure the mediator and output after transients decay, estimate the local slope d ln(x*)/d ln(w), and ask whether it rises as the removal arm saturates. This tests molecular operating regime rather than classifying the network from its wiring diagram alone.
