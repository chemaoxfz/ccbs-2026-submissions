Lecture 12 — Extension: A decision is a boundary, not a state count

Author: fanglipei (20261203497) · Lecture 12 (20 October 2026) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: MultiFate's three stable states are three outcomes, but choosing among them is a crossing problem — a pulse commits only if it carries the state across the separatrix, the minimum duration diverges at the saddle-node, and equal doses can produce opposite fates.

The point I am adding

Section 9 of the lecture ends with an experimental fact: a six-day doxycycline pulse moves a sorted A-high population into a both-high state that survives withdrawal of the drug. Appendix A2 reproduces the direction of that switch with eq. (25), using a five-lifetime pulse at full induction. Both are existence arguments. They show that a transient signal can select a state; they do not say what makes a pulse sufficient. The lecture calls the duration and fold-change illustrative, so the sufficiency condition is left open.

I want to fill that gap with the model's own phase plane. The three stable states are usually read as three decisions, and the combinatorial count (3, 7, 15, …) as the size of the decision library. But a decision is not a point; it is the boundary between points. A pulse commits the state exactly when, at the moment it ends, the state lies on the far side of the separatrix of the A-high basin. This turns "how much signal is enough" into a geometric question, and the geometry has two consequences: the minimum duration diverges as the pulse amplitude approaches the saddle-node, and the same integrated dose can land on opposite sides of the boundary.

The rest of this note computes that boundary from the lecture's equations, checks that the code reproduces the lecture's three states, and exhibits two pulses with identical dose and opposite outcomes. The point is not that the experiment is wrong. It is that dose is the wrong control variable for a dynamic decision.

Work it

The model, from the lecture's equations

I use eqs. (24)–(25) exactly, with γ = 1 and the lecture's parameter values. The only change is notation: qA and qB are total amounts divided by KM, and τ is time in units of the baseline removal lifetime.

φ = 2 / (1 + √(1 + 8(qA + qB)/Kd))
CAA = φ²qA²/Kd,    CBB = φ²qB²/Kd
h(C) = Cn / (1 + Cn),    n = 1.5
dqA/dτ = 0.8 + 20 h(CAA) − qA
dqB/dτ = 0.8 + 20 [ h(CBB) + sB(1 − h(CBB)) ] − qB

Here α/(KMδ) = 0.8, β/(KMδ) = 20, Kd/KM = 1, and sB ∈ [0,1] is the control. With sB = 0, Newton's method from a grid returns exactly the lecture's states: (19.985, 0.834), (0.834, 19.985) and (18.694, 18.694), with saddles at (19.761, 4.659) and (4.659, 19.761). The match to the lecture's Fig. 14 is the check that this is the lecture's model and not a lookalike. The A-high start is (19.985, 0.834).

The separatrix and two pulses

The basin boundary is computed by bisection: for each qA, find the qB at which the final state switches from A-high to both-high. It passes through the saddle and sits at qB ≈ 4.6 when qA ≈ 20 (Fig. 2b). The start is far below it. A pulse is now a vertical excursion: sB = σ for 0 ≤ τ < T, then zero.

Take two pulses with the same dose σT = 0.30. Pulse 1 has σ = 1, T = 0.30; pulse 2 has σ = 0.08, T = 3.75. Pulse 1 crosses the boundary at τ = 0.214 and ends at (18.694, 18.694). Pulse 2 peaks at qB = 3.771, which is 0.90 below the boundary at that qA, and returns to (19.985, 0.834). Same dose, opposite fates. The reason is the removal filter: B has lifetime 1, so a short strong pulse accumulates B faster than it is removed, while a long weak pulse settles at a quasi-steady B level that never reaches the boundary.

The commitment boundary

Repeating the bisection over σ gives the commitment boundary T*(σ) (Fig. 3a). At full induction T* = 0.214; at σ = 0.2, 1.24; at σ = 0.1, 3.15; at σ = 0.05, 29.8. The boundary has a vertical asymptote at σc = 0.0482, the saddle-node value where the A-high state and the saddle merge. Below σc no duration commits, because the induced B level never reaches the separatrix. The threshold dose D* = σT* is not constant: it falls from 2.29 near the fold to 0.214 at σ = 1. A constant-dose line therefore crosses the boundary — for D = 0.30, at σ ≈ 0.11 — which is the general form of the two-pulse result.

Near the fold the divergence has a universal exponent. In the normal form ẋ = r + x², with r ∝ σ − σc during the pulse, the time from the old stable point x ≈ −√μ to the ghost is T = (1/√r)[π/2 + arctan(√(μ/r))], which tends to π/√r as r → 0. Hence T* ∝ (σ − σc)−1/2. The computed points give a log–log slope of −0.52 over the nearest decade (Fig. 3b). This is the saddle-node of Lecture 11 seen at the nonlinear level: the eigenvalue goes to zero, and the time to leave the ghost diverges.

One number is worth stating plainly. At full induction the model needs T* = 0.214 lifetimes, while the lecture's illustrative protocol uses 5. The lecture says the protocol is not fitted, so this is not evidence against the experiment. It says that the model's full-induction threshold is about twenty times shorter than the illustrated pulse, which places the experiment well inside the committed region. That is what an existence demonstration wants.

The code

The computation is a two-variable RK4 integration plus a bisection on the pulse duration. The core is below; the figures use the same functions.

import math

ALPHA, BETA, KD, N = 0.8, 20.0, 1.0, 1.5
Q0 = (19.98541, 0.83420)          # the A-high state, from the lecture

def phi(qA, qB):
    return 2.0 / (1.0 + math.sqrt(1.0 + 8.0*(qA + qB)/KD))

def hill(C):
    z = C**N
    return z / (1.0 + z)

def rhs(q, sB, gamma=1.0):
    qA, qB = q
    p = phi(qA, qB)
    CA, CB = p*p*qA*qA/KD, p*p*qB*qB/KD
    return (ALPHA + BETA*hill(CA) - gamma*qA,
            ALPHA + BETA*(hill(CB) + sB*(1.0 - hill(CB))) - gamma*qB)

def rk4(q, T, sB, dt=0.004):
    n = int(round(T/dt))
    for _ in range(n):
        k1 = rhs(q, sB)
        k2 = rhs((q[0]+dt*k1[0]/2, q[1]+dt*k1[1]/2), sB)
        k3 = rhs((q[0]+dt*k2[0]/2, q[1]+dt*k2[1]/2), sB)
        k4 = rhs((q[0]+dt*k3[0],   q[1]+dt*k3[1]),   sB)
        q = (q[0] + dt*(k1[0]+2*k2[0]+2*k3[0]+k4[0])/6,
             q[1] + dt*(k1[1]+2*k2[1]+2*k3[1]+k4[1])/6)
    return q

def ends_high(sB, T):
    q = rk4(Q0, T, sB)
    q = rk4(q, 120.0, 0.0, dt=0.01)   # relax after the pulse
    return q[1] > 5.0                # committed (high B) vs A-high

def T_star(sB):
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0
    while not ends_high(sB, hi):
        hi *= 1.5
        if hi > 5000: return float('inf')   # below the fold
    for _ in range(40):
        mid = (lo + hi)/2
        if ends_high(sB, mid): hi = mid
        else: lo = mid
    return (lo + hi)/2

(0.214, 1.238, 3.154, 4.650)
>>> ends_high(1.00, 0.30), ends_high(0.08, 3.75)
(True, False)

What I would argue against / where this breaks

Noise. The calculation is deterministic. A real cell reads a separatrix through noisy molecule counts; near the boundary, commitment is a probability, not a yes/no. The right object is P(commit | σ, T), and its 50% contour will not coincide with my T*(σ). The lecture's appendix B2 draws the same distinction between a tolerance on the mean and an error probability. My curve is a deterministic first-passage time: it says where the mean trajectory crosses, not where a single cell decides.

Parameters and control. The numbers are conditional on the lecture's illustrative parameters and on the step control. A ramp, a change in removal (protocol two), or a different Hill exponent moves σc and T*. The fold and the −1/2 exponent are robust because they are codimension-one features of a saddle-node; the numerical values are not predictions for Zhu et al.'s experiment.

Reversibility. "Commitment" here means basin membership, and that is reversible: a second pulse can move the state back, as the lecture's protocol two does. If commitment means an irreversible developmental point of no return, the relevant object is a slower variable or a destroyed saddle, not this separatrix. The calculation establishes the cost of choosing a state, not the cost of making the choice permanent.

The state count. This is where I would push the lecture's accounting further, not where I think it is wrong. Counting stable states — 3, 7, 15, 30, 56, 99 — counts the outcomes a circuit can hold; it does not count the difficulty of selecting one. The difficulty is the distance to the boundary and the shape of the basin, which the count does not see. Two circuits with the same number of states can have very different commitment costs. A decision library should be measured by its boundaries, not only by its shelves.
