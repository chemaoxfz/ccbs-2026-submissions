WEEK 6 — Extension: From enzyme saturation to vanishing gradients

Author: Weihan Luo · Week 6 (2026-10-26) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: An enzyme’s saturating response becomes a sigmoid in log-concentration coordinates, giving a concrete way to understand how repeated small derivatives can suppress gradients across neural-network layers.

The point I am adding

An enzyme helps convert substrate into product. When substrate is abundant, adding more barely increases the conversion rate: the enzyme is saturated. Lecture 11, Section 1, describes this using fast binding, dilute enzyme and a fixed enzyme total. Write the substrate total as qS, the binding scale as Kd, and the maximum rate as Vmax. The normalized rate p and its reaction order H are [1]

p=vVmax= qSKd+qS ,H= ∂lnv∂lnqS=1−p

Reaction order measures a percentage response: H = 0.01 means a small 1% substrate increase produces approximately a 0.01% rate increase. Now set a = ln(qS/Kd). Substitution gives exactly the logistic sigmoid used in neural networks, together with its familiar derivative:

p(a)= 11+e−a =σ(a), dpda=p(1−p)

The two sensitivities are related but different: H = 1 − p describes fractional output change, whereas dp/da = pH describes absolute change in normalized output. Near maximum activity, both become small. My extension is to compose sigmoid responses and ask how much of an upstream change survives. For scalar neural layers,

hℓ=σ( wℓhℓ−1 +bℓ) GN= ∂hN∂h0= ∏ℓ=1N wℓhℓ(1−hℓ)

This is the chain rule. Backpropagation multiplies the final loss derivative by these same local factors when propagating it toward earlier layers. Glorot and Bengio studied how saturated sigmoid units hinder gradient propagation and training [2]. The enzyme curve supplies an accessible starting point for that sensitivity calculation.

Work it

I compare two deliberately controlled chains: all weights are 1, and each layer has output p = 0.5 or 0.99 at the reference input. Choosing h0 = p and every bias as ln(p/(1 − p)) − p keeps each layer at that chosen output. Thus the input-output derivative is exactly [p(1 − p)]N. These are local derivatives of two specified networks, rather than a training experiment.

My calculation: saturation compounds across layers.
Output in each layerOne-layer derivativeFive-layer derivative
0.5 (mid-response)0.259.766 × 10−4
0.99 (saturated)0.00999.510 × 10−11

After five layers, the saturated chain transmits a derivative about ten million times smaller. Even the mid-response chain shrinks sensitivity under unit weights; saturation makes the reduction much stronger.

Reproduce the figure and numerical comparison (Python, NumPy and Matplotlib) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def sigmoid(preactivation):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-preactivation))

log_ratio = np.linspace(-6, 6, 241)
response = sigmoid(log_ratio)
local_gain = response * (1 - response)
depths = np.arange(0, 11)
operating_points = (0.5, 0.99)
colors = ("#167d8d", "#b44660")

plt.rcParams.update({"font.size": 10, "svg.fonttype": "none"})
figure, axes = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(7.2, 6.8))
axes[0].plot(log_ratio, response, color=colors[0], lw=2.4,
             label="Normalized enzyme rate = sigmoid")
axes[0].plot(log_ratio, local_gain, color=colors[1], lw=2.4,
             label="Slope with respect to log input")
axes[0].set(xlabel="Log concentration ratio  a = ln(q_S / K_d)",
            ylabel="Response or local gain", xlim=(-6, 6), ylim=(0, 1.04))

for target_output, color in zip(operating_points, colors):
    bias = np.log(target_output / (1 - target_output)) - target_output
    hidden = target_output
    sensitivity = 1.0
    sensitivities = [sensitivity]
    for layer in depths[1:]:
        hidden = sigmoid(hidden + bias)
        sensitivity *= hidden * (1 - hidden)
        sensitivities.append(sensitivity)
    np.testing.assert_allclose(
        sensitivities, (target_output * (1 - target_output)) ** depths,
        rtol=1e-12, atol=0,
    )
    axes[1].semilogy(depths, sensitivities, "o-", color=color, lw=2,
                     markersize=4, label=f"Each layer at p = {target_output:g}")
    print(f"p={target_output:g}: one-layer gain="
          f"{target_output * (1 - target_output):.6g}; "
          f"five-layer gain={sensitivities[5]:.12g}")

axes[1].set(xlabel="Number of sigmoid layers (unit weights)",
            ylabel="Input-output sensitivity |dh_N / dh_0|",
            xlim=(0, 10), ylim=(1e-21, 2))
axes[1].set_xticks(np.arange(0, 11, 2))
axes[1].set_yticks([1, 1e-4, 1e-8, 1e-12, 1e-16, 1e-20])

for axis in axes:
    axis.legend(loc="lower left", bbox_to_anchor=(0, 1.02),
                borderaxespad=0, frameon=False, fontsize=9)
    axis.grid(alpha=0.18)
    axis.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)

figure.tight_layout(h_pad=2.7)
figure.savefig("week6-saturation.svg", metadata={"Date": None})

What I would argue against / where this breaks

The coordinate change establishes the same response function; it does not show that enzymes train by backpropagation. Reaction order and activation derivative also should not be conflated: at low substrate, H approaches 1 while dp/da approaches zero. A biochemical cascade needs its own concentration balances and dynamics. The neural example isolates one mechanism: weights, biases, other activation functions and additional paths change the gradient product, so depth alone does not determine whether learning succeeds. What carries across is the need to track sensitivity through the whole composition.
