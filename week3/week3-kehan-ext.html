Week 3 — Extension: A dimer Hill law is not automatically a total-protein Hill law

Author: Kehan Lang · Week 3 (2026-09-21) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: Fast dimerization and promoter binding give an exact degree-two repression law in free monomer, but the same law can be seriously wrong when written in total protein if dimers store an appreciable fraction of the protein.

The point I am adding

Lecture 6 derives a useful reduction for a dimeric self-repressor: when dimerization and promoter binding are fast, free promoter has the form D = qD/[1 + (R/KH)2]. Here R is free monomer. The squared term has a clear mechanism: two monomers form a dimer, and that dimer binds the promoter. The lecture also cautions that this expression transfers to total protein only when monomers dominate the total.

This essay makes that warning quantitative. A fluorescent measurement or a bulk assay often reports total protein, not free monomer. I ask: if two systems have the same half-repression scale KH, can a degree-two Hill curve in total protein still identify promoter availability? Within the stated fast-equilibrium model, the answer is no. Changing how readily monomers are stored in dimers can move half repression to a much larger total concentration without changing the free-monomer regulatory law.

Work it

Consider a repressor monomer R, free dimer CRR, free promoter D, and promoter-bound dimer CDRR. Reversible dimerization and promoter binding are assumed fast compared with protein production and removal. Their equilibrium constraints are

2R ⇌ CRR,    D + CRR ⇌ CDRR
CRR = R2/Kdim,    CDRR = DCRR/KD.

Promoter material is conserved: qD = D + CDRR. Substituting the two constraints and defining KH = √(KdimKD) gives

D(R) = qD / [1 + (R/KH)2].

This is exact as a function of free monomer in the reduced model. At R = KH, exactly half the promoter is free. The coordinate measured in an experiment, however, may be the total protein constituent count. Each free or promoter-bound dimer contains two monomers, so the appropriate total is

qR(R) = R + 2R2/Kdim + 2qDR2/(KH2 + R2).

The three terms are monomer, free-dimer, and promoter-bound-dimer contributions. They are all nonnegative, and the expression increases with R, so it defines one physical free-monomer value for each total. But it is not generally true that qR ≈ R. The familiar shortcut

Dnaive(qR) = qD / [1 + (qR/KH)2]

therefore needs a storage check, not merely a Hill exponent.

For the calculation below, I set KH = 1 μM and qD = 0.02 μM. I compare two ways to keep their product KdimKD = 1 μM2 fixed. In the monomer-dominant case, Kdim = 100 μM and KD = 0.01 μM, so dimers are scarce near R = 1 μM. In the dimer-storage case, Kdim = 0.10 μM and KD = 10 μM, so a 1 μM free-monomer pool implies 10 μM free dimer.

At half repression, R = KH in both systems. The first case has qR = 1.04 μM; the second has qR = 21.02 μM. Thus the regulatory chemistry has not changed its free-monomer half point, but a total-protein measurement would place that same point about twenty-fold farther to the right.

A Hill-shaped dose-response is not a species label. Here the exponent two is mechanistically tied to dimer formation, and the free-monomer formula remains valid under the fast constraints. What fails in the dimer-storage example is a different step: replacing the unobserved free monomer by total protein. The error is not repaired by reporting the same nominal KH, because different pairs (Kdim, KD) can share that product while storing very different amounts of protein in dimers.

This suggests a practical modeling rule. If a reporter measures total repressor, use the total constraint to reconstruct free monomer before applying a promoter-occupancy law; alternatively, fit the model directly in total-protein coordinates. A steep total-protein curve may reflect dimer storage as well as promoter binding, whereas a poor total-Hill fit does not by itself refute dimer-mediated repression. Thermodynamic promoter models similarly require the molecular states and the measured quantity to be specified, rather than treating a fitted Hill curve as the mechanism itself [2].

What I would argue against / where this breaks

The conclusion is deliberately narrow. It assumes reversible dimerization and promoter binding relax rapidly, one promoter-binding site, a well-mixed compartment, and equilibrium constants independent of protein concentration. Slow dimer assembly, multi-step promoter states, cooperative binding at several DNA sites, irreversible aggregation, or feedback that changes the binding rates require an expanded state model. At low copy number, deterministic concentrations also omit integer dimer counts and promoter-state fluctuations; a chemical master equation would then be the appropriate extension.

Nor does this calculation claim that every degree-two Hill coefficient demonstrates physical dimerization. Different mechanisms can produce similar effective curves. Its more limited result is that even when dimerization is the correct mechanism and fast equilibrium is justified, free and total protein are interchangeable only after checking that the dimer and promoter-bound storage terms are small in the concentration range being interpreted.
