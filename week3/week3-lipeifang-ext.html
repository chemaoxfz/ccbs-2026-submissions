Week 6 - Extension: Why rare stochastic switching becomes exponentially slow

Author: lipeifang · Student ID: 20261203497 · Week 6 (2026-09-21) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: A deterministic bistable model can look permanently locked on an experimental time scale, because the mean noise-induced switching time grows approximately as exp(system size x barrier height), even though the chemical master equation still permits switching.

The point I am adding

Lectures 5 and 6 give two complementary descriptions of a biochemical network: the chemical master equation/Gillespie process describes random integer jumps, while time-scale separation explains how a fast variable can be removed to obtain a reduced model. The next question is what happens when the reduced deterministic dynamics has two stable states. The deterministic equation says that a trajectory starting in either well stays there forever. The stochastic model says something subtler: a sufficiently unlikely sequence of reaction events can carry the system over the unstable state between the wells.

This is called metastability. Each well is locally stable, but not truly absorbing. The important extension is that the escape time is usually not merely “a little longer” at larger copy number. In a broad class of Markov jump processes, it has the large-deviation form

mean escape time   τ(N)  ≈  A(N) exp[N ΔS].

Here N is a system-size parameter (roughly a volume or copy-number scale), ΔS is the stochastic barrier or quasi-potential difference, and A(N) is a slower prefactor. Thus increasing molecule number suppresses relative fluctuations, but it also makes a rare switch exponentially harder. This gives a precise meaning to the lecture's warning that a deterministic limit can miss a biologically important first-passage event.

For a gene circuit, this distinction matters operationally. A circuit may switch many times in a small cell or during a long lineage experiment, while the same circuit appears irreversibly ON or OFF in a larger compartment or over a short observation window. The deterministic fixed points are unchanged; the observable switching statistics are not.

Work it

To make the scaling visible without hiding it inside a simulator, consider the dimensionless overdamped diffusion approximation

dx = -U'(x) dt + sqrt(2/N) dW,    U(x) = (x2 - 1)2/4.

The two stable states are at x = -1 and x = +1; the unstable saddle is at x = 0. The barrier from either well to the saddle is

ΔU = U(0) - U(±1) = 1/4.

Kramers' approximation for the mean escape time from one well is therefore

τK(N) = [2π / sqrt(U''(-1)|U''(0)|)] exp(NΔU)
= (2π/sqrt(2)) exp(N/4) ≈ 4.44 exp(N/4).

Doubling N from 20 to 40 multiplies the predicted switching time by exp(5) = 148.4, even though the drift field -U'(x) and its two stable fixed points have not changed. This is the key quantitative difference between “noise is small” and “switching is impossible.”

What I would argue against / where this breaks

I would argue against the statement “a stable deterministic fixed point means the cell will stay there.” Stability is local information about the drift. It does not determine the tail probability of a long sequence of molecular events. For a switching experiment, the relevant estimator is not only the mean abundance; it is a first-passage distribution or a survival curve. A model can match the mean trajectory and still predict the wrong switching lifetime.

There are also three important limits to the calculation. First, the double-well diffusion is a continuous approximation, whereas the exact molecular process has integer states and reaction-specific propensities. For small copy numbers, I would validate the estimate by running Gillespie trajectories and measuring the empirical first-passage time. Second, when the barrier is not large compared with the noise, the Kramers asymptotic formula and its prefactor can be inaccurate. Third, a nonequilibrium reaction network need not have a scalar potential U; then the barrier is a quasi-potential/action obtained from the master equation or a WKB/large-deviation calculation, not simply a height read from an energy landscape.

Finally, “fast variable” does not automatically mean “irrelevant variable.” Eliminating a fast promoter or RNA can preserve the average production rate while removing the rare event that initiates a switch. A reduction is therefore adequate only after checking the observable and time window, as Lecture 6 emphasizes.
