Eliminating an RNA lifetime

Two clocks race on a single transcript — translation at kp, degradation at γm. Whichever wins first decides how many proteins it leaves behind, and the answer is a geometric packet. This worksheet derives that packet from the two clocks, then asks which of its features survive when the rates are pushed into their limits.

Assignment · Lecture 6, Playground C

Eliminate an RNA lifetime. Let an RNA translate at rate kp and disappear at γm. Use competing clocks to derive its geometric protein packet. Decide what survives if both rates increase at fixed ratio, and what survives if translation stays fixed.

01The object · one transcript, two clocks

An RNA is a race between two exponential clocks

A single transcript is born at time zero. While it lives it translates — each event deposits one protein with rate kp — and it is destroyed at rate γm. Nothing else is resolved: these are composite channels with declared clocks, exactly as Lecture 3's squiggle arrows and Lecture 5's propensity table treat them.

02Derivation · the geometric packet

Run the clocks independently, then read the ladder

Each event that happens to the live RNA is a draw from a race. Translation wins a single race with probability q, after which the race restarts with unchanged clocks (the transcript remembers nothing). The number of translation wins before the first degradation is therefore geometric.

(1) · race probability — exponential clocks tie only with probability zero
q=kpkp+γm,1−q=γmkp+γm.
(2) · the packet — B independent races, terminate on degradation
Pr(B=j)=(1−q)qj,j=0,1,2,…
(3) · moments of the geometric law
E[B]=q1−q=kpγm,Var(B)=B¯(1+B¯).

Why it is geometric, in one line

Condition on the transcript surviving j consecutive races: that is qj. End with one degradation race: 1−q. Multiply — the geometric ladder of the lecture's count-ladder figure, standing on its side.

Completeness check: Σ (1−q)qj = 1, so a finite packet is guaranteed; degradation is reached almost surely. There is no catastrophic "no packet" outcome with missing probability.

Relationship to the lectures

This is Lecture 5's burst addendum (B̄tl = ktl/dm) written as a race, and the exact mechanism behind Lecture 6 §9a's "an ON period produces a geometric packet of mean ρ/β." Averaging out a fast promoter state (Lecture 6) and averaging out a fast RNA lifetime (here) are the same operation applied to different hidden clocks.

What survives in Limit A

The packet is the survivor. Because E[B]=kp/γm is a function of the ratio alone, the entire geometric law — mean, width, and the excess protein Fano 1 + E[B] — is unchanged as both clocks accelerate. The system is a burst process: Poisson-arriving packets of a fixed mean, presentable without resolving any transcript. That is Lecture 6 §9a's burst limit, and Lecture 5's geometric-packet Fano F = 1 + B̄.

What is eliminated: the RNA lifetime 1/γm and every temporal feature of a burst. Each packet is pressed toward an instant — the transcript's history is averaged out, never its size. Bursts stop being visible episodes on the protein timescale.

What survives in Limit B

The survivor is the mean production rate kp·⟨M⟩ and the elementary translation event itself. As γm→∞ with kp fixed, E[B]=kp/γm→0: the geometric packet degenerates to zero (almost always) or one (rarely). Compensating rm keeps protein produced — but now nearly every protein is its own lone event, so arrivals become Poisson and the Fano factor falls to 1.

What is eliminated: the packet and its excess noise. A large width that a mean alone could never reveal (Lecture 5's standing warning) is exactly what dies here. This is Lecture 6 §9a's fast-fixed-rate switching → Fano-one limit.

Both limits preserve the mean protein production rate; they differ in which source of noise is retained. Agreement in the mean cannot distinguish the two reductions — only a Fano factor or a waiting-time statistic can (Lecture 6 §9a).

05Close the contract

Make the observable choose the model

A model is finished when it names what it predicts and what would reject it. The state, event, and observable language of the two lectures closes this problem exactly as the core pages ask.

State
M ∈ {0,1} (transcript alive) and P ∈ ℤ≥0 (protein count). The live transcript is one molecule; zero is a genuine state with no translation clock.
Events
M⇝M+P (jump +1, clock kp); M⇝∅ (jump −1, clock γm). Both composite channels with declared clocks.
Probability
The packet is geometric, Pr(B=j)=(1−q)qj with q = kp/(kp+γm). No CME needed: the two-clock race makes the distribution in one pass.
View & estimator
Three views, three operations: the packet law (exact), the mean & Fano (analytical E[B]=kp/γm; F=1+E[B] in the fast-mRNA limit), and a path (Gillespie). Fano is a time-weighted residence average, reported with its window.
Falsifier
A measured protein Fano plainly below 1 (sub-Poisson) at fixed fast-mRNA rates rejects this model outright — no choice of kp, γm permits it. A Fano above 1+E[B] rejects the geometric-packet assumption (e.g. true promoter bursts, not an RNA-lifetime race). The two limits share every mean, so only a second-moment or timing observable can tell them apart.
