Week 3 — Extension: Can Molecular Degradation Reverse Competition Between Two Stochastic Signals?

Author: wangfei · Week 3 (2026-09-15) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: Two signals can compete for the same limited molecular target, and the faster-binding signal need not dominate if its occupied complex is cleared sufficiently quickly; at low copy number, the dominant signal also fluctuates over time.

The point I am adding

Signals A and B compete for a finite pool of identical targets R. A may bind faster than B, but an A-bound target may also be cleared faster. Does faster association guarantee competitive dominance, or can differential degradation reverse the occupancy bias?

Work it

I test the claim with a minimal stochastic target-competition model, derive its Chemical Master Equation, obtain an analytic steady-state check, and compare it with Gillespie trajectories and a parameter heatmap.

Reaction network

A + R → AR    (kA)
B + R → BR    (kB)
AR → R   (dA)
BR → R   (dB)

The bound signal is cleared and the target is recycled. Therefore:

RT = R + AR + BR.

Free A and B are treated as buffered reservoirs with fixed effective abundances NA and NB. This is a minimal illustrative model, not a fitted biological circuit.

State, jumps and propensities

Let X(t)=(i,j), where i=NAR and j=NBR. Then R=RT-i-j and i+j≤RT.

ChannelJumpPropensity
A binds(i,j)→(i+1,j)a₁=kANA(RT-i-j)
B binds(i,j)→(i,j+1)a₂=kBNB(RT-i-j)
AR clears(i,j)→(i-1,j)a₃=dAi
BR clears(i,j)→(i,j-1)a₄=dBj

Chemical Master Equation

Define Pi,j(t)=Pr{NAR(t)=i,NBR(t)=j}. Probability flows into (i,j) through binding or clearance from neighboring states and flows out through the four reactions:

dPi,j/dt = kANA(RT-i-j+1)Pi-1,j + kBNB(RT-i-j+1)Pi,j-1
+ dA(i+1)Pi+1,j + dB(j+1)Pi,j+1
− [kANA(RT-i-j)+kBNB(RT-i-j)+dAi+dBj]Pi,j.
CME derivation

For A binding, a trajectory arriving at (i,j) must come from (i−1,j), which has RT−i−j+1 free targets. B binding is analogous. AR clearance arrives from (i+1,j) at rate dA(i+1), and BR clearance from (i,j+1) at rate dB(j+1). The final bracket is the total rate of leaving the current state.

Analytic steady-state check

Let α=kANA and β=kBNB. For one target, steady-state flux balance gives:

αPR=dAPAR,    βPR=dBPBR.

Hence:

PAR/PBR = (kANA/dA) / (kBNB/dB).

Association rate alone therefore does not determine stationary occupancy. A faster-binding signal can lose its mean occupancy advantage if its bound state is sufficiently short-lived.

Gillespie direct method

At state (i,j), calculate a₀=a₁+a₂+a₃+a₄. Draw u₁,u₂∼Uniform(0,1). The next waiting time is:

τ = −ln(u₁)/a₀.

The selected reaction is the first channel whose cumulative propensity exceeds u₂a₀. Update the integer state, advance time by τ, and repeat.

Parameter heatmap

The heatmap shows the exact stationary probability Pr(AR>BR) for the minimal independent-target model. Blue favors A occupancy; orange favors B. Near the transition, low copy number makes the outcome probabilistic.

What the stochastic model adds

The deterministic mean describes average occupancy. The CME gives the full state probability Pi,j(t), while Gillespie trajectories show individual molecular histories. At low copy number, AR and BR can repeatedly exchange dominance even when one has the larger stationary mean.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

The model assumes identical independent targets, constant free A and B, mass-action association, first-order clearance, instantaneous target recycling, and no cooperativity, feedback, spatial effects or receptor internalization states. Thus the conclusion is deliberately narrow: within this stochastic target-competition model, differential degradation can offset or reverse an association-rate advantage.
