Does temporal structure of power matter?

Steady versus bursty supply at the same mean input

CCBS · EST 5053 · extension mini-essay · Lectures 5–6 · Week 3 · 2026-09-21

The claim

Equal mean source flux does not guarantee equal function when the carrier pool is noisy, the consumer is nonlinear, or the consumer has a low-energy threshold. In the fast, linear, low-noise limit, steady and bursty supply should become equivalent. A bursty source is therefore a temporal perturbation, not automatically a better or worse energy source.

1Problem

A source can deliver the same average number of carrier molecules in two very different time patterns.

Week 2 treated an auxiliary source as a mean flux. That is appropriate for a deterministic balance, but it hides shot noise and waiting times. Here I ask one narrower question: with the same mean source flux, does a constant stream produce the same consumer output as a compound-Poisson burst stream?

2Claim and assumptions

The primary readout is useful product per unit time, not the carrier pool alone. I assume one well-mixed compartment, a discrete carrier E, a saturable consumer, and a first-order leak. The source does not respond to the pool in this null model.

  • Constant reference: unit births occur at rate J̄; a separate deterministic ODE is a zero-noise control.
  • Unit-event stochastic control: Poisson births of one carrier at rate J̄.
  • Bursty source: bursts of b carriers at rate λb = J̄/b. The mean input is identical.

These three conditions prevent a comparison between deterministic input and noisy input from being mislabelled as a burst-size effect.

3Model

The chemical events are

source: E → E + 1 (rate J̄) or E → E + b (rate J̄/b);
work: E → E − 1 + Y (rate v(E));
leak: E → E − 1 (rate ℓE).

The consumer law is v(E) = VmaxE/(KE+E). For the numerical illustration, J̄ = 4 min−1, b = 5, Vmax = 6 min−1, KE = 10 and ℓ = 0.08 min−1. The deterministic steady state solves

0 = J̄ − VmaxE*/(KE+E*) − ℓE*.

It gives E* = 10.895 and a long-run deterministic consumer flux of 3.128 min−1. The ODE value reported below is the finite-window quantity Y(T)/T from E(0)=0, so its transient value need not equal the steady-state flux. The stochastic assay uses exact Gillespie event times, a 60 min observation window, 20,000 trajectories per stochastic condition, and the recorded seeds 20260921 (unit events) and 20260922 (bursts).

4Computational assay and controls

For the benchmark below I ran 20,000 independent trajectories per stochastic condition with the same parameter draw and the seeds recorded above. The primary endpoint is cumulative product Y(T)/T (min−1). Secondary endpoints are the mean, variance and Fano factor of E, the fraction of the observation window with E<3, and the recovery time after a source interruption. Because v(E) is concave, excursions above the operating point do not compensate for time spent near zero. A different consumer law could reverse the mean effect, which is why the assay must not be summarized as “bursts are always harmful.” The ODE begins at E=0, so its 0.016 threshold fraction is the initial deterministic rise to E=3 divided by 60 min; the stochastic fractions are time-weighted trajectory averages.

6Falsification, limits and next test

Falsifiable prediction

When the consumer is linear, the carrier relaxation time is at least ten-fold faster than the consumer response, and the stationary carrier CV is below 0.1, steady and bursty conditions should fall inside a two-sided 5% relative equivalence margin for Y/T (defined as |μburst−μsteady|/μsteady≤0.05). If a properly powered equivalence test still rejects that margin, the model is missing a hidden nonlinear sink, finite-volume effect or source feedback.

The model omits spatial routing, membrane leakage mechanisms, expression burden, growth dilution and ROS. Gillespie trajectories are predictions, not single-cell data. A future experiment should match both mean input and mean E* in a second analysis; otherwise a pool-mean difference and a fluctuation effect are confounded.

Reproduction code

The following self-contained JavaScript uses the stated parameters, initial condition E(0)=0, observation window and seeds. It implements fourth-order Runge–Kutta for the ODE and the direct Gillespie method for the two stochastic sources. Running it with Node.js 24.20.0 returns the rounded values shown above (small last-digit differences can result from a different JavaScript runtime).

const P={J:4,V:6,K:10,ell:.08,T:60,N:20000};
function rng(seed){let x=seed>>>0;return()=>{x^=x<<13;x>>>=0;x^=x>>>17;x>>>=0;x^=x<<5;x>>>=0;return(x>>>0)/4294967296;};}
function q(a,p){const i=(a.length-1)*p,j=Math.floor(i),f=i-j;return j+1<a.length?a[j]*(1-f)+a[j+1]*f:a[j];}
function ode(){let e=0,y=0,h=.01,f=e=>P.J-P.V*e/(P.K+e)-P.ell*e,v=e=>P.V*e/(P.K+e);for(let t=0;t<P.T-1e-12;t+=h){const z=Math.min(h,P.T-t);let k1=f(e),l1=v(e),e2=e+k1*z/2,k2=f(e2),l2=v(e2),e3=e+k2*z/2,k3=f(e3),l3=v(e3),e4=e+k3*z,k4=f(e4),l4=v(e4);e+=z*(k1+2*k2+2*k3+k4)/6;y+=z*(l1+2*l2+2*l3+l4)/6;}return y/P.T;}
function sim(b,seed){const R=rng(seed),aY=[],aL=[];for(let n=0;n<P.N;n++){let t=0,E=0,Y=0,L=0;while(t<P.T){const a0=P.J/b,a1=P.V*E/(P.K+E),a2=P.ell*E,A=a0+a1+a2;if(!A){L+=P.T-t;break;}const dt=-Math.log(1-R())/A,end=Math.min(P.T,t+dt);if(E<3)L+=end-t;if(t+dt>=P.T)break;t+=dt;const u=R()*A;if(u<a0)E+=b;else if(u<a0+a1){E--;Y++;}else E--;}aY.push(Y/P.T);aL.push(L/P.T);}aY.sort((x,y)=>x-y);const m=aY.reduce((x,y)=>x+y,0)/P.N,s=Math.sqrt(aY.reduce((x,y)=>x+(y-m)**2,0)/(P.N-1));return[m,s,q(aY,.05),q(aY,.5),q(aY,.95),aL.reduce((x,y)=>x+y,0)/P.N];}
console.log('ODE',ode());console.log('unit',sim(1,20260921));console.log('burst',sim(5,20260922));
