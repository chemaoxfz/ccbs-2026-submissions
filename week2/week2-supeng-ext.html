Week 2 — How long must fast growth last to erase memory?

Author: Peng Su · Week 2 (2026-09-12) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: A transient growth pulse erases a positive-feedback circuit's memory only when its strength and duration push the system across the switching threshold restored after normal growth resumes.

1 · The question beyond the lectures

Lecture 3 already supplies the demo of positive-autoregulation model, and Lecture 4 then explains its three crossings and the tangency that creates or destroys a stable-threshold pair. After recomputing those fixed points under different conditions, this mini essay further addresses the unresolved temporal question: if faster growth is only a pulse, when does a temporary loss of the ON fixed point become permanent loss of memory?

A static bifurcation diagram assumes that a parameter is held indefinitely. A bacterium instead encounters finite episodes: a nutrient upshift, recovery from stress, or transfer into fresh medium may raise growth for minutes or generations and then end. When normal growth returns, the original ON state and its unstable switching threshold also return. The cell's fate is then determined not only by whether the pulse crossed a fold, but by how far the protein concentration moved while the fold was crossed. This essay calculates that strength–duration condition.

2 · Make growth a pulse

Let N be protein molecule number and V cell volume, so the protein concentration x = N/V. If dV/dt = μV, differentiating x contributes the dilution term −μx. Molecular degradation adds −γx, giving the effective first-order removal coefficient λ = γ + μ. A transient increase in growth therefore appears as a transient increase in λ.

Now take the positive-autoregulation example used in the course and expose this parameter:

dx/dt = p(x) - λx,    p(x) = 0.15 + 3x4/(1 + x4).   (1)

The basal term keeps OFF above zero; the saturating Hill term supplies positive feedback. The model is dimensionless and is not fitted to one bacterium. By imposed a square growth pulse λ = 1 before and after the pulse, and λ = λp for a duration T. Thus pulse strength is λp, pulse duration is T, and the regulatory production law is unchanged.

λ(t) = 1 outside the pulse;   λ(t) = λp during a pulse of duration T.   (2)

3 · The static folds are calibration, not the new result

To know how strong a pulse must be, the tangency criterion from Lecture 4 was applied to the positive-autoregulation model. A steady state satisfies p(x) = λx. At a fold the production and removal curves also have equal slopes:

p(x) = λx,    p′(x) = λ.   (3)

Here p′(x) = 12x3/(1 + x4)2. Eliminating λ gives p(x) = xp′(x); with y = x4, this becomes 3.15y2 − 8.70y + 0.15 = 0. The resulting folds simply calibrate the pulse experiment:

λ < 0.554one stable ON state
0.554 < λ < 1.825OFF and ON memory
λ > 1.825one stable OFF state

At normal removal, λ = 1, the fixed points are 0.152 (stable OFF), 0.681 (unstable threshold) and 3.119 (stable ON). A pulse weaker than the upper fold, λp ≤ 1.825, leaves a high stable state above the restored threshold, so simply waiting cannot drive an initially ON cell across 0.681 in this deterministic model. A pulse stronger than 1.825 temporarily deletes ON, but duration still decides whether the deletion becomes permanent.

4 · Crossing a fold is necessary but not sufficient

Start at the normal ON state, xON = 3.119. During a supercritical pulse, λp > 1.825, no high fixed point remains and x decreases. When the pulse ends, λ returns to 1 and all three fixed points return. If the state is still above 0.681 it lies in the restored ON basin and climbs back; if it has fallen below 0.681 it lies in the OFF basin and continues downward. Temporary deletion of an attractor is therefore not yet permanent erasure of its memory.

For a given pulse strength, separation of variables gives the minimum time needed to move from the original ON state to the restored basin boundary:

Tc(λp) = ∫0.6813.119 dx / [λpx − p(x)].   (4)

For λp = 2, Equation (4) gives Tc = 4.003 time units. A three-unit pulse ends at x = 1.112 and returns ON. A seven-unit pulse ends at x = 0.077 and remains OFF. The same pulse amplitude therefore produces opposite long-term memories solely because its duration differs.

5 · The new result: strength trades against duration

Equation (4) converts a static fold into a finite-pulse prediction. At λp = 1.85, only slightly above the fold, erasure requires 11.87 time units. Raising the pulse to 2.0 lowers the boundary to 4.00; at 2.5 it is 1.60, and at 4.0 it is 0.61. Close to 1.825, the denominator in Equation (4) becomes very small near the former ON–threshold collision, so the trajectory passes through a dynamical bottleneck and Tc rises sharply. This is the finite-pulse consequence of the fold, not another calculation of where the fold lies.

The boundary also separates two meanings of “strong enough.” A pulse must first be supercritical, λp > 1.825, to remove the high attractor. It must then last longer than Tc(λp) to carry the state below the threshold that returns when normal growth resumes. Thus neither amplitude nor duration alone predicts erasure; they form a joint control variable.

6 · Experimental prediction and limits

This provides an experimental prediction. Prepare genetically identical cells in the ON state, expose them to conditions that transiently increase effective dilution, and restore the original condition. Short pulses should recover ON, whereas sufficiently long pulses should yield OFF descendants even after the environment is restored. Varying both pulse strength and duration would estimate the boundary Tc(λp). Measuring growth and fluorescence simultaneously would help distinguish genuine state switching from simple concentration dilution.

The numerical values 1.825 and 4.003 are properties of this dimensionless teaching model, not universal bacterial constants. Changing basal expression, feedback strength, cooperativity or the Hill scale moves both the fold and the strength–duration curve. Noise could also switch some cells before the deterministic boundary, turning the sharp line in Figure 2B into a probability band.

The second limit is biological. Growth does not turn only the removal line. Changes in bacterial growth alter cell size, gene dosage, RNA-polymerase allocation, ribosome abundance and translation capacity. Klumpp, Zhang and Hwa showed that even constitutive gene expression changes systematically with growth rate. In a real cell, both p(x) and λx may move, so the direction of the net shift must be measured rather than assumed.

Circuit expression can itself slow growth by consuming resources or producing a toxic protein. Tan, Marguet and You constructed a T7 RNA-polymerase positive-feedback circuit in which circuit activation reduced growth and circuit–host feedback generated bistability. Growth can therefore be a state-dependent variable rather than an externally imposed square pulse. A fuller model would add an equation for μ(x) and ask how feedback reshapes the pulse boundary.

7 · So what?

The lectures establish how positive feedback makes memory and how a saddle-node destroys a stable state. The finite-pulse calculation adds a different statement: temporary destruction of the ON attractor does not guarantee permanent forgetting. The state must spend enough time in the altered landscape to cross the threshold of the landscape that will return. For synthetic circuits, that means a growth perturbation should be specified by amplitude and duration, not by growth rate alone. A bacterium forgets only when faster growth is both strong enough and long enough.
