Week 2 — Extension: A Fixed Point for Soluble Protein, Built from Crowding on Both Sides

Author: Yan Runzi · Week 2 (2026-09-13) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: Soluble protein concentration settles to a stable level for the same structural reason a repressed gene does — because both the addition curve and the removal curve in Lecture 4's f+(x) − f−(x) picture bend against growth, one by diffusion-limited translation in a crowding cytoplasm and the other by concentration-driven misfolding and aggregation — except here the "repressor" of production is the protein's own bulk, not a dedicated transcription factor.

Same Crossing, New Repressor

Lecture 4 built a one-variable fixed point as an addition-removal crossing, ẋ = f+(x) − f−(x), and later built a two-gene toggle where each protein represses the other's production through a Hill-shaped occupancy function. Protein homeostasis inside a single cell can be read the same way, but with a twist: the thing doing the repressing is not a separate molecule, it is the protein pool itself, acting through physical crowding.

At low soluble protein concentration x, the cytoplasm is comparatively dilute: ribosomes translate close to their maximum elongation rate, aminoacyl-tRNA ternary complexes find ribosomes quickly, and newly made chains fold correctly often enough that net production of soluble, correctly folded protein is high. As x rises, two things work against it at once. First, translation itself slows down: ternary complexes are bulky, and their delivery to the ribosome becomes diffusion-limited as the cytoplasm fills up, which caps how fast new protein can be made in the first place [1]. Second, of the protein that is made, a growing fraction fails to fold correctly or aggregates instead of staying soluble — aggregation kinetics are famously concentration-driven, so removal from the soluble pool accelerates faster than production ever does [2]. The two curves therefore do not merely add and subtract; they bend toward each other, and where they cross is a genuine fixed point in Lecture 4's sense — not just a ceiling on synthesis.

The Calculation

Write net production as a Hill-repressed addition curve — the same functional form as the lecture's toggle-switch arm, but self-repressed by the protein's own concentration instead of by a partner gene — and net removal as dilution plus a concentration-accelerated aggregation/clearance term:

f+(x) = μ / (1 + (x/K)2)      f−(x) = δx + κx2

Here μ is the dilute-cytoplasm production rate, K sets the crowding scale at which translation output has already halved (motivated by the diffusion-limited translation regime reported for a crowded cytoplasm [1]), δ is ordinary dilution, and the quadratic term κx² stands in for concentration-driven misfolding and aggregation, whose rate is empirically reported to rise faster than linearly with protein concentration [2,3]. Taking illustrative values μ = 5, K = 2, δ = 0.5, κ = 0.05 — in the same spirit as Lecture 4's own teaching parameters, chosen to place a crossing rather than fit one protein — solving f+(x) = f−(x) numerically gives a single crossing:

x* ≈ 2.735,   f′(x*) ≈ −1.60 < 0  ⟹  stable

The negative slope is exactly Lecture 4's stability condition: just left of x*, production still outruns removal and x is pushed up; just right of it, crowding-driven removal wins and x is pushed back down. Soluble protein has a homeostatic set point for the same structural reason the lecture's autorepressed gene does.

Where This Breaks: Quality Control Is Not Infinite

The single stable crossing above assumes the cell's clearance machinery — chaperones, the proteasome, autophagy — can always keep pace, so that removal keeps accelerating as x grows. Real quality-control capacity is not unbounded: aging and chronically stressed cells lose the ability to refold or degrade aggregates as their clearance systems become saturated or exhausted [3]. In the model above, that corresponds to f−(x) flattening out at high x instead of continuing to rise as κx² — clearance running at a fixed maximal rate rather than one that keeps growing with the aggregate load. If the removal curve saturates while the crowding-limited production curve f+(x) still has some tail left at high x, f+ can cross back above f− a second time. That would turn the single stable fixed point in Figure 1 into a Lecture-4-style three-crossing picture: a low, healthy stable state, an unstable threshold, and a second stable state at pathologically high aggregate load — a structural echo of the "proteostasis collapse" seen in aging and in aggregation diseases, where the cell does not drift smoothly upward but tips past a threshold into a qualitatively different regime. The calculation above only establishes the single-crossing case; showing that a saturating clearance term actually creates the second pair of crossings is the natural next step this essay does not do.
