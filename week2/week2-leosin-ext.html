Signs decide stability exactly when there is no loop to tune

Leo Sin (施延庆) · Week 2, lectures 3–4 · CCBS 2026 · ~1600 words

One sentence: The sign pattern of a Jacobian settles local stability for every choice of magnitudes exactly when the wiring has no loop that magnitudes could tune — no mutually activating or mutually repressing pair, and no loop through three or more species — because the only numbers that survive giving each species its own unit are the products around loops; so signs alone decide precisely for the wirings that can neither remember nor keep time, and the share of wirings they decide falls from 7 of 9 at two species to 5% at four.

1The statement lecture 4 asked for

Lecture 4 §1 sets the target: "We need statements such as 'every field with this sign pattern is stable'." §8 then supplies the tool that reads numbers, the Hurwitz test, and the observation that "the sign pattern of the Jacobian is the local wiring diagram."1 What it does not say is when the wiring alone already settles the test. That question has an exact answer, found in economics by Quirk and Ruppert, carried into ecology by May, and completed by Jeffries, Klee and van den Driessche: a sign pattern is sign stable when every matrix carrying those signs, whatever the magnitudes, is Hurwitz.3–6

In the lecture's terms: at a fixed point, A = J(x*) with aij = ∂Fi/∂xj, so species j points at species i with the sign of aij. Every diagonal entry starts at −λ from dilution (lecture 3 §4) and degradation only lowers it, so take aii < 0 throughout.1,2 Then the theorem reduces to two tests on the off-diagonal signs.

Sign stability with self-removal on every species. The wiring is Hurwitz for every choice of magnitudes if and only if
  1. no pair is mutually activating or mutually repressing: aijaji ≤ 0 for all i ≠ j; and
  2. no directed loop runs through three or more species.
The other conditions of the full theorem, a nonpositive diagonal with at least one negative entry, a nonzero determinant and Jeffries' color test, are automatic once every aii < 0.4–6

Figure 1 puts the nine two-species wirings through the tests. Seven pass. The two that fail are the two lecture 4 could build memory from: mutual activation, and the mutual repression of its toggle (12). For those two, and only those two, the Hurwitz rule (17) needs numbers. The trace is negative for free, and det A > 0 becomes a12a21 < a11a22: the product of the two interaction slopes must stay below the product of the two removal rates. That is lecture 4 §3's slope test, addition must not outrun removal at the crossing, promoted to a loop.

2Why: the numbers that no unit can absorb

The proof I could reconstruct is a change of units, and it names the magnitudes the signs cannot absorb. Give each displacement its own unit: zi = diwi with di > 0. Then ẇ = Bw with B = D−1AD,

bij = aij dj/di.  (1)

B is similar to A, so it has the same eigenvalues and the same diagonal. The off-diagonal magnitudes change, but in a constrained way: bijbji = aijaji, and around any directed loop the d's cancel, so every loop product is untouched (Figure 2B). Loop products are the unit-free magnitudes of a wiring; everything else about the off-diagonal entries is a choice of units.

Now suppose tests 1 and 2 hold. Group the species into strongly connected blocks. The only loops allowed are negative pairs, so each block is a tree of negative pairs and the blocks feed forward into one another: A is block-triangular and its eigenvalues are those of the blocks. Inside a block, walk the tree from a root and on each edge set (dj/di)2 = −aji/aij, a positive number precisely because the pair is negative. Then bij = −bji on every edge: B = Λ + S with Λ = diag(aii) and S skew-symmetric. Take V = Σwi2. Along ẇ = Bw,

V̇ = 2wTΛw + 2wTSw = 2 Σ aiiwi2 < 0,  (2)

because the skew term vanishes identically. V only decays, so the block is Hurwitz, so A is. This is lecture 4 §9's Lyapunov move with a quadratic V whose weights are the units, and it works for every magnitude because a tree leaves every ratio dj/di free (Figure 2A). Where it stops is equally explicit: a positive pair has bijbji > 0 in every unit system, and a loop through three or more species leaves one product no D touches. The leftover number is a loop gain, and stability depends on it. That any such loop can actually be tuned into instability is the converse half, proved by Jeffries, Klee and van den Driessche;6 I check it numerically instead.

3Work it

Counting. Enumerate every off-diagonal sign pattern with a negative diagonal and apply the two tests: 7 of 9 wirings at n = 2, 223 of 729 at n = 3, 28,321 of 531,441 at n = 4. The share of wirings whose stability is fixed by signs alone falls from 78% to 31% to 5.3% (Figure 3B, Table 1). The three-species count can be done by hand: 73 = 343 patterns have no positive pair, 120 of those carry a directed three-loop, and 223 are left.

Checking. For each of the 729 three-species wirings I drew 400 magnitude sets, every nonzero entry log-uniform over two decades (0.1 to 10, diagonals included), and counted how many were Hurwitz (Figure 3A, Table 2). The 223 sign-stable wirings gave 89,200 matrices and no failure. Each of the other 506 wirings failed at least once, so every wiring the tests reject is one where the numbers really do go either way; the median rejected wiring is Hurwitz about half the time.

The sign of the loop says how stability is lost. Table 2 also records what crossed the axis. In the 450 wirings with a positive loop, an unstable draw usually carries a real eigenvalue that has crossed zero: 80,545 draws, against 8,266 with only a complex pair. In the 56 wirings whose loops are all negative, 2,375 draws were unstable and not one had a real eigenvalue ≥ 0. That is a theorem, not a sampling accident (three lines, below): a negative-loop wiring can only lose stability through a complex pair, a clock, while a real crossing, the fold of lecture 4 §5 that creates or destroys a memory state, needs a positive loop. This is Thomas's rule read at one fixed point: a positive loop is necessary for multiple steady states, a negative one for sustained oscillation.7–9

why only negative loops cannot produce a real crossing, in three lines

Expand det(−A) over permutations, each a product of directed cycles. A fixed point contributes −aii > 0; a cycle of length k contributes (−1)k−1 from the permutation sign times (−1)k times a negative loop product, which is positive. So every term is positive, and the same holds for every principal minor: −A is a P-matrix. A P-matrix has no real eigenvalue ≤ 0, since adding tI with t ≥ 0 keeps every principal minor positive and so never makes it singular. Hence A has no real eigenvalue ≥ 0.

the code, in full (Python 3, numpy; ~20 s) 
import itertools, math, numpy as np

# --- the two sign tests, every diagonal entry negative (self-removal) ---
def long_loop(S):                       # a directed loop through >= 3 species; j -> i iff S[i][j] != 0
    n = len(S)
    return any(all(S[v[(m+1) % k]][v[m]] != 0 for m in range(k))
               for k in range(3, n+1) for v in itertools.permutations(range(n), k) if v[0] == min(v))

def sign_stable(S):
    n = len(S)
    if any(S[i][j] * S[j][i] > 0 for i in range(n) for j in range(i+1, n)):
        return False                    # a positive pair: mutual activation or mutual repression
    return not long_loop(S)

def wirings(n):                         # every sign pattern on the off-diagonal, diagonal fixed at -1
    off = [(i, j) for i in range(n) for j in range(n) if i != j]
    for signs in itertools.product((-1, 0, 1), repeat=len(off)):
        S = [[-1 if i == j else 0 for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
        for (i, j), s in zip(off, signs): S[i][j] = s
        yield S

for n in (2, 3, 4):
    tot = sum(1 for _ in wirings(n)); ok = sum(sign_stable(S) for S in wirings(n))
    print(f"n={n}: {ok} of {tot} wirings are sign-stable ({100*ok/tot:.2f}%)")

# --- Monte Carlo: give every nonzero entry a random magnitude, two decades log-uniform ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def draw(S):
    return np.array(S, float) * 10 ** rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=np.shape(S))

def loop_signs(S):                      # sign of every directed loop, length 2..n
    n = len(S); out = []
    for k in range(2, n+1):
        for v in itertools.permutations(range(n), k):
            if v[0] != min(v): continue
            p = math.prod(S[v[(m+1) % k]][v[m]] for m in range(k))
            if p: out.append(p)
    return out

N, rows = 400, []
for S in wirings(3):
    cls = ("sign-stable" if sign_stable(S) else
           "positive loop" if any(p > 0 for p in loop_signs(S)) else "only negative loops")
    hur = re_bad = im_bad = 0
    for _ in range(N):
        ev = np.linalg.eigvals(draw(S)); bad = ev[ev.real >= 0]
        if len(bad) == 0: hur += 1
        elif np.any(abs(bad.imag) < 1e-9): re_bad += 1      # a real eigenvalue crossed zero
        else: im_bad += 1                                    # only a complex pair crossed
    rows.append((cls, hur / N, re_bad, im_bad))

for cls in ("sign-stable", "positive loop", "only negative loops"):
    fr = sorted(r[1] for r in rows if r[0] == cls)
    print(f"{cls:20s} {len(fr):3d} wirings  Hurwitz fraction min/median/max = "
          f"{fr[0]:.3f}/{fr[len(fr)//2]:.3f}/{fr[-1]:.3f}  unstable draws: real {sum(r[2] for r in rows if r[0]==cls)}, "
          f"complex {sum(r[3] for r in rows if r[0]==cls)}")

# --- the toggle of lecture 4, eq (12): loop gain a12*a21 against a11*a22 = 1 ---
g = lambda x: 4 / (1 + (4 / (1 + x*x))**2) - x            # x-nullcline evaluated on the y-nullcline
xs = np.linspace(0, 5, 50001); v = g(xs)
for k in np.flatnonzero(v[:-1] * v[1:] < 0):
    a, b = xs[k], xs[k+1]
    for _ in range(60): m = (a+b)/2; a, b = (a, m) if g(a)*g(m) <= 0 else (m, b)
    x, y = (a+b)/2, 4/(1+((a+b)/2)**2)
    a12, a21 = -8*y/(1+y*y)**2, -8*x/(1+x*x)**2
    print(f"toggle ({x:.4f},{y:.4f}): loop gain {a12*a21:.4f}, eigenvalues {-1-math.sqrt(a12*a21):+.4f} {-1+math.sqrt(a12*a21):+.4f}")

# --- a ring of three, a_ii = -1, loop product P: eigenvalues -1 + P^(1/3) * cube roots of unity ---
for P in (-7.9, -8.0, -8.1, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1):
    A = -np.eye(3) + np.sign(P) * abs(P)**(1/3) * np.roll(np.eye(3), 1, axis=0)
    ev = np.linalg.eigvals(A); lead = ev[np.argmax(ev.real)]
    print(f"ring P={P:+.1f}: max Re = {lead.real:+.4f}, Im = {abs(lead.imag):.3f}")

4Where this breaks

Failing the tests is not instability. Sign stability is a statement about a family; for one fixed point with one set of numbers it says nothing. About half of the draws for the rejected wirings were Hurwitz, and the toggle's two memory states are Hurwitz at loop gain 0.25 with the same wiring that is a saddle at 1.72. The tests say when you may stop computing, never that a fixed point is unstable.

The signs belong to the fixed point, not to the network. A Hill law has one sign everywhere, but a substrate-inhibited enzyme, or lecture 3 §9's PFK, flips the sign of a slope with the state, and positive autoregulation makes aii > 0 at the middle crossing of lecture 4 §4, a positive loop of length one. Species with no removal at all, a closed phosphorylation cycle or a conserved pool (lecture 3 §10), have aii = 0; then the color test and the determinant condition matter, and a zero eigenvalue may be a conserved total rather than a failure of recovery, the slice Kehan's essay takes this week. Read the Jacobian at each fixed point before reading its signs.

"For every magnitude" is not "robust", and my range is a choice. A sign-stable wiring can recover arbitrarily slowly, and it inherits the Jacobian's limits from lecture 4 §8: local, one fixed point, silent on basins. The fractions in Figure 3A and Table 2 depend on sampling two decades; widen it and every rejected wiring fails more often. Only the 1.000 of the sign-stable class, the zero real crossings of the negative-loop class, and the counts are range-free.

What I take from it: lecture 4's structure-to-function map has a first row that can be filled exactly. No loop, or only negative pairs: stable, numbers never needed. Only negative loops: stable or a clock, never a switch. A positive loop anywhere: the loop gain decides, and the one-dimensional slope test is its simplest case. The map is complete for the wirings that do nothing and empties out for the ones that compute, which is what "structural" has to mean.
