Cooperativity turns positive feedback from amplification into memory

Author: zhuxiaomeng · Week 2 Extension · CCBS 2026

One-sentence claim: Positive feedback creates biological memory only when sufficient nonlinearity produces two stable states separated by an unstable threshold.

The point I am extending

Lecture 3 introduces Hill functions as reduced descriptions of cooperative regulation, while Lecture 4 explains fixed points and stability. Putting these ideas together gives a sharper statement: positive feedback alone is not a memory switch. A system remembers only if its production and removal curves intersect at three fixed points, with the middle point acting as a threshold between two stable states.

A minimal model

I used the one-variable model dx/dt = a + b*x^n/(K^n + x^n) - x. The first term is basal production, the Hill term is self-activation, and the last term is removal. I fixed a=0.08, b=2.5, and K=1, then changed only the Hill coefficient n.

What the calculation shows

Hill coefficientFixed points (approximately)Interpretation
n=1x=1.629One stable state: amplification, but no memory.
n=4x=0.080, 0.796, 2.519Low and high states are stable; the middle state is an unstable threshold.

For n=1, the production curve is too gently curved to create two alternative stable states. For n=4, cooperativity makes the curve sigmoidal, allowing three crossings. A perturbation that does not cross the middle point returns to its original state; a sufficiently large perturbation crosses the threshold and settles in the other state. This persistence after the input disappears is the system's memory.

Why this matters

The structural lesson is that feedback describes a circuit connection, whereas memory is a dynamical property. Cooperativity changes the geometry of the rate curves and can create bistability. This also explains why one simulated trajectory is not enough: different initial conditions must be tested to reveal the two basins of attraction.
