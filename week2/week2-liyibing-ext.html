Why does an enzyme-catalysed reaction saturate?

More substrate initially makes a reaction faster, but a finite enzyme pool imposes a ceiling.

Extension mini-essay · liyibing · Lecture 3 · CCBS 2026

1The claim

Lecture 3 reduces a multi-step enzyme mechanism to a single effective rate law.1 I want to use that reduction to explain one familiar observation: increasing substrate concentration eventually stops producing a proportional increase in reaction rate.

An enzyme reaction saturates because substrate fills a finite pool of enzyme molecules. At high substrate concentration almost every enzyme is already occupied, so the rate approaches the catalytic capacity Vmax.

2The mechanism

For a one-substrate reaction, free enzyme E binds free substrate S to form the complex ES, which can either dissociate or make product:

E + S ⇌ ES → E + P
binding k+ · dissociation k− · catalysis kcat

The total enzyme concentration is fixed on the catalytic timescale:

ET = E + ES.

Product appears at rate v = kcatES. The question is therefore how the occupied enzyme concentration ES depends on S.

According to the Briggs–Haldane reduction, ES rapidly relaxes to a quasi-steady state, so dES/dt ≈ 0.2

  1. Write dES/dt ≈ k+(ET−ES)S − (k−+kcat)ES = 0.
  2. Define KM = (k−+kcat)/k+.
  3. Solve for occupancy: ES = ETS/(KM+S).
  4. Multiply by kcat. At saturating substrate concentration, ES approaches ET, meaning that almost all enzyme is present as the enzyme–substrate complex. The maximum rate is therefore Vmax = kcatET:
v = Vmax S/(KM + S),    Vmax = kcatET.

After dividing by Vmax and defining x = S/KM, every one-substrate Michaelis–Menten curve collapses onto

v/Vmax = x/(1+x).

3The saturation curve

The code behind Figure 1. The axes are normalized, so no fitted parameter or experimental dataset is used.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = np.linspace(0, 8, 400)      # x = S / K_M
y = x / (1 + x)                 # y = v / V_max

plt.plot(x, y)
plt.scatter([1], [0.5])
plt.axhline(1, linestyle="--")  # v = V_max asymptote
plt.xlabel("S / K_M")
plt.ylabel("v / V_max")
plt.ylim(0, 1.05)
plt.show()
RegimeApproximationInterpretation
S ≪ KMv ≈ (Vmax/KM)SMost enzyme is free; adding substrate gives an almost proportional gain.
S = KMv = Vmax/2Half of the catalytic capacity is used.
S ≫ KMv ≈ VmaxAlmost all enzyme is occupied; enzyme turnover, not substrate supply, limits the rate.

The curve bends because each added substrate molecule has less unoccupied enzyme left to find. Saturation is therefore a finite-capacity effect: S controls the fraction of enzyme occupied, whereas kcatET sets the ceiling.

4Where it is wrong

The curve assumes a one-substrate mechanism, a quasi-steady ES complex and free substrate S. The reduction is safest when ET/(KM+ST) ≪ 1; otherwise binding can deplete substrate and free S differs from the experimentally controlled total ST.3 In addition, KM = (k−+kcat)/k+ is not generally the binding dissociation constant Kd = k−/k+. Cooperativity, inhibition, multiple substrates and conformational transitions can produce different curves.
