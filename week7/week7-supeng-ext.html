Affinity has a proteome cost floor

Author: Peng Su · Lectures 14 15 · CCBS 2026

One sentence: If a designed intracellular binder must maintain at least Creq target-bound complexes, its minimum expression is ET=Creq(1+Kd/S), so improving affinity saves growth capacity mainly while Kd is comparable to the ligand concentration; once Kd≪S, stoichiometry imposes an irreducible proteome-cost floor.

Turning a binding specification into a growth cost

Lecture 14 treated the proteome as a finite budget shared by metabolic and ribosomal machinery. Lecture 15 then emphasized that a designed protein must work at an appropriate concentration and that expression burden can change its cellular performance. Took these statements as background, a quantitative question can be asked that if a binder must produce a specified amount of bound complex, how much proteome does its affinity actually save?

Let S be a free ligand concentration maintained by a reservoir, Kd the dissociation constant, and ET the total concentration of the designed binder. For independent 1:1 binding, Lecture 15 gives the equilibrium occupied fraction

θ = S/(Kd+S).

The concentration of bound complex is therefore C=θET. Suppose the cellular task requires an absolute complex concentration of at least Creq, rather than merely a large occupied fraction. Solving C≥Creq for the least binder that can meet the task gives

ET,min = Creq (Kd+S)/S = Creq(1+Kd/S).

Let α convert binder concentration into its fraction of total cellular protein, so φD=αET. This factor contains the binder’s molecular mass and the cell’s total protein concentration. The minimum proteome cost is then

φD,min = αCreq(1+Kd/S).

The two terms have different meanings. The term αCreq is unavoidable: every required complex needs one binder molecule. The additional term αCreqKd/S pays for unbound binder. Better affinity can remove the second term but never the first.

How the expression cost enters the growth law

To connect this result to Lecture 14, add the designed protein as a nonproductive sector in the minimal two-sector growth model. Let φR and φE be the ribosomal and metabolic fractions, with translation capacity σ and precursor-supply capacity k. The constraints become λ≤σφR, λ≤kφE, and φR+φE+φD=1. At the growth-maximizing allocation, both productive capacities are equal, giving

λ*(φD) = (1−φD) kσ/(k+σ) = (1−φD)λ0*.

Within this deliberately small model, the binder’s proteome fraction becomes the same fractional loss of the maximum growth rate. Combining the equations converts a molecular design parameter into a cellular cost:

λ*/λ0* = 1−αCreq(1+Kd/S).

Where the affinity growth connection breaks

The conclusion, which protein design should maximize affinity, applies when function requires an absolute number of complexes. If a reporter depends only on fractional occupancy, ET cancels from θ and the same expression requirement does not follow. Strong binding can also perturb the supposedly fixed free ligand pool; finite ligand totals require the full binding quadratic rather than the reservoir formula.

The growth calculation is likewise a steady, optimal, two-sector model. Real burden depends on transcription, translation efficiency, folding, degradation, localization and toxicity, so α need not be constant across designs. A tighter binder obtained mainly by reducing its dissociation rate may also reset more slowly, as Lecture 15 already warns. The useful extension is therefore narrower: once the desired complex abundance is specified, affinity has a calculable expression benefit, but that benefit saturates at a stoichiometric proteome floor.
