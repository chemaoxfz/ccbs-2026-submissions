CCBS / 2026 Fall / Week 07 · Lectures 13 & 14

More reactions,
slower cells?

Why an expanded metabolic network can coexist with reduced cellular growth.

Author: Li Changkai  ·  Extension mini-essay  ·  2 November 2026

One-sentence claimAdding an optional reaction cannot reduce the optimal objective in ordinary flux balance analysis (FBA), but expressing the enzyme for that reaction can reduce real growth by taking proteome away from ribosomes and essential metabolism.

The point I am adding

Lecture 13 uses FBA to optimize a biomass flux under stoichiometric and uptake constraints; Lecture 14 explains why making catalytic machinery itself costs resources. Combining them reveals a useful distinction: adding a possible reaction and expressing its catalyst are different operations. [1][2]

The first operation has a simple mathematical guarantee. If a new reaction can carry zero flux, and all old constraints and the biomass objective stay unchanged, every old feasible solution remains feasible by setting its new flux to zero. Therefore

max vbiomass, new  ≥  max vbiomass, old Feasible-set inclusion; an optimization theorem, not a claim about the phenotype of an enzyme-expressing cell.

Work it: charge the cell for the protein

Take a deliberately small balanced-growth model. Let the fixed housekeeping fraction be φQ, leaving Φ = 1 − φQ of total protein for three parts: ribosomes φR, useful metabolic enzymes φM, and an expressed but unused enzyme φU. Translation and precursor supply both have to support the same specific growth rate λ: [2]

φR + φM + φU = Φ;   λ ≤ min(σφR, κφM) σ and κ are capacities per unit protein fraction (h−1); all fractions are nonnegative.

For fixed φU, the maximum occurs when neither productive capacity is wasted: σφR = κφM. Solving the two equalities gives

λ*(φU) = σκσ + κ(Φ − φU) Thus λ*(φU) / λ*(0) = 1 − φU/Φ. The burden is relative to the allocatable proteome, not all protein.

As a hypothetical worked example (not a fit to a bacterium), set Φ = 0.60, σ = 3 h−1, and κ = 1.5 h−1. Allocating 12% of total protein to an unused enzyme leaves only 48% for the two growth-supporting sectors. The optimum falls from 0.60 to 0.48 h−1, a 20% decrease; the model doubling time rises from 69.3 to 86.6 minutes.

Where this claim breaks

Making a new reaction available in the genome is not the same as making its enzyme: a silent gene need not impose this protein-allocation burden. Conversely, an expressed enzyme can earn back its cost if the new pathway becomes useful under another condition. The calculation assumes fixed housekeeping share, constant per-protein capacities, balanced growth and no direct enzyme toxicity; it cannot predict transient regulation or a real organism's cost without data. [3]

Empirical check, kept separate from the toy numbers. Scott et al. (2010) measured growth defects from gratuitous protein expression in E. coli and derived a growth-law explanation using proteome allocation. That supports the mechanism; the values 0.60, 3 and 1.5 above are my illustrative choices, not their measured parameters. [3]
