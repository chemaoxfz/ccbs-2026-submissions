Week 7 — Extension: the precursor pool taxes the growth rate, not the growth law

Author: fanglipei (20261203497) · Week 7 (2026-11-02) · CCBS 2026 · build-on: Lecture 14, §7 and §10

One sentence: the precursor pool of Lecture 14 does not compete for the protein budget like a third sector — it discounts the metabolic sector's capacity by a factor that depends on the growth rate it is supporting, k → k/(1+λ/kA); read that way the whole correction is one dimensionless number, it provably cannot move the λ–φR growth law, and it turns the constant protein price of growth used in the lecture's own budget into a price that rises with λ.

The point I am adding

§10 closes the growth model by keeping the precursor pool MA instead of assuming it negligible, and reports the price: with k = 2, σ = 3, kA = 8 h-1 the optimal growth rate falls from 1.20 to 1.11 h-1, so "the small-pool approximation systematically over-estimates". That is true, but stated as a numerical caveat it hides what the pool is doing. The same algebra, read one step further, says something sharper.

At the optimum two facts hold at once, and both are already in the lecture: translation runs at capacity, so φR = λ/σ; and the pool sits at its steady ratio a = MA/M = λ/kA. Put both into the precursor balance of §7, k(1−φR) = λ(1+a):

k(1 − λ/σ) = λ(1 + λ/kA)  ⟺  λ/σ + λ/k + λ2/(k kA) = 1.

Nothing new — except the reading. The right-hand equation is exactly the small-pool condition λ/σ + λ/k = 1 with k replaced by

keff(λ) = k / (1 + λ/kA).

So the pool is not a third claimant on the budget. It is a discount on one of the two capacities, and the discount is set by the growth rate that capacity is supporting: the supply line of the growth envelope in §7 does not disappear, it rotates down, more steeply the faster the cell tries to grow. Three consequences follow immediately, and none of them is visible in the bare number 1.11.

  1. The correction collapses into a single dimensionless group γ, the product of the pool ratio and the metabolic share.
  2. Because the optimum always keeps φR = λ/σ, the tax moves the operating point along the growth law and cannot change its slope or create an intercept.
  3. The protein cost of growth stops being constant: it becomes 1/σ + 1/k + 2λ/(k kA), which is the one place where the linear budget of §11–§12 stops being exact.

Work it

1 · One number does the whole correction

Write λ0 = kσ/(k+σ) for the pool-free optimum and x = λ/λ0. Dividing the pooled condition by (1/σ + 1/k) gives x + γx2 = 1 with

γ ≡ λ02/(k kA) = a0 · φE,0
tax = 1 − [ √(1+4γ) − 1 ] / (2γ)  ≈  γ − 2γ2

where a0 = λ0/kA is the pool-to-protein mass ratio and φE,0 = λ0/k the metabolic sector share, both evaluated at the uncorrected optimum. Two ratios, multiplied. The pool costs growth in proportion to how much stock is carried per unit protein and to how much of the proteome is spent supplying it — and that is the whole correction, for any capacities.

Two things in that table are worth more than the headline 7.7 %. First, the tax is not a constant of the organism: it tracks the pool ratio a = λ/kA, so it is paid mostly where growth is fast. Take the two E. coli rates the lecture quotes — 73 min per doubling on glucose minimal (λ = 0.57 h-1) and 26 min on a rich defined medium (λ = 1.58 h-1) — and fix σ at the value the measured growth law implies: a slope of 4.5 units of RNA per protein per h-1 in r = 0.087 + λ/4.5, converted with §4's ρ = 0.76 g of sector protein per g of RNA, gives σ ≈ 5.9 h-1. Reading k off λ0 = kσ/(k+σ) at each rate and keeping kA = 8 h-1, the model charges 5.7 % on the minimal medium and 11.4 % on the rich one. The pool is cheap for a slow cell and dear for a fast one, so "more ribosomes" buys less than a linear return: the faster the cell runs, the larger the stock it drags, and the last term of the budget is the bill for it. Second, the correction is a footnote only if γ < 0.01, which needs kA ≳ 60 λ0 (for φE,0 ≈ 0.6) — a precursor stock turning over in about a minute or less. The lecture's kA = 8 h-1 is a 7.5-minute turnover: not an odd parameter, but precisely the regime in which the pool is worth modelling.

2 · The growth law itself does not move

Panel A is the reason the tax is invisible in the composition data. Both optima lie on the line λ = σφR, so λ* and φR* shrink by the same factor 1−ε: the model predicts a growth law with the same slope and the same intercept (zero in this bare two-sector version), and the pool changes only which point on the line the cell occupies.

That is a claim with a test attached, using the data the lecture already shows. Scott et al.'s relation for E. coli is r = 0.087 + λ/4.5 (RNA per protein), i.e. a positive intercept. No value of kA can produce a positive intercept here: the tax is paid out of λ, and φR follows λ down the same ray. So the intercept cannot be an artefact of the pool, and whatever produces it must modify a capacity — reserve or inactive ribosomes, or the accounting of the fixed sectors, exactly the candidates §8 lists. The extension makes that a necessity rather than a choice, and it also separates hypotheses that are otherwise easy to confuse: adding a pool to the two-branch diagram of §8 shortens both branches but flips neither sign, so "the pool is finite" and "the nutrient or the drug changed a capacity" are orthogonal explanations, not rivals.

3 · What it does touch: the price of growth

The whole protein cost of supporting λ is λ/σ + λ/k + λ2/(k kA). Panel B plots it per unit growth. The budget of §11–§12 is the γ → 0 limit of this expression: it charges 1/σ + 1/k per unit λ, a constant, and §12 then folds that constant into wRλ. With a finite pool the marginal price is

∂cost/∂λ = 1/σ + 1/k + 2λ/(k kA)

which is 0.833 at λ = 0 and 0.972 at λ* — 17 % higher. An average-cost coefficient fitted at λ = 0.6 (0.871) and applied at λ* undercharges by 3.7 %. Neither number is large, but they say the right thing about the objects: a protein cost per unit growth is a local quantity, valid at the growth rate where it was fitted, and any budget written with a constant wR silently assumes the pool is negligible at that operating point. This is the same diagnostic the lecture applies to λmax and Kc in §7: a fitted constant absorbs a mechanism and then hides it.

# pool_tax.py (core; the file beside this page also draws Figure 2)
import math
K, SIGMA, KA = 2.0, 3.0, 8.0            # h^-1, Lecture 14 s.10

def lam_small(k, s):                    # both capacities used, no pool
    return k*s/(k+s)

def lam_pool(k, s, kA):                 # root of l/s + l/k + l^2/(k*kA) = 1
    b = 1/s + 1/k                       # stable form: 2/(b+sqrt(b^2+4/(k*kA)))
    return 2.0/(b + math.sqrt(b*b + 4.0/(k*kA)))

def tax_from_gamma(lam0, k, kA):        # 1 - lam*/lam0, gamma = lam0^2/(k*kA)
    g = lam0*lam0/(k*kA)
    return 0.0 if g == 0 else 1.0 - (math.sqrt(1+4*g) - 1)/(2*g)

def cost(lam, k, s, kA):                # protein fraction spent on growth rate lam
    return lam/s + lam/k + lam*lam/(k*kA)

lam0 = lam_small(K, SIGMA)              # 1.2000
for kA in (4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 1e9):
    lam = lam_pool(K, SIGMA, kA)
    print(f"{kA:6.1f} lam*={lam:.4f} phi_R*={lam/SIGMA:.4f} "
          f"a={lam/kA:.4f} tax={100*(1-lam/lam0):5.1f}%")
# the same tax at two measured E. coli rates, sigma from the growth law
SIG_EC = 4.5/0.76                       # slope 1/4.5 RNA/protein, rho = 0.76
for lam in (0.57, 1.58):                # 73 min and 26 min per doubling
    k = lam*SIG_EC/(SIG_EC - lam)       # k re-read at each rate
    print(f"lam={lam} k={k:.3f} tax={100*tax_from_gamma(lam,k,KA):.1f}%")

# marginal vs average protein price of growth
for lam in (0.6, lam_pool(K, SIGMA, KA)):
    print(f"lam={lam:.3f} marg={1/SIGMA+1/K+2*lam/(K*KA):.4f} "
          f"avg={cost(lam,K,SIGMA,KA)/lam:.4f}")

What I would argue against / where this breaks

γ is not identifiable from the growth law. Two observables (λ, φR) at one condition fix σ = λ/φR and leave one equation — (1+λ/kA)/k = 1/λ − 1/σ — for the pair (k, kA). The same point (1.108, 0.369) is produced by (k, kA) = (2.00, 8), (2.24, 4) and (1.88, 16). So the 7.7 % above is a parametrization, not a measurement, and the identifiability argument of §8 — composition pins the limiting capacity — does not extend to the pool: a third observable is needed (the shape of λ(φR) across two nutrient levels, or the pool itself).

Everything depends on the optimum. §10's eigenmode is not the optimum, and if the cell runs a fixed allocation rule fR the pool tax moves the composition as well as the rate, so the "law untouched" argument fails: the observed law becomes a property of the dynamics, not of the budget. My claim is conditional on the maximization assumption, which the lecture itself flags as an assumption about a target rather than about a control rule.

The tax can become a subsidy. a = λ/kA is a steady-state relation. The lecture's own candidate signal — precursor availability feeding back on ribosome synthesis via ppGpp — is a loop designed to lower the pool when supply lags; along such a regulated trajectory a is not monotone in λ and keff can locally rise. The whole exercise presumes an unregulated pool, which is precisely the transient regime the lecture's optional branch A1 is about.

kA is a lump, and the comparison is unit-sensitive. It bundles free amino acids, charged tRNA, energy charge and assembly-ready material; its value would change with carbon source, like σ and k. And γ is dimensionless only if λ, k and kA share units — transplanting the term into the teaching units of Lecture 13's overflow model is therefore a scaling illustration, not a prediction, which is why I did not do it here.
