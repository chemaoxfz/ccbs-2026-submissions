Can a slow response explain the cost of nutrient pulses?

Cells grew faster on shorter nutrient cycles. Fixed response times predict a different pattern of growth losses across the four feeding rhythms.

Siheng Wang  ·  3 November 2026

01 / ProblemSame medium, different clock

Imagine feeding one culture a continuous stream of dilute broth. A second culture gets a poor broth for half of each cycle and a richer broth for the other half. The two cultures experience the same average concentration. If cells take time to speed up after a meal, it seems reasonable that switching more often should make growth more costly.

Nguyen and colleagues ran this experiment with surface-attached Escherichia coli. They alternated 0.1% and 2% LB broth, with a continuous 1.05% control. Growth was faster on a 30-second cycle than on a 15-minute cycle. The authors compared cycling cells with steady controls and also with cells experiencing a single nutrient shift. Here I ask a narrower question. Could the observed dependence on period arise simply because growth approaches its steady value slowly?1

The distinction matters for an experimenter choosing what to measure next. If a slow adjustment suffices, measuring its time constant should predict growth across feeding schedules. If the timing dependence has the wrong direction even after allowing different speeds of adjustment, the next measurement should ask whether the physiological state changes across cycles.

02 / ChallengeWhich steady culture is the right comparison?

Begin with the units. In an idealized culture, let B(t) be its growing biomass and r(t) its instantaneous rate in doublings per hour. A culture doubles once when the accumulated rate reaches one. Over a period of T hours, its average rate is

r¯=1Tlog2(B(T)B(0))=1T∫0Tr(t)dt.

The experiment estimated rates from changes in individual cell volume and pooled these measurements within each experimental day. A biomass growth rate would weight cells by their volume. I use the published single-cell observable for the comparison and use the integral to define the model’s time average.1

Consider three steady rates measured on the same day. Write L for growth in poor broth, H for growth in rich broth and M for growth in broth at their midpoint concentration. If growth could switch instantly between the endpoint rates, spending half the time at each would give J=(L+H)/2. Denote measured growth during cycling by F. Adding and subtracting J splits the loss into two measurable differences.

M−F=(M−J)+(J−F).

The first difference is already present in the three constant environments. For a growth curve that bends downward, the gain from poor to midpoint exceeds the gain from midpoint to rich, so M>J. The second difference is the extra deficit relative to time spent at the two steady endpoint rates. Its sign is an observation. Nguyen et al. used this endpoint reference in Figure 3. The new test below asks what kinds of slow adjustment could account for a departure from it.1

A worked example with a saturating response

Monod used a saturating curve to describe growth on a limiting nutrient. In this example, f(s)=rmaxs/(K+s), where s is nutrient concentration and K is the concentration at half-maximum growth. Set the maximum rate to one doubling per hour and alternate between 0.25K and 1.75K. At the midpoint K, the model gives 0.500. The two endpoints give 0.200 and 0.636, averaging 0.418. The difference of 0.082 doublings per hour comes from curvature alone. Either a 30-second cycle or a one-hour cycle gives the same result under instantaneous switching. These numbers illustrate the geometry. LB is a mixture, and the empirical comparison below uses measured controls directly.2

03 / ApproachFollow the growth lost on the way up

Suppose the current medium sets a target rate u(t), equal to H or L. The actual rate r relaxes toward the target with time constant τ>0. Immediately after an upshift the cell grows too slowly for its new medium. Immediately after a downshift it grows faster than the poor-medium reference. The rates need not reach their targets before the next switch.

τdrdt=u(t)−r(t).

Integrate both sides over one cycle. In a repeating response the final rate equals the initial rate, so the accumulated change on the left is zero. The area lost on the upshift exactly matches the area gained on the downshift, even when both transitions are slow.

r¯=J−τT[r(T)−r(0)]=Jat periodic steady state.

This is a testable property of the proposed response law. Increasing τ shrinks the within-cycle swing but leaves the cycle mean fixed. The boundary term also shows when this reasoning fails. In a startup window the rate may end far from where it began. An experiment testing the prediction should compare complete cycles after that drift has subsided.

At 30 seconds the mean extra deficit is only 0.029 doublings per hour, with day-level differences on both sides of zero. At 15 minutes it is 0.881. Treating the midpoint rate as the only reference would fold the steady gap into both of those numbers. With two to three complete days per period, these are descriptive contrasts. Nguyen et al. also found the 30-second condition to be the exception in their comparison with the endpoint mean.1

Can separate upshift and downshift times rescue the explanation?

Give the same law two positive, fixed time constants. During rich medium the rate approaches H with time constant τH. During poor medium it approaches L with τL. In the repeating cycle, let Δr be the positive rise in rate during the rich half. The rate falls by precisely that amount during the poor half. Integrating the law separately on those two intervals gives

J−r¯=(τH−τL)ΔrT.

A slower upshift now does generate a deficit. Its direction across periods is constrained. For short cycles the rate moves only a little, but it does so many times per hour. For long cycles the swing approaches its finite ceiling H−L, and switches per hour become scarce. Solving the two phases shows that the normalized swing per unit time decreases strictly as the period grows.

Derive the direction for every positive choice of time constants

Let a=exp[−T/(2τH)] and b=exp[−T/(2τL)]. If the rich half begins at r0 and ends at r1, then r1=H+(r0−H)a and r0=L+(r1−L)b. Solving for the swing Δr=r1−r0 gives

ΔrH−L=(1−a)(1−b)1−ab.

To see how the swing changes with period, define a time factor for each phase.

wτ(T)=Tcoth(T4τ).

Substituting the exponential form of the hyperbolic cotangent into the swing gives

J−r¯H−L=2(τH−τL)wτH(T)+wτL(T).

Each denominator term increases with T. Indeed, for x>0, the derivative of xcothx is positive because sinh(2x)>2x. Hence a positive normalized deficit is largest in the short-period limit and shrinks toward zero. A negative deficit approaches zero from below. The result holds for any fixed pair of time constants and any two distinct endpoint rates.

Dividing each day’s deficit by its own endpoint rate difference makes the comparison independent of that difference’s size. The mean fractions are 0.005, 0.349, 0.504 and 0.348 at periods of 0.5, 5, 15 and 60 minutes. A fixed slower upshift predicts its largest positive fraction at the shortest period. The point estimates put the smallest there. On two of the three 15-minute days the fluctuating rate even fell below the low control, outside the range between the model’s two targets. Thus changing the response times alone cannot reproduce the pattern of means under the model assumptions. Different periods were tested on different days, and the experimental estimator pools cells while the model weights time equally. The mismatch concerns the reported means. A formal test would need to account for those differences and for between-day uncertainty.

05 / SignificanceMeasure what a previous pulse leaves behind

The directional test narrows the next question. A cell might change the target it can sustain in rich broth after repeated exposures. It might also carry fuel or enzymes through the poor interval. Nguyen et al. observed that growth after repeated upshifts stabilized faster and at a lower rate than after a single shift from a steady environment. Their model built from isolated shifts also predicted the greatest cost at short periods. The calculation here explains why an entire family of fixed-target responses shares that directional constraint, without fitting a shift trajectory.1

Two experiments show how to look for such a state. Sekar et al. compared wild-type bacteria with a mutant lacking glycogen phosphorylase, an enzyme needed to mobilize stored glycogen. With 30 seconds of rich broth followed by five minutes without carbon, the wild type attained a higher maximum growth rate than the mutant. Their maxima were similar in continuous rich broth. In a different experiment, Lambert and Kussell alternated glucose and lactose. Prior exposure to lactose shortened the delay on a subsequent encounter, and sustained expression of a lactose-processing enzyme shortened recovery. The first experiment motivates measuring stored fuel. The second motivates measuring retained machinery. Neither intervention was performed in the equal-duty LB experiment.34

A discriminating experiment. Repeat the 30-second and 15-minute schedules in parallel on the same days for wild type, a glycogen-mobilization mutant and a complemented strain. Use a tracer to verify the waveform at the cells, and measure each strain’s three steady controls. Compare equal-time phase bins over complete cycles, also reporting the original cell-pooled estimator. Imaging in the published experiment could resolve the cycle mean at 30 seconds but could not resolve its within-cycle growth waveform at the two-minute imaging cadence. Faster imaging or independent phase sampling would be needed for that measurement.1

If losing glycogen mobilization selectively increases the short-period deficit after its own controls are subtracted, and restoring the gene reverses that effect, the result would support a contribution from stored fuel. If all three strains retain the same timing dependence, glycogen mobilization alone would be a poor explanation for that contrast. The decisive measurement is growth in the same medium after different histories, alongside a measurement of the internal state proposed to carry that history.

Data and reproduction

Show the 13 source rows and the calculation

Run this Python 3 script to reproduce the table, normalized deficits and model example. Blank cells record missing controls.

from collections import defaultdict
from statistics import mean
from math import tanh
import csv
import io

raw = '''period_min,date,fluctuating,steady_low,steady_mid,steady_high
0.5,2017-11-12,1.8391,1.0965,2.4439,2.9497
0.5,2017-11-14,2.1229,1.217,2.4692,2.6744
0.5,2018-01-04,1.8373,0.843,2.2755,2.9907
5,2017-10-10,1.6728,1.4814,2.6296,2.8919
5,2017-11-15,1.6198,1.3525,2.3198,2.9588
5,2018-01-11,1.3032,0.9503,2.1744,
15,2017-11-13,1.5064,1.3282,2.4434,3.1206
15,2018-01-12,1.3302,,2.4605,
15,2018-01-16,0.9695,0.987,2.0733,2.7382
15,2018-01-17,0.8595,1.0341,2.0362,2.7467
60,2018-01-29,1.2941,0.7944,2.3412,2.8273
60,2018-01-31,1.1211,0.8357,2.1422,2.7684
60,2018-02-01,1.0272,0.913,2.2123,2.7572'''
results = defaultdict(list)
for row in csv.DictReader(io.StringIO(raw)):
    if not (row['steady_low'] and row['steady_high']):
        continue
    low, mid, high, observed = (float(row[k]) for k in
        ('steady_low', 'steady_mid', 'steady_high', 'fluctuating'))
    endpoints = (low + high) / 2
    extra = endpoints - observed
    results[float(row['period_min'])].append(
        (mid, endpoints, observed, mid-endpoints, extra, extra/(high-low)))

# Columns: period, n, M, J, F, steady gap, extra deficit, normalized deficit.
for period in sorted(results):
    days = results[period]
    print(period, len(days), *[round(mean(column), 5) for column in zip(*days)])

# Check the illustrative Monod example and the fixed lag prediction.
f = lambda s: s / (1 + s)  # s is measured in units of K
print('Monod midpoint and endpoint mean', f(1), (f(.25) + f(1.75))/2)
def deficit_fraction(T, up, down):
    denominator = T/tanh(T/(4*up)) + T/tanh(T/(4*down))
    return 2*(up-down)/denominator
for T in sorted(results):
    print('5 min up, 1 min down:', T, round(deficit_fraction(T,5,1),5))
