A screening score is a property of a design and a seed budget

Software that decides which designed proteins get built reports its verdict as a number. That number depends on how many times you ran the software, and almost nobody says how many.

Yibing Li · a tutorial essay on computational protein design

Designing a protein that sticks to a chosen target used to be a career's worth of work. Since about 2020 it has become a computational workflow a graduate student can run, because three pieces of software will now propose a shape, write a sequence for it, and predict whether that sequence folds into the shape and grips the target. The catch is volume. These tools produce millions of candidate designs, laboratories can test thousands, and so a fourth job appears that nobody had to do before: deciding which candidates are worth the cost of making.

That decision is made by the third tool, AlphaFold, acting as a filter. It predicts the designed complex and emits a confidence number, and designs whose number clears a threshold get their genes ordered. This essay is about a property of that number which is easy to miss and, once seen, hard to unsee. The number is not a property of the design alone. It is a property of the design together with the number of random seeds somebody drew.

That would be a minor bookkeeping point if the dependence were small. It is not. On the only published measurement that varies the seed budget while holding the model, the benchmark and the accuracy threshold fixed, the reported success rate moves from 36% to 61%. The repair is cheap and costs no extra computing: fix the number of seeds in advance, and report the fraction of runs that passed instead of the best one.

The case for that repair, though, is not the one I expected to make. The obvious argument is that extra seeds manufacture lucky passes for weak designs. Fitted against real data, that argument fails by two orders of magnitude, and finding out why is where the essay earns its keep.

The chain of argument

Problem. Computation, not experiment, now decides which designed proteins get made. The filter doing the deciding improves success roughly tenfold, yet still passes mostly non-binders, and its reliability varies tenfold between targets. Sections 1–3.

Challenge. The filter's verdict depends on a parameter nobody standardises or reports: how many random seeds were sampled before the best result was kept. Worse, the statistic used to rank the samples is the statistic used to make the decision, so there is no independent check at the moment of deciding. Sections 4–6.

Approach. Find the one published measurement that varies the seed budget and nothing else, recover its values exactly from the vector data in the PDF rather than reading them off the picture, and fit explicit sampling models to it. Define precisely what each way of reporting a result estimates. Sections 6–9.

Proof. The intuitive "independent attempts" model is refuted by that curve, predicting 100% where 61% is measured. A model in which each target carries its own rate fits a hundred times better, and implies both a hard ceiling near 63% and a strongly U-shaped distribution of rates. Two consequences follow for screening: designs whose reliability differs eighteenfold become indistinguishable, and a pass found at a large budget is weaker evidence than one found at a small budget. Sections 7–12.

Significance. Reporting the pass fraction recovers information that has already been computed and thrown away, and makes published success rates comparable with each other. The proposal is falsifiable using data that already exists, with no new protein made. Sections 13–14.

How to read this

No background in protein design or statistics is assumed. Section 1 builds the biology from scratch; sections 3, 6, 8 and 9 each stop to teach the one idea they need before using it. Section 6 has an interactive demonstration that makes the central point in about thirty seconds, and readers in a hurry could go there first. Section 12 is the crux: it states exactly which part of the evidence carries over to the case the essay is really about, and which part does not.

Every claim that rests on a paper carries a superscript, and every reference links to a PDF stored beside this file. Every number the essay computes comes from scripts in analysis/, listed in the README so they can be re-run.

Problem

A computation decides which proteins get made

What a designed binder is, where in the pipeline a computation turns into a commitment, and what the number it emits actually means.

1Designed binders, and why most fail

Start from nothing: what the object is, what the goal is, and why the problem is one of volume.

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a specific three-dimensional shape, and the shape is what gives it a job. Proteins that grip other proteins do much of the interesting work in biology and medicine: an antibody grips a virus, a receptor grips a hormone, a drug may work by gripping a protein that would otherwise do something harmful. A protein built to grip a chosen target is called a binder, and the region where the two touch is the interface.

The two directions

Prediction asks: given this sequence of amino acids, what shape will it fold into?
Design asks the reverse: given the shape I want, what sequence will produce it?

Prediction was largely solved for single proteins by AlphaFold2 in 2021.8 Design is harder, and the modern approach works by generating a candidate and then running prediction forwards as a check on it. That check is this essay's subject.

The reason design is hard is combinatorial. A short binder of 60 amino acids, with 20 choices at each position, admits 2060 possible sequences, a count vastly larger than the number of atoms in the observable universe. No search can enumerate that space, so design proceeds by generating plausible candidates and filtering them.

Three pieces of software, all released within the last few years, now do this in sequence. RFdiffusion generates a three-dimensional backbone shaped to sit against the chosen target, using a diffusion model of the same family that generates images from noise.6 ProteinMPNN then writes an amino-acid sequence likely to fold into that backbone.7 Finally AlphaFold predicts what the written sequence would actually do, so that the prediction can be compared with what was intended.2 A celebrated early example of the general approach produced miniproteins that block the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein from entering human cells.9

Read that attrition from right to left and the problem states itself. The laboratory can test a few thousand designs. The generator can produce two million. Something has to choose between them, and in practice the chooser is the AlphaFold stage.

2Where the pipeline commits

The AlphaFold stage is not a readout. It is the point at which the pipeline decides what to synthesise.

The filtering protocol in general use comes from Bennett and colleagues. They initialise AlphaFold's internal representation with an encoding of the designed binder structure, supply the target as a template, and keep the designs whose predicted error across the interface is low.2 The arrangement is called AlphaFold with an initial guess: rather than asking the model to find the structure unaided, it is shown the intended answer and asked whether it agrees.

Their threshold, an interface predicted aligned error below 10, is the one most later pipelines inherit. Designs above it are discarded without ever being made.

The filter earns its place. On two targets where the earlier physics-based filters also produced binders, the AlphaFold-filtered libraries had experimental success rates eight and thirty times higher, and on two further targets the physics-based filters produced no successful binders at all.2 A roughly tenfold improvement from a single screening step is a large effect, and it is why the stage exists. The same filter-then-synthesise structure recurs in later campaigns, such as the design of binders to bioactive helical peptides.10

Being load-bearing is exactly what makes the filter's own properties worth examining. If a screen decides which genes get ordered, then whatever the screen depends on, the campaign depends on too. The rest of this part asks what the screen's number means, and how much it is worth.

3What a confidence score estimates

The filter's number is the model's assessment of its own geometric accuracy. It is not a statement about binding, and that difference drives everything later.

Two quantities do the work. Both are worth defining carefully, because the argument later turns on what they are not.

The two confidence numbers

pLDDT, predicted local distance difference test, runs from 0 to 100 and is attached to each individual amino acid. It estimates how accurately that residue's local neighbourhood has been placed. Above 90 the placement is usually reliable; below 50 the region is probably disordered, or simply unknown to the model. AlphaFold was trained to predict this quantity alongside the structure itself.8

PAE, predicted aligned error, is attached to each pair of residues and measured in ångströms. It estimates the error you would expect in one residue's position if the structure were lined up perfectly on another. It therefore reports confidence in the relative arrangement of two parts, which is exactly what is wanted when asking whether a binder sits correctly against its target.

Restricted to the residue pairs that span the two chains, PAE becomes an interface score. That restriction is what Bennett and colleagues call interface pAE.2 It is a sensible thing to screen on, because a model that cannot confidently place the binder against the target has not represented an interface at all.

What the score is not

Both quantities answer one question: how accurate does the model expect its own coordinates to be? Neither answers: how likely is this protein to bind? Nothing in either definition refers to affinity, to a dissociation constant, or to any experimental outcome.

The link between a confident prediction and a real binder is an empirical regularity, established by building designs and testing them. It is not part of what the score means. That distinction is what lets the next section ask how strong the regularity is, and get an answer that is not reassuring.

Challenge

The verdict is not a property of the design

How weak the filter actually is, the free parameter that moves it, and the two different things a screening result could mean.

4How much one pass is worth

A pass is a weak and strongly target-dependent signal, and the field says so in its own papers.

Start with how selective the filter is. Running AlphaFold with an initial guess costs roughly 30 GPU-seconds per design, and only about 2.3% of designs clear the interface pAE threshold; replacing the older sequence-design step with ProteinMPNN followed by a structural relaxation raises that pass rate to about 6.6%.2 So the screen removes most of what it sees.

Now ask what survives. Bennett and colleagues ran their retrospective analysis on the earlier SARS-CoV-2 design set,9 then mounted prospective campaigns against four receptors. Across those four targets the number of experimentally confirmed binders ranged from 1 to 17, out of roughly five thousand filtered designs tested per target. The authors state the position plainly: success rates among the targets remain low (<1%).2 A design that passes the screen is, in those campaigns, still far more likely than not to be a non-binder.

A later meta-analysis puts a number on how much this varies. Pooling 3,766 experimentally characterised binders across 15 targets, Overath and colleagues report that the precision achieved at the best available threshold, meaning the fraction of designs above the cutoff that really bind, ranges from 0.1 to 1 depending on the target.4 On some targets a passing score means almost everything; on others almost nothing. There is no single number for what a pass is worth.

The designers of newer pipelines say the same. The BindCraft authors, reporting experimental success rates between 10% and 100% with an average of 46.3%, nonetheless record that when they assessed AlphaFold3 as a filter they still found a large proportion of false positive predictions.3 In the same discussion they flag a limitation of their own method that will matter in section 12:

a potential limitation is the use of the AF2 i_pTM metric for the ranking of designs Pacesa et al., 2025
The problem, stated

The AlphaFold screen is a weak classifier doing load-bearing work. It improves success roughly tenfold, admits a few per cent of designs, and still passes mostly non-binders, at a rate that varies by an order of magnitude between targets. A signal that weak can be moved by a parameter nobody is holding fixed, and the next section shows that one is.

5The same model reports 36% or 61%

Hold the model, the benchmark and the accuracy threshold fixed. Vary only how many random seeds you draw. The headline moves by 25 percentage points.

A random seed is the number that initialises a program's random-number generator. Run the same model on the same input with a different seed and you get a different sample from the same distribution, in the way that shuffling a deck twice gives two different orders. In AlphaFold3 the structure module is a diffusion model, so the seed drives the sampling directly; the authors note that the model can be run with different random seeds to generate alternative results, and that unless stated otherwise their results come from selecting the top confidence sample from running 5 seeds of the same trained model, with 5 diffusion samples per model seed, for a total of 25 samples to choose from.1 Twenty-five samples computed, one reported.

The AlphaFold3 paper also measures what that choice buys. For antibody-antigen targets with little homology to anything in training, it plots the percentage predicted correctly against the number of seeds per target, from 1 to 1,000, ranking the samples by a confidence score. Top-ranked predictions keep improving all the way out, with the difference between 5 and 1,000 seeds significant at P = 2.0 × 10−5. I recovered that panel's values from the vector drawing operators inside the published PDF, so the numbers below are exact up to axis calibration rather than estimated by eye from the picture.

At one seed, 36% of targets are predicted correctly. At a thousand, 61%. Same model, same targets, same definition of correct. The only thing that changed is how many times the dice were rolled before the best roll was kept.

This is not a criticism of the AlphaFold3 authors, who plotted the dependence explicitly and labelled the axis. It is a statement about what a success rate is. Reported without its seed budget, it is incomplete in the way a length is incomplete without its units. And budgets do differ widely in practice: a later antibody-docking study notes that the 60% figure is with using 1000 seeds, against their own single-seed results on a different benchmark, and separately reports generating 100 seeds per target simply to find how many were needed before a median stopped moving.5 They settled on twenty.

What this comparison can and cannot establish

Figure 3 is a like-for-like measurement, and the 36%-to-61% claim rests on it alone. The cross-paper comparison in the previous paragraph is not like-for-like: the two benchmarks differ, as do the accuracy thresholds each quotes. That is the point being made rather than a flaw in it. Because the seed budget is neither standardised nor always stated, published success rates are not generally comparable, and the contribution of the budget cannot be separated from the contribution of the method.

6Two readouts, two estimands

Before asking which way of reporting is better, say precisely what each one is an estimate of. The whole argument is contained in the difference.

Fix a single candidate, and fix everything about how it is run except the seed: the sequence, the database of related sequences, the model weights, the number of refinement cycles, the templates, and the initial-guess setting. Each run then either passes the screening criterion or it does not. Write p for the probability that one run passes. This is a property of that candidate under that configuration, and it is what differs between designs.

The one piece of statistics needed

A trial with two outcomes and a fixed probability is a Bernoulli trial: a weighted coin. Here the coin is one AlphaFold run, heads is "passes the threshold", and p is how heavily the coin is weighted. The weighting is a property of the design, and the whole point of a screen is to find the designs whose coin is heavily weighted towards heads.

An estimator is a recipe for guessing a quantity from data. It is unbiased if, averaged over many repetitions, it lands on the true value rather than systematically above or below it. Holding that word in mind for two paragraphs is all the preparation this essay needs.

Now draw n runs and let k be the number that passed. Two quantities can be reported, and current practice reports the first.

The two readouts

Best-of-n. Report the single best run, or equivalently report that the candidate passed if any run passed. The probability of that event is

P(at least one pass )=1− (1−p) n (1)

The pass fraction. Report k/n, which has expectation

E[k/n] =p (2)

Equation (1) says that every candidate's reported outcome climbs towards certainty as the budget grows, whatever its p, with no change to the design whatever. Equation (2) says the pass fraction is an unbiased estimator of the candidate's own rate, and that its expectation does not depend on the budget at all. That is the whole difference, and the demonstration below makes it visible.

Demo · the same twenty runs, reported two ways

Set how reliable a design is, and how many seeds you spend on it. Each square is one AlphaFold run. Then compare the two lines underneath: one of them changes when you move the budget slider, and one of them does not.

Move the budget slider with the rate held fixed. The design has not changed, yet the best-of-n verdict flips from "fails" to "passes" and then stays there. Move it further and the pass fraction wobbles around its true value but does not drift. A reader given only a best-of-n result cannot invert equation (1) without knowing n, and even knowing it, recovers only a single yes-or-no observation about a quantity between 0 and 1.

Equation (1) is also a model of the curve in Figure 3, if one assumes that every target shares the same p and that runs are independent. Figure 3 shows it missing the measurement badly. The next section asks by how much, and what that failure reveals.

Part III

Why "more chances" is the wrong account

The intuitive model of seed sampling is refuted by the measurement it is supposed to explain. What replaces it is more informative.

7Independence fails by two orders of magnitude

If extra seeds simply bought extra independent chances, the curve in Figure 2 would have flattened at the top within ten runs. It does not.

The intuitive account of best-of-n sampling is the one equation (1) encodes: each run is an independent attempt, so more attempts mean more chances for one of them to succeed. Take it seriously as a model of Figure 2, give every target the same rate, and fit it. The one-seed point fixes p = 0.362 immediately, since at n = 1 equation (1) returns p. Extrapolating to a thousand seeds:

What the code produced

With p = 0.362 and independent runs, equation (1) predicts 100.000% of targets correct at n = 1,000. The measured value is 60.9%. Fitting p freely to all nineteen digitized points rather than anchoring on the first gives a best homogeneous fit of p = 0.039 with a root mean squared error of 0.326, which is the size of the effect being modelled. The model is not approximately right. (analysis/seed_budget.py)

Two orders of magnitude is not a correction term. It means the mechanism is wrong, and the direction of the failure says how. The measured curve rises far more slowly than independence allows and then flattens, while the independent model saturates almost immediately. Something is preventing additional seeds from delivering additional chances.

The reason is visible in how these models work. Runs of the same model on the same input are not independent trials of a fair process. They share the weights, the multiple sequence alignment, the templates, and whatever the model has learned about this sequence. Wayment-Steele and colleagues make the structured character of this variation concrete: to get AlphaFold2 to produce an alternative conformation reliably they had to change the composition of the alignment by sequence clustering, because the alignment, not the seed, selects which state comes back.11 Relatedly, AlphaFold's confidence behaves like a learned energy-like preference over structures rather than a lookup of memorised answers,12 which is precisely the sort of thing that returns the same confident answer again and again. A seed perturbs the sampling. It does not give the model a fresh opinion.

A correction to the obvious framing

The natural way to raise this issue, and the way I first framed it, is: run enough seeds and something will eventually pass by luck, so the headline overstates. That argument is quantitatively wrong, and the measurement that refutes it is the same one that motivates the essay. Seeds are correlated strongly enough that luck runs out fast. The real cost of best-of-n is different, and sections 8 to 11 develop it.

8Per-target rates, and a ceiling

Let each target keep its own rate, and allow some targets to be unreachable. The curve is then reproduced closely, and it comes with a hard limit.

The second piece of statistics needed

Section 7 gave every target the same coin. The obvious repair is to let each target have its own coin, which means replacing the single number p with a distribution over possible values of p: a statement of how common each degree of reliability is across targets.

The Beta distribution is the standard choice for a quantity that must lie between 0 and 1. It has two shape parameters, a and b. When both exceed 1 it is a hump with most targets near the middle. When both fall below 1 it is the opposite: a U, with mass piled at the two ends and the middle hollowed out. Which of those two shapes comes back is a result, not an assumption, and section 9 is about the one that did.

Replace the single shared rate with a distribution. Each target i carries its own per-seed rate pi. A fraction f of targets are reachable, with pi drawn from a Beta distribution; the remaining 1 − f have pi = 0 and are never predicted correctly, at any budget. The expected success rate at budget n is then the average of equation (1) over that distribution:

S(n)= f(1− B(a,b+n) B(a,b) ) (3)

where B is the beta function. Equation (3) has three free parameters against nineteen points.

What the code produced

Best fit: a reachable fraction f = 0.634, with Beta(a = 0.317, b = 0.244) over the reachable targets. Root mean squared error 0.0033, a hundredfold improvement on the homogeneous fit. The ceiling that follows is 63.4%: roughly 37% of these targets are not predicted correctly at any seed budget whatever. (analysis/seed_budget.py)

The ceiling converts the vague phrase "diminishing returns" into a schedule. Reaching 90% of what sampling can ever deliver takes about 97 seeds. Reaching 95% takes about 861. Reaching 99% takes roughly 138,000. The useful range is the first hundred or so runs, which is the same order as the twenty seeds that Hitawala and Gray arrived at empirically by generating a hundred per target and watching a median stop moving.5

So the model fits, and its fitted parameters are interpretable. Both Beta shape parameters came back below 1, which is a strong statement about the shape of the rate distribution rather than merely its mean. That is worth looking at directly.

9Near-certain or near-hopeless

The fitted per-seed rates are U-shaped. Most targets are nearly always right or nearly always wrong, and the thin middle is the only place a seed budget changes anything.

A Beta distribution with both shape parameters below 1 piles its mass at the two ends and hollows out the middle. Since the fit returned a = 0.317 and b = 0.244, the implication is that targets are mostly either reliably solved or reliably missed, with relatively few sitting at intermediate rates. Before taking that seriously, it is worth checking whether the U-shape is a property of the data or an artefact of choosing a Beta.

How to fit a shape without assuming one

Lay out a grid of candidate rates: 0, 0.02, 0.04, and so on up to 1. Ask what weight each rate should carry so that the resulting mixture reproduces the measured curve, requiring only that weights cannot be negative, since a negative share of targets is meaningless. This is a standard problem called nonnegative least squares, and it has a unique answer.

The point of doing it is that the grid can express any shape at all: a hump, a U, a spike, several spikes. If the U-shape survives when nothing forces it, the U-shape is in the data.

The check is to refit with no shape assumption at all: put a grid of candidate rates from 0 to 1, including an exact zero, and solve for nonnegative weights on that grid by nonnegative least squares. The free mixture can produce any shape the data prefer.

What the code produced

Under the fitted Beta, 22.7% of all targets have a per-seed rate above 0.9 and 51.4% below 0.1. Under the free mixture, 30.2% above 0.9 and 49.9% below 0.1, with 41.0% of the total mass sitting at exactly zero and only 19.9% anywhere between 0.1 and 0.9. The free mixture's own fit error is 0.0068, still fifty times better than the homogeneous model. (analysis/robustness.py)

The two methods agree on the shape and differ by a few points on the particulars, which is what should happen when a three-parameter family and a fifty-one-point grid are fitted to the same nineteen numbers. Neither is precise enough to support a confidence interval, and none is quoted.

There is independent corroboration, and it comes from a direction that did not involve fitting anything. Hitawala and Gray, benchmarking AlphaFold3 on their own post-cutoff antibody set, describe the distribution of their accuracy scores as bimodal, concluding that the model either has difficulty sampling the correct binding interfaces or generates high-quality complexes.5 That is the same statement arrived at by observation: easy or hopeless, with little in between.

The claim

A seed budget is close to irrelevant for most targets, because most targets are near-certain or near-hopeless. Its leverage over whether a candidate ever passes is concentrated on the minority with intermediate rates. Whether that concentration is a problem depends on what gets reported from the runs, which is the subject of Part IV.

Part IV

What best-of-n costs a screen

Two consequences that follow from the fitted model, and then the exact point at which a structure-prediction result stops bearing on a design screen.

10An 18-fold gap, collapsed onto one bit

Two designs whose per-run reliability differs eighteenfold both pass a twenty-seed screen. The pass fraction separates them immediately.

Take two candidates. The first passes the screening criterion on 90% of runs. The second passes on 5%. Under any reading, these are different designs: one reproduces its intended interface almost whenever asked, the other does so occasionally. A pair like this is not exotic. Under the distribution fitted in section 9, roughly half of all candidates sit below 0.1 and roughly a quarter above 0.9, so two candidates drawn from opposite ends of it are the common case rather than a contrived one.

Now run each at a twenty-seed budget and report whether anything passed.

What the code produced

At n = 20, the reliable candidate passes with probability 1.0000 and the marginal one with probability 0.6415. At n = 50 the figures are 1.0000 and 0.9231. The pass fractions, meanwhile, remain near 0.90 and 0.05 regardless of budget. (analysis/seed_budget.py)

Two designs differing eighteenfold in per-run reliability produce the same binary outcome most of the time. That is the cost, and it is a cost of the reporting rule rather than of the sampling. The twenty runs needed to compute k/n are the same twenty runs that were already performed. Every number required to separate the candidates was computed and then discarded in favour of the maximum.

It is worth being precise about what k/n would and would not tell you here. It estimates how reproducibly the screen endorses this design under a fixed configuration. It does not estimate the probability that the protein binds. A design with k/n = 0.9 has a reproducible computational endorsement, which given the base rates in section 4 still leaves it far more likely than not to be a non-binder. The claim is comparative, not absolute: between two designs that both passed, the one that passed 18 times out of 20 and the one that passed once are different pieces of evidence, and current practice records them identically.

11The winner's curse

The larger the budget that produced a pass, the weaker the candidate that pass is likely to belong to.

Section 10 compared two fixed candidates. Now ask the question a screen actually faces: given that some candidate passed, what is it likely to be? Selection on a sampled maximum is not neutral, because a large budget rescues weak candidates more often than it rescues strong ones, who needed no rescuing.

Under the mixture of section 8, with p distributed as Beta(a, b) over reachable candidates, the expected rate of whatever passed is the ratio of two integrals, both of which have closed forms:

E[p∣ pass in n]= E[p(1− (1−p)n )] E[1− (1−p)n ] (4)

Panel B of Figure 4 evaluates it. At a single seed, a pass implies an expected per-run rate of 0.84, because at one seed only a reliable candidate is likely to pass at all. At twenty seeds the same event implies 0.67. At a thousand, 0.59.

The claim

A pass obtained at a large budget is weaker evidence than a pass obtained at a small one. Because the budget is usually unreported, a reader cannot tell which kind of pass they are being shown. This is a property of selecting on a sampled maximum, and it does not depend on the specific Beta. Any non-degenerate distribution of rates produces the same monotone decline, because larger budgets progressively admit candidates with lower rates. Only if every candidate shared one rate would the decline vanish, and section 9 is the evidence that they do not.

The effect is modest in size, a decline of about a quarter over three decades of budget, and it points the same way as section 10. Both are consequences of reporting a maximum. Neither, so far, has been measured on an actual design screen, and the next section explains why that gap is not a detail.

12Where the correspondence holds, and where it breaks

Figure 2 measures structure prediction against known answers. This essay is about screening designs with no known answer. The sampling arithmetic transfers. The validity does not.

Everything quantitative in Parts III and IV derives from one digitized curve, and that curve comes from antibody–antigen docking evaluated against solved crystal structures. A binder screen is a different activity. The correspondence has to be stated exactly, or the argument is an analogy.

What is shared. In both settings there is a candidate, a run that either succeeds or fails under a fixed configuration, a per-candidate rate p, a budget n, and a rule that reports the best of the n outcomes. Equations (1) through (4) describe that structure and nothing more. They are statements about Bernoulli trials and selection, so they hold in any setting with those ingredients, including a binder screen. The empirical content carried over from Figure 2 is narrower: that per-run outcomes for a fixed candidate are strongly correlated across seeds, and that rates vary enormously between candidates. Both are properties of the model and the sampling, not of the evaluation criterion, which is why it is reasonable to expect them in a screen.

Where it breaks, and it breaks in the direction that matters. In prediction, the statistic used to rank the samples and the statistic used to score the result are different. AlphaFold3 ranks by interface pTM and is then scored by DockQ against an experimental structure.1 Sampling harder is therefore honest search: the answer is fixed in advance, and more samples genuinely find it more often. The seed-budget curve in Figure 1 measures a real improvement.

In screening, the two statistics are the same. The interface pAE that ranks the samples is the interface pAE that decides whether to order the gene. There is no independent criterion at decision time, because the experimental outcome does not exist yet and will not for months. Sampling harder moves the readout without moving the design.

This is not a hypothetical concern raised from outside the field. It is what the BindCraft authors flagged about their own pipeline when they named the use of interface pTM for ranking designs as a limitation,3 and it is why they added orthogonal self-consistency checks rather than simply sampling more. It is also why Hitawala and Gray, when they wanted to evaluate confidence metrics as screening filters, deliberately took the top-ranked prediction from twenty seeds, describing it as a way to simulate the confidence filtration common in blind prediction ranking.5 The practice is standard. The budget behind it is not reported.

The limit of what is established here

Nothing in this essay measures k/n on a binder screen, because no published dataset reports it. Figure 2 establishes that seed budget moves a success rate in structure prediction. Sections 8 to 11 establish what follows arithmetically if a screen has the same sampling structure. Whether it does is an empirical question, and section 14 proposes the experiment that would answer it. Until then the recommendation in section 13 rests on the cost of the reporting change being near zero, not on a demonstration that it improves selection.

Part V

What to report, and what would settle it

A reporting rule that costs nothing, and the experiment that would show whether it is worth anything.

13The reporting rule

Fix the budget in advance, state the configuration, report the fraction and the runs. Every one of those runs has already been performed.

The argument so far says that a screening result is incomplete without its seed budget, and that the maximum discards the information needed to tell reproducible endorsements from marginal ones. Both problems are fixed by what is written down, not by what is computed. Concretely:

The rule
  1. Preregister the budget. Choose n before looking at any result, and keep it the same across the designs being compared. A budget chosen after seeing that one design needed more attempts is not a budget, it is a selection.
  2. Freeze and state the configuration. The sequence, the multiple sequence alignment or the single-sequence setting, the model weights, the recycle count, the templates, and the initial-guess setting. Only the seed varies. Section 6's p is defined relative to this configuration and means nothing without it.
  3. Report k/n, with the per-run scores. Not the best score and not the mean alone. The distribution of pLDDT and interface pAE across runs is already in the output files.
  4. Call it what it is. k/n measures how reproducibly the screen endorses a design. It is not a probability of binding, and section 4's base rates are the reason.

The cost is close to zero for any pipeline that already samples. AlphaFold3's default draws twenty-five samples and reports one.1 BindCraft runs hundreds to thousands of trajectories per target.3 In both cases the runs that k/n needs have been executed and their scores written to disk. What is proposed is to stop throwing them away.

Where a pipeline currently uses a single seed, as many published protocols do, the rule does carry a real compute cost, and section 8 bounds it usefully: the first hundred or so runs deliver most of what sampling can ever deliver, so a budget of ten or twenty is a defensible place to start rather than an arbitrary one. For a filter costing roughly 30 GPU-seconds per design,2 a twenty-seed budget on a few thousand designs is a substantial but not prohibitive expense, and it would be spent on the stage that decides what gets synthesised.

14The cheapest test, and how this could be wrong

The proposal is falsifiable with data that already exists, and there is a specific result that would retire it.

The experiment does not need new proteins. It needs re-running an existing screen with the seed varied and the outcome already known.

The test

Take a published set of designs with recorded experimental outcomes, for instance the 3,766 characterised binders assembled by Overath and colleagues.4 Re-run the AlphaFold initial-guess screen at a fixed budget of twenty seeds per design, with the configuration in section 13 held constant. For each design, record k/n and the best single-run score. Then ask one question: does k/n predict experimental binding better than the best single run does, as measured by average precision on the same designs?

The comparison is the point. The best single run is current practice, so it is the baseline that k/n must beat to be worth reporting. Because both quantities come from the same twenty runs, the comparison is free of any difference in compute, and because the outcomes are already published, it is free of any new wet-lab work.

Three results would weaken or retire the argument, and they should be stated as plainly as the claim. If k/n turns out to be near 0 or near 1 for nearly every design that passes, then the maximum discards nothing and the reporting change buys nothing; section 9's thin middle would be thinner in a screen than in antibody docking. If k/n predicts binding no better than the single best score, it is a repeatability diagnostic with no decision value, which is a considerably weaker claim than this essay makes. And if the AlphaFold2 initial-guess protocol turns out to be effectively deterministic across seeds once the configuration is frozen, then the premise simply does not apply to that pipeline, although it would continue to apply to the diffusion-based samplers that have replaced it.

What the evidence does establish is narrower than the recommendation and worth separating from it. A published success rate moves from 36% to 61% on one model and one benchmark with nothing varying but the seed budget, and that budget is not standardised across the papers that report such rates. The independence model that would explain this as accumulated luck is refuted by the same measurement, by two orders of magnitude. What replaces it is a strongly bimodal distribution of per-candidate rates, fitted two ways and corroborated by an independent observation of bimodality, under which sampling matters little for most candidates and a great deal for the minority in between. For that minority, reporting the maximum discards the only information that distinguishes them, and the information has already been computed.

Whether recovering it improves which genes get ordered is not yet known. It is, however, answerable with twenty seeds, a published outcome column, and no new protein at all.
