Resource allocation: constraints, objectives, and algorithms

From proteome budgets to optimal control. Theory gives the feasible set and the objective; algorithms give the solution and its verification.

Fang Lipei · 20261203497 · course essay

Resource allocation is a narrow mathematical problem: under a total budget, divide limited resources among competing uses. In a cell the resources are proteome, energy and time; in a factory they are budget, equipment and labour. The form is the same, a choice under constraints.

The same problem has three levels. The steady-state level asks what the shares are in a given environment. The dynamic level asks how the shares change from an old environment to a new one, which is an optimal control problem. The fluctuation level asks which policy maximises long-run growth when the environment switches repeatedly, which is a periodic-average or stochastic control problem. All three share the same budget constraint. They differ in time axis and objective.

This essay runs a two-sector model through all three levels. Section 1 writes allocation as a budget. Section 2 states the steady-state problem and explains why its linear programming version is a slice rather than an equivalent. Section 3 gives the costate and the switching function, and states what is and is not proved. Section 4 compares algorithm families and their conditions. Section 5 works a reproducible example. Section 6 lists common errors and a checklist. Every number is produced by analysis/run_analysis.py.

How to read this

A reader who wants only the methods can stop at the algorithm table at the end of Section 4. A reader who wants only the example can go to Section 5. Part I

Theory

Write the constraint, then the objective, then talk about optimality. Reverse the order and the conclusion drifts.

1Budget: allocation as a constraint

Shares summing to one is an accounting identity, not a mechanism.

Coarse-grain the proteome into three sectors: ribosomes φR, biosynthetic enzymes φA, and fixed functions φQ. The budget is

φR+φA+ φQ=1 (1)

This equation says only that the three parts add to one. It does not say which part should be large, and it does not say which one limits the others. Treating an accounting identity as a mechanism is a common error in this literature. The constraint tells us what can be chosen. The mechanism tells us why this point is chosen.

Growth is set by the tightest sector. Ribosomes provide translational capacity, enzymes provide biosynthetic capacity, and growth takes the smaller of the two:

μ=kφR ⋅min(1, φAφA* ) (2)

Here k is the translational capacity per unit ribosome share, and φA* is the enzyme share required in the current environment. Taking the smaller is not a mathematical trick. Each pathway has a capacity ceiling, and the realised flux cannot exceed the smaller of the two. This form turns the question where the limitation sits into a computable object.

The two sides differ. When the enzyme share is above requirement, extra enzyme does not raise growth, because the enzyme term is capped at one, and it only occupies budget that ribosomes could use. When the enzyme share is below requirement, growth is bilinear in the two shares, so the trade-off is not symmetric: the marginal return per unit share depends on the current shares. A model that treats both sides as linear misses this.

Observationally, shares come from quantitative proteomics and growth rates from culture or dilution rate1. Both are measurable, but units must agree. Proteome mass fraction, dry-mass fraction and volume fraction cannot be interchanged. Mix the units and the optimal solution means nothing.

Definition

A resource-allocation problem has three parts: a resource vector with a budget constraint, allocatable variables, and an objective. Without all three it is not an optimisation problem, only a description. The reserve q used later is a ratio, q = A(0)/A*, not a proteome fraction.

2Steady state: capacity constraints and a linear slice

The general problem is nonlinear. The linear program is a slice of it, useful for intuition but not equivalent.

Fix the environment and maximise growth. The constraints are the two capacities, the budget, and non-negativity:

maxμ s.t. μ≤kφR, μ≤kφR φAφA*, φR+φA+φQ=1, φR,φA≥0 (3)

The second constraint is bilinear in φR and φA, so this is not a linear program. No single constant coefficient on φA reproduces it across the feasible set.

With φQ and φR fixed, the budget fixes φA = S − φR, where S = 1 − φQ. No allocation decision is left on this slice. What remains is to evaluate the two growth bounds at that point, the translation bound and the enzyme bound. Evaluating a point is not optimising over it.

Substituting the budget reduces the steady problem to one variable. Write a = φA and S = 1 − φQ. Then

μ(a)= k(S−a) min(1, aφA* ) (4)

On the branch a < φA*, the derivative is k(S − 2a)/φA*, so an interior optimum sits at a = S/2. The requirement branch caps it at φA*:

aopt= min( φA*, S2) (5)

For the parameters used here, S = 0.45 and φA* = 0.15, so aopt = 0.15, φR = 0.30 and μopt = 1.44 h⁻¹. During feast the enzyme does not limit growth. If its share could go to zero, the maximum would be kS = 2.16 h⁻¹. The quoted value near 2.0 retains an enzyme share of about 0.03, and without that retained share the rich maximum is 2.16.

An illustrative linear program is a different object. Give the two capacities independent coefficients, μ≤kRφR and μ≤kAφA, with constants k_R and k_A. Both constraints can bind, and a finite optimum is attained at a vertex when it is unique. Alternative optima occur when the objective is constant along an optimal face, not because the constraint matrix is degenerate. Two tight constraints alone do not imply that shares are unidentifiable.

RBA writes capacity constraints that are linear in protein fractions; the two-capacity form above is a minimal illustration, not the full RBA model. The two-sector dynamics below is not RBA.

Steady-state models often use an empirical relation: ribosome share rises linearly with growth rate. Written with a slope and an intercept, the intercept is often read as excess ribosomes and the slope as translational capacity2. This is an empirical constraint, not a mechanism. Put it into a model and it fits steady-state data. Treat it as a causal explanation and fitted parameters become regulatory goals.

An easy confusion

A linear relation can arise from two very different mechanisms: capacity ceilings, or regulatory feedback. Steady-state linearity alone cannot separate them. Separation needs a perturbation experiment, not another fit4.

3Dynamics: optimal control and costates

Add a time axis and the objective enters the answer. The costate is the dynamic version of a shadow price.

The dynamic model treats shares as states and the split of synthesis flux as the control. Let u be the share sent to ribosomes and v the share sent to the enzyme sector, with u+v=1−φQ. Shares dilute with growth:

φR˙= μ(u−φR) , φA˙= μ(v−φA) (6)

Here S = 1 − φQ, u = S − v, and admissible controls satisfy 0 ≤ v ≤ min(vmax, S). The horizon T and the initial state are fixed, and the objective is J = ∫0Tμdt. Because the budget is conserved, φR = S − φA, so a one-state formulation is enough. On the smooth branch φA < φA*, μ = k(S − φA)φA/φA*. At φA = φA* the growth law is not differentiable, so the costate derivative is read branch by branch, and the threshold is handled by matching branches and checking the costate jump. These are necessary conditions, not a proof of global optimality.

The objective can be accumulated growth over a finite horizon, terminal biomass, or the time to recover a given share. When biomass follows B˙=μB, with fixed initial biomass, maximising terminal biomass and maximising accumulated growth are the same problem, so these two are not different objectives. The time-to-recovery objective is genuinely different.

Once the objective is written as a functional, Pontryagin conditions give costate equations and a switching function. The Hamiltonian is

H=μ+ λRμ(u−φR) +λAμ(v−φA) (7)

After substituting u = S − v, the control enters linearly, so the switching function is

σ=∂H∂v =μ(λA−λR) (8)

The costates satisfy λ˙R=−∂H∂φR,λ˙A=−∂H∂φA with terminal condition λ(T)=0 for free terminal states. If σ keeps one sign, the optimal control sits on a boundary. A zero of the switching function at an isolated time is an ordinary switching point, not a singular arc. A singular arc requires the switching function to vanish on an interval, together with its time derivatives, and the state constraints must be checked separately. This essay does not claim a singular arc for the two-sector model.

The numerical example does not solve the full optimal control problem. It evaluates a restricted policy family: a constant enzyme allocation during feast, and the cap during famine until the requirement is met, holding only if it is met within the famine. Within that family the scan returns the best member. The word best below always means best in this family, not a proof of global optimality. State constraints still matter: shares cannot be negative and synthesis flux has an upper bound, so numerical solutions show saturation at those bounds. A report should say which bounds are physical and which are algorithmic.

When the objective becomes the long-run average growth rate, the problem becomes periodic optimal control. The average weakens the terminal condition, and the policy depends on environmental statistics: how long the feast is, how long the famine is5. If the feast duration tends to infinity while the famine duration stays fixed, the cycle approaches a rich phase followed by a fixed-length famine, which is not a static environment. The static model describes a single fixed environment. A periodic problem does not approach it by lengthening the period alone: long phases approach separate steady states within each environment, and the cycle still switches. The zero-reserve end of the scan is therefore a conjecture about one limit, not a derived consequence. Sadeghi Arjmand writes RBA as a finite-horizon optimal control problem and shows that the static solution is a limiting case3.

Three sentences of theory

The constraint sets the feasible set. The objective sets the optimal solution. The costate sets the relative price of resources across time. Each holds on its own, and together they form the allocation theory. Data can rule out mechanisms, parameters and objectives; they do not choose the control formalism.

Part II

Algorithms

The mathematical form chooses the algorithm. Do not solve a linear program with dynamic optimisation, and do not replace verification with fitting.

4Choosing among algorithms

First decide which level the problem belongs to, then choose the algorithm. Structure, constraints and available solvers decide the details.

The steady-state layer is a constrained optimisation. When the constraints are linear, the simplex method and interior-point methods are the standard choices. They differ in scaling and warm-start behaviour, so the choice depends on problem structure and solver availability, not on size alone. The solution alone is not enough: dual variables and sensitivity say which constraint is genuinely tight and which parameter deserves another measurement. Constraint generation suits many variables with few active constraints: solve a relaxation, then add violated constraints one at a time.

The dynamic level has three families. Direct methods parameterise the control, or discretise both states and controls in a collocation scheme, and turn the problem into a nonlinear program. They are robust and accept state constraints and bounds easily. The cost is many variables and a need for good initial guesses. Indirect methods use Pontryagin conditions to turn the problem into a two-point boundary value problem, solved by shooting or collocation. They return costates and are accurate near a known solution, but are sensitive to initial guesses and awkward with state constraints. Dynamic programming solves the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, which suits low-dimensional and stochastic problems and becomes infeasible as dimension grows.

The practical order is this. Use a direct method to get a feasible solution and the shape of the policy. Then use an indirect method to check the costates and the switching function. Agreement between the two is evidence, not proof of a global optimum. Check grid independence, and tighten tolerances until the conclusion stops moving. If the optimum sits on a boundary, confirm that the boundary is physical and not a numerical cap.

Three implementation details matter. First, scaling: shares lie between 0 and 1, growth rates between 0 and 2, and flux parameters span orders of magnitude, so unscaled finite differences lose accuracy. Second, initial guesses: direct methods are forgiving, indirect methods are extremely sensitive to costate guesses, so estimate the policy shape with a direct method first. Third, verification: substitute the solution back into the state equations for residuals, substitute the costates into the transversality conditions, and resolve on a different grid.

The fluctuation layer needs evaluation before optimisation. Periodic problems use a cycle map, iterated to a fixed point, with a one- or low-dimensional scan outside. Stochastic problems estimate the average growth rate by Monte Carlo, but Monte Carlo evaluates a policy; finding the policy also needs a search or a learning step. Sample size is set by variance, and the report should give an interval rather than a single mean.

Parameter estimation and model validation form a separate layer. Least squares gives point estimates, profile likelihood gives intervals, and identifiability analysis says which parameters cannot be pinned down together. Cross-validation checks prediction rather than fit. A model that fits existing data has not thereby shown that it predicts a new perturbation.

5A reproducible example

Run a two-sector model through the three levels, with numbers from a script rather than memory.

Parameters are orders of magnitude: k is 4.8 h⁻¹, the fixed share is 0.55, the enzyme share required in minimal medium is 0.15, and the cap on the enzyme synthesis share is 0.18. During feast the enzyme is not limiting; during famine it is. At steady state the model gives a rich-medium growth rate near 2.0 h⁻¹ with the retained 0.03 enzyme share, and a minimal-medium rate near 1.44 h⁻¹. These match the parameter table and serve as a unit check.

Start with a single downshift. Take four initial reserves, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8. The policy runs enzyme synthesis at the cap, then holds once demand is met. Onset times are 0.12, 0.16, 0.26 and 0.51 h (Figure 1). A larger reserve has a higher absolute target, so the relative 25% threshold is crossed later. This does not mean recovery is slower: with a larger reserve the cell starts closer to its final growth rate. Within the shift, a larger initial reserve shifts budget from ribosomes to enzyme. Over the interval 0.2 to 0.8 the net effect on growth is positive, so the best reserve sits at the upper boundary of that interval. This does not show that a single shift cannot select a finite reserve outside the interval. The cost of carrying the reserve is paid before the shift, so a single shift does not price it; whether a finite reserve can be selected depends on the prior environment.

The cycle layer prices the reserve. Holding the enzyme share during feast takes synthesis flux that could go to ribosomes, while the benefit arrives at the start of the next famine. Define r as the constant enzyme synthesis share during feast, with r in [0, min(vmax, S)]. Famine uses the cap until φA reaches φA*, then holds; if the requirement is not reached within the famine, the cap stays on. The state at the start of a cycle is the fixed point of the cycle map. The quantity maximised is

μ¯(r)= 1T ∫0T μ(t)dt (9)

The scan uses 31 grid points on [0.005, 0.15] (spacing 0.00483), then bounded refinement with xatol 1e-6. Integration uses rtol 1e-9 and atol 1e-11, and the fixed-point tolerance is 1e-9. At the best point the average is 1.6680 h⁻¹; changing r by ±0.005 changes it by 0.06 to 0.08%, and by ±0.01 by 0.23 to 0.35%. The residual 8e-11 shows periodic closure, not optimisation accuracy.

Scanning the feast gives Figure 3: over 0.5 to 8 h, the best reserve falls as the feast lengthens. The longer the feast, the longer a given reserve must be maintained, so the cost grows while the benefit stays fixed at the start of each downshift. A reserve of zero is a conjecture about an infinite feast, not a result of the scan.

Within this model the observed 0.21 corresponds to a feast of about 2.5 h with a 1 h famine, or about 3.4 h with longer famines. The local slope is about -0.18 per hour for a 1 h famine and -0.07 for longer famines, so a reserve measured to 0.05 places the feast within 0.3 to 0.7 h. These are conditional correspondences, not estimates of a real environment.

Numbers and their assumptions

Onset times at reserves 0.2 and 0.6 differ by about 0.14 h. Treating the 0.2 h single-point error as an independent standard deviation, a two-sided test at 0.05 with power 0.8 needs about 33 pathways per group. If the coefficient of variation is 0.25, the absolute standard deviation is 0.0525. The figures of about 2 and 25 are standard-error targets for 0.05 absolute and 5% relative precision. For a 95% confidence-interval half-width the normal-approximation counts are about 5 and 97. Small samples with an estimated variance need a t correction. The 33 figure assumes independent, equal-variance groups, and pathways within a cell are not independent biological replicates.

The example demonstrates a workflow: write the constraints, choose the objective, solve the steady problem and the dynamic family, check boundaries, report precision. It does not establish a specific biological strategy. Parameters are orders of magnitude, and modeled onset times are 2 to 4 times shorter than the measured onset range in Wu et al. (Figure 3l), so only trends are compared.

Part III

Practice

Errors usually sit in the modelling order and the interpretation, not in the solver.

6Error list and checklist

Check the problem definition, then the algorithm, then the interpretation.

The first error is treating accounting as mechanism. Shares summing to one is an identity and does not say which sector limits the others. The second is an undeclared objective. Without an objective there is no optimum, only a description. The third is misreading a boundary solution. Linear models often optimise at a boundary, and calling it an interior optimum leads to wrong conclusions. The fourth is unit confusion. Proteome mass fraction, dry-mass fraction and volume fraction cannot be interchanged, and neither can rates and accumulated amounts. The fifth is testing a dynamic model with steady-state data. A steady-state curve carries no timing information and cannot validate a switching policy. The growth law also has sub-optimality evidence, so not every steady-state point should be read as optimal8. The sixth is overfitting and non-identifiability. With many parameters and little data the optimum is unstable, and intervals should be reported instead of points.

Two more are easy to miss. First, the average used here assumes stationary environmental statistics. Real environments have long tails, and a long tail changes the optimal reserve. Second, constraints can change over time, for example when the fixed share rises under stress. Treating a constraint as constant gives wrong predictions when the environment moves. Cross-species comparison also suggests a shared allocation backbone, so single-species optimality should not be extrapolated6. Spatial structure introduces nutrient gradients and breaks the well-mixed assumption7.

A report should give three things: the source of each constraint, the objective function, and the verification of the solution. Without all three the reader cannot tell whether a conclusion is a property of the model or an artefact of the solver. Archiving code and parameters alongside the text is more useful than restating numbers in prose.

Return to the opening. Resource-allocation theory has three parts: constraints, objectives, costates. The algorithms fall into four layers: steady-state optimisation, dynamic optimal control, fluctuation evaluation, and estimation. State them separately and the model can be tested. Mix them and the conclusion is only self-consistent, not falsifiable.
