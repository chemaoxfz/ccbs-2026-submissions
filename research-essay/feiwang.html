When pancreatic repair fails to reset

A quantitative hypothesis for how KRAS, inflammation and tissue context cooperate in pancreatic cancer initiation

Fei Wang · 9 November 2026 · Quantitative Biological Systems
Literature synthesis and an original illustrative model · No patient data fitted

Central claim

On a KRAS-driven route toward pancreatic cancer, sufficiently strong nonlinear feedback could convert a transient injury into a persistent precursor-prone state, making recovery dynamics a useful causal question alongside mutation status.

A mutation changes what a cell can do. It does not, by itself, specify what that cell will do next. My research interest in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), tissue mechanics and bacteria makes this distinction especially consequential: an environment may initiate a transition, stabilize it, or merely help an established tumor grow. These are different claims. This essay asks when a damaged pancreatic system can return toward its previous state, and when it may fail to reset.

The quantitative contribution is deliberately small: one differential equation, its stability analysis, and a reproducible comparison of two injury pulses. The calculation yields a testable result: two inputs with the same integrated magnitude can produce opposite long-term outcomes. It is a conditional result about a model, not a measurement of cancer risk.

1Problem: a driver mutation is not a complete trajectory

PDAC is a malignant epithelial cancer of the pancreas. Pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) is a microscopic precursor lesion. Acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM) describes a change from enzyme-producing acinar identity toward a duct-like state. In the injury-associated acinar route considered here, altered cell identity and precursor formation precede invasion. These terms should not be used interchangeably.

In 46 large samples of grossly normal human pancreatic tissue, Braxton and colleagues reported a mean burden of 13 PanINs per cubic centimetre, with extensive independent origins and frequent oncogenic KRAS mutations.1 This provides a useful puzzle: detecting a driver or a precursor does not identify its future. The reported density is a tissue observation, not an annual transformation probability. Nor should a surgical tissue series be treated as a random sample of healthy people. Here this study motivates the question, rather than calibrating the model.

The genetic explanation remains essential. The Cancer Genome Atlas analysis found KRAS mutations in 140 of 150 PDAC samples and recurrent alterations in TP53, CDKN2A, SMAD4 and other genes. It also identified alternative drivers in KRAS-wild-type tumors.2 Thus, my argument concerns a common KRAS-driven route, not every pancreatic malignancy. Additional genetic changes, selection and invasion are not replaced by a feedback model. The narrower question is how genetically susceptible tissue becomes and remains permissive for neoplasia.

2Challenge: persistence can have several causes

Three experimental programmes sharpen the question. First, Daniluk and colleagues showed that inflammatory stimulation produced prolonged Ras activity and pancreatic pathology in mice expressing oncogenic K-Ras. Disrupting IKK2 or inhibiting Cox-2 weakened this interaction.3 Their perturbations support an inflammatory feedback mechanism. They do not measure a Hill coefficient or demonstrate the two stable steady states assumed below.

Second, Del Poggetto and colleagues found that a resolved inflammatory event could leave epithelial changes that increased susceptibility to subsequent KRAS-driven tumorigenesis. Histology and inflammatory NF-κB/STAT3 activation could normalize while transcriptional and epigenetic changes persisted.4 This finding prevents a tempting shortcut: persistent susceptibility need not mean continuously elevated inflammatory signaling. A tissue can look recovered while retaining a different response to the next challenge.

Third, Burdziak and colleagues integrated single-cell transcriptional and chromatin measurements with mouse perturbations. Their analysis connected early plastic states to cell-cell communication, and epithelial Il33 suppression altered the progression of those states.5 This makes a tissue-level feedback interpretation plausible. However, inferred communication or cell-state similarity alone is not lineage proof. The perturbation evidence is stronger than a receptor-ligand correlation, and neither establishes the scalar model used here.

The distinction that changes the argument

Ongoing feedback, latent epigenetic memory, slow relaxation and expansion of a selected clone can all produce an apparently persistent phenotype. A single late endpoint cannot tell them apart. The model below isolates the first mechanism so that its predictions can be compared with the others.

3Approach: balance activation against recovery

Let X denote an effective epithelial activation coordinate. It could eventually be constructed from longitudinal signaling and cell-state measurements, but no such index has been estimated here. Its meaning is restricted to the transition from a low-activation state to a sustained, precursor-prone state. It is neither a tumor-cell count nor a probability of developing cancer.

dXdt=U(t)+VX2K2+X2−δX(1)

The first term is an external or basal drive. The second is saturating self-amplification, which summarizes intracellular and tissue-level feedback. The last is linear relaxation toward lower activation. Here K is the half-saturation scale, V is a maximum feedback rate, and δ is a relaxation-rate constant. The exponent two is a modeling assumption about effective nonlinearity, not a measured stoichiometry or a claim that two molecules bind. The model assumes X≥0, nonnegative input and feedback, and positive K and δ. The quantities U and V have units of activation per unit time, while K has the same units as X. Nonnegative initial states remain nonnegative, since the derivative at zero is the nonnegative input.

Define dimensionless variables x=X/K and τ=δt, with u=U/(δK) and b=V/(δK). Equation 1 becomes:

dxdτ=f(x;u,b)=u+bx21+x2−x(2)

This rescaling prevents unjustified units. A simulated duration of five means a physical duration of 5/δ, not five hours or days. The scale 1/δ belongs to the linear loss term alone. With feedback, the local recovery time near a stable equilibrium is 1δ|fx(x*)|, so actual recovery can be substantially slower. The illustrative values are u0=0.12 and b=1.8. They were selected to reveal a possible dynamical regime. They are not extracted from an experiment, and the figures are not patient data.

4Proof within the model: when can two states coexist?

An equilibrium is a state where activation and relaxation balance. Setting Equation 2 to zero and clearing its positive denominator gives:

x3−(u+b)x2+x−u=0(3)

The local slope determines whether a small perturbation decays. If the slope is negative, a slightly higher state is pushed down and a slightly lower state is pushed up. That equilibrium is stable. A positive slope makes small deviations grow:

fx=2bx(1+x2)2−1(4)

At the chosen baseline, the three equilibria are 0.1713, 0.6212 and 1.1274. Their slopes are −0.4179, +0.1643 and −0.2131, respectively. The outer two are stable. Their local recovery times are approximately 2.3930 and 4.6925 dimensionless time units, respectively, rather than one unit. These are local small-perturbation times, not a complete recovery duration after a large pulse. The middle one is an unstable boundary: after restoring the baseline input, states below it return toward 0.1713 and states above it approach 1.1274.

The same equation predicts hysteresis: the transition on increasing input occurs at a different value from the transition on decreasing input. Solving the equilibrium and zero-slope conditions gives fold inputs of 0.09440 and 0.15356. Under sufficiently slow changes, a system on the low branch switches upward near the upper fold. A system on the high branch remains there until the input falls below the lower fold. Removing an injury back to 0.12 therefore need not undo what the injury started.

Positive feedback is not enough

The feedback-production slope reaches its maximum at x=1/3. That maximum is 9b/(83). It exceeds the unit relaxation slope only when:

b>839≈1.5396(5)

This is necessary, not sufficient, for bistability: the input must also lie in the appropriate interval. For the particular gain b=1.8, there are exactly two stable positive equilibria separated by an unstable equilibrium only when 0.0944049617<u<0.1535634744. At either endpoint, a stable and an unstable equilibrium merge, so the endpoint is not an ordinary two-stable-state regime. These numerical bounds are specific to this gain. For example, at b=1.4 the net rate is strictly decreasing and the system has one equilibrium for every positive input. Even strong feedback can fail to create a switch if its response shape changes. Replacing the squared Hill response by x/(1+x) gives:

x2+(1−u−b)x−u=0(6)

For positive input, the product of the two roots is negative. Exactly one root is positive, so the first-order Hill model cannot exhibit the same two-state switch. The nonlinearity assumption is doing real work. Observing an activating pathway diagram is not enough to justify a bistability claim.

Why does the equal-area intuition fail? Input is not stored without loss. Relaxation operates throughout the exposure, while self-amplification depends nonlinearly on the current state. The weak pulse spends too much of its duration near a low-rate bottleneck. The stronger pulse reaches a region where feedback can sustain activation after the external input disappears. The general conclusion is not that shorter insults are always worse. It is that amplitude, duration and the state at withdrawal matter separately.

The critical duration can be calculated without an arbitrary final-state cutoff. Define xL=0.1713309411 as the low equilibrium and xU=0.6212298379 as the unstable equilibrium, both evaluated at the restored baseline input. During a pulse with constant input, rearrange Equation 2 and integrate between these states:

Tcrit(A)=∫xLxUdxu0+A+bx21+x2−x(7)

This expression applies when the net rate stays positive across the integration interval. For the chosen gain and baseline, the critical amplitude is Ac=uupper fold−u0≈0.0335634744. A finite-duration switch requires a pulse strictly above this value. At or below it, no finite constant pulse switches the deterministic system from this low initial state. At equality the trajectory approaches the fold bottleneck asymptotically. These conditions depend on the initial state, rectangular input and absence of noise. Just above it, the critical duration becomes very long. At amplitude 0.10, the critical duration is 5.8211. For an amplitude above the critical value, durations strictly below the calculated critical duration return low and durations strictly above it approach the high equilibrium. At exact equality, the ideal deterministic trajectory remains at the unstable equilibrium after withdrawal. Numerical rounding or real fluctuations can push it to either side, so an approximately critical simulation must not be presented as a definite switch.

6Connecting the model to PDAC mechanics and bacteria

My mechanobiology perspective suggests a specific extension: mechanical context might change the effective gain or recovery time of a susceptible tissue. Laklai and colleagues connected PDAC genotype, epithelial tension, integrin/STAT3 signaling and matricellular fibrosis to tumor progression.6 That is a reason to test whether mechanics modifies early feedback, not evidence that a stiffer pancreas independently initiates cancer. The study has a 2023 correction concerning duplicated images, which should accompany its interpretation.7

There is no measured conversion in this essay from a kilopascal change to a change in b. A mechanical perturbation could alter the basal drive, feedback, relaxation, survival, or several together. These possibilities are not identifiable from one final value of x. The parameter mapping is a hypothesis to estimate with time-resolved perturbations.

A uniformly pro-tumor stromal term would also be misleading. Rhim and colleagues found that disrupting Hedgehog-dependent stromal support could produce more aggressive pancreatic tumors in mice.8 Some stromal functions restrain disease. The useful question is which cell population, matrix property and disease stage modify which dynamical process. The model does not justify indiscriminate stromal removal.

Bacteria introduce another potential layer. Pushalkar and colleagues reported that microbiome perturbations altered pancreatic neoplasia and immune suppression in mouse models.9 A microbial function could, in principle, contribute to inflammatory input or change immune control of abnormal cells. Those are different mechanisms, and neither is represented by a universal “bacterial abundance” parameter. Detecting a species in an established human tumor does not establish that it initiated that tumor. For low-biomass tissue, contamination controls and temporal evidence are essential to any such claim.

A focused extension for my research

Does a defined mechanical context change the pulse duration required for a KRAS-mutant epithelial system to remain altered after stimulus withdrawal, and is any change mediated by epithelial signaling, persistent chromatin remodeling, or immune interactions? A bacteria-related signal could later be added as an independently controlled input. This asks for a mechanism and an interaction, rather than assuming that mechanics and bacteria must cooperate.

7Significance: a model should specify how it can fail

The central prediction is history dependence at matched final conditions. A useful conceptual test would compare initially unprimed and previously stimulated KRAS-mutant epithelial systems after restoring the same medium and mechanical conditions. Independently vary pulse amplitude and duration, measure the actual input rather than its nominal label, and track the same cells or lineages over time. Include matched wild-type systems to test genetic context. Proliferation, death, identity and signaling should be measured separately, because changes in population composition can mimic changes within a cell.

For the mechanics extension, a factorial design with independently characterized stiffness and inflammatory input would test whether the withdrawal boundary shifts. Matched ligand presentation and transport controls are necessary. A change in endpoint marker expression is insufficient to assign the change to feedback gain. Fit full trajectories across conditions, compare alternative models on withheld pulse schedules, and treat biological preparations rather than individual cells from one preparation as independent replicates.

A minimal extension for latent memory would introduce a slow variable m that records prior injury and modulates feedback:

dmdτ=konq(τ)(1−m)−koffm,b(m)=b0+αm(8)

Here 0≤q≤1 is a scaled injury signal, and 0≤m≤1 is a bounded memory coordinate. Positive formation and loss rates preserve this interval, provided the initial memory lies in this interval. Using separate rates allows relatively rapid memory formation during an insult and slow decay afterward: at q=0, memory decays with time scale 1/koff, while at q=1 the approach rate is kon+koff. A single small rate in both formation and decay would slow both processes and would not, by itself, establish rapid memory acquisition.

This is an uncalibrated extension, not a fitted account of the Del Poggetto study, and it was not used to generate Figures 2–4. A positive α is an explicit hypothesis that memory increases effective feedback, rather than a measured sign or magnitude. Low present activation and altered future responsiveness can coexist for appropriate parameters, but this is not guaranteed. When memory changes the gain, the fixed-gain thresholds above must be recalculated. Memory may also act through other parameters, and extra parameters require independent longitudinal measurements.

The original scalar model is deterministic, spatially uniform and restricted to one effective state. It omits stochastic switching, cell heterogeneity, evolutionary competition, vascular transport and additional driver alterations. It cannot predict PDAC incidence, time to invasion, a clinical treatment threshold or whether an established tumor will regress. Other routes, including ductal and cystic precursor routes, require separate treatment. Failure to reset is therefore a candidate mechanism for persistent susceptibility, not a sufficient explanation of invasive cancer.

8What this analysis adds

A quantitative account of pancreatic cancer initiation should ask both what makes a cell susceptible and what makes an altered state persist. Genetics defines important constraints. Injury can expose plasticity. Feedback and memory provide distinct ways for prior events to influence future behavior. Tissue mechanics and microbial functions are possible modifiers whose causal roles must be tested at the relevant stage.

The original calculation adds a precise claim to that framework: under sufficiently strong nonlinear feedback, equal integrated inputs can lead to recovery or persistence because the state at withdrawal falls on different sides of an unstable boundary. The accompanying first-order Hill countermodel shows why this result is not guaranteed by the presence of feedback. The next scientific step is to measure recovery trajectories and challenge the proposed boundary, rather than label every persistent phenotype a switch.

9Methods, provenance and reproducibility

This essay follows the question-first, literature-to-argument workflow in idea-to-essay (repository revision 82fcfb0, accessed 9 November 2026). The work proceeded through a literature sweep, argument design, a second search for alternative interpretations and counterevidence, original calculations, and standalone HTML assembly. It is a curated narrative analysis, not a systematic review or an automatically generated pipeline report.

Primary evidence was sought for human precursor burden, pancreatic genetics, inflammatory feedback, epithelial memory, cell-cell communication, mechanics and microbial effects. Full-text results were inspected for Daniluk, Del Poggetto, Burdziak, TCGA and Rhim. Full local source files were obtained for Daniluk and Burdziak. The Braxton publisher page, Laklai abstract and Pushalkar abstract were used only for bounded contextual statements. Access gaps and the Laklai correction are recorded in the companion evidence ledger. No quantitative model parameter was taken from an abstract.

Equilibria were computed from the cubic, fold positions from the zero-slope condition, and pulse boundaries by quadrature. The reference Python implementation integrates the two pulse segments separately. Halving its maximum step from 0.02 to 0.01 changed the reported withdrawal state by less than 10⁻¹². Substituting the equilibria back into Equation 2 gave a maximum residual below 10⁻¹². The quadrature threshold agreed with direct integration to better than 10⁻⁷ in state units. These checks test numerical consistency, not biological validity. The optional browser demonstration uses a separate fourth-order Runge–Kutta implementation with step at most 0.01.

Show the core reproducible calculation
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad, solve_ivp

u0, b = 0.12, 1.8
f = lambda x, u: u + b*x*x/(1+x*x) - x
roots = np.sort(np.roots([1, -(u0+b), 1, -u0]))
x_low, x_unstable, x_high = roots

def withdrawal(A, T):
    sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, y: f(y, u0+A), (0, T),
                    [x_low], rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12,
                    max_step=0.02)
    return sol.y[0, -1]

for A, T in [(0.04, 15), (0.12, 5)]:
    x_end = withdrawal(A, T)
    print(A*T, x_end, "high" if x_end > x_unstable else "low")

Tcrit = quad(lambda x: 1/f(x, u0+0.1),
             x_low, x_unstable, epsabs=1e-11)[0]
print(roots, Tcrit)
