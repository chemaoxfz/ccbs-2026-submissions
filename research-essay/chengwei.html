When Faster Digestion Barely Raises the Glucose Peak

A four-state model of capacity limits and their appearance in interstitial glucose

Chengwei Jin · CCBS 2026 · Research Essay · 10 November 2026

Abstract

Converting a fixed meal into glucose faster need not produce a proportionally higher blood glucose peak. This essay connects digestion, intestinal absorption, blood glucose, and local interstitial glucose in a four-state model. Reaction accounting and elementary enzyme kinetics motivate its structure. Conservation and finite throughput then give lower bounds on absorption time and upper bounds on blood glucose. In an illustrative experiment, a fourfold increase in digestion capacity raises the blood peak by only 1.14%. Yet removing absorption saturation does not eliminate every peak plateau: slow delivery and the fast-digestion limit can produce similar insensitivity. A local exchange model explains which changes survive in the interstitial signal. The central result is a distinction between demonstrating a capacity effect within a model and identifying that mechanism from a final glucose curve.

The argument in five steps

Problem: explain why faster digestion may barely raise the peak. Challenge: several unobserved processes can produce similar curves. Approach: build a four-state model with explicit assumptions. Proof: derive bounds, compute trajectories, and test a competing mechanism. Significance: determine what a glucose curve can establish and what additional information is needed.

Three detailed calculations can be expanded below

1The question: what limits the effect of faster digestion?

The amount of carbohydrate eaten, the rate at which meal glucose reaches the circulation, and the glucose signal recorded near a subcutaneous probe are different quantities. An upstream process can accelerate while a downstream process changes little. This motivates a precise question: with total meal amount fixed, when does faster digestion mainly change intermediate storage and timing rather than blood peak height?

The question has established precedents. Dalla Man and colleagues modeled oral glucose absorption against tracer-derived plasma appearance. Their model separates stomach and intestinal stores, with nonlinear gastric emptying and first-order intestinal absorption.1 Moxon and colleagues combined gastric emptying, starch hydrolysis, and intestinal mass transfer to examine reaction and transport limitations.2 Their assumption of sufficiently rapid epithelial uptake does not validate a fixed absorption capacity here. The existence of possible bottlenecks is therefore a starting point, not a discovery claimed by this essay.

More comprehensive glucose simulators also exist. Visentin and colleagues used triple-tracer single-meal data from 51 people with type 2 diabetes to construct a model with insulin regulation.3 The present model instead isolates one chain of processes so its assumptions can be followed through to analytical consequences. It is not fitted to an individual and does not attempt to replace that physiological detail.

The measurement endpoint also matters. Schmidtke and colleagues modeled blood–subcutaneous differences in anesthetized rats after insulin injection.4 Their study motivates treating local exchange dynamically, but cannot supply a universal delay for human postprandial continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). We first analyze blood glucose and then ask what a local interstitial response preserves.

2Constructing a model whose assumptions can be checked

States, units, and controlled input

Use amounts for intestinal stores and concentration increments for blood and interstitial fluid. They cannot be added without a volume conversion. The four states are:

Table 1. States and their physical meanings
StateDefinitionUnit
P(t)Undigested carbohydrate in glucose equivalentsmmol
L(t)Glucose in the intestinal lumen, not yet absorbedmmol
g(t) = Gb(t) − Gb0Blood glucose increment from baselinemmol/L
i(t) = Gi(t) − Gi0Local interstitial increment from its own baselinemmol/L

P is measured in glucose equivalents: the glucose amount complete digestion would release. Thus transferring one unit from P to L conserves the tracked material. The baselines subtracted from blood and local interstitial glucose need not be equal. A zero intestinal initial state refers to the tracked meal, not a literally empty gastrointestinal tract.

To isolate digestion, prescribe delivery to the modeled intestinal region instead of estimating gastric emptying. Let a total amount D enter uniformly over duration T:

u(t)={D/T,0≤t<T,0,t≥T,P(0)=L(0)=g(0)=i(0)=0.(1)

Time zero is the onset of intestinal delivery, not necessarily the first bite. Changing digestion capacity below leaves D and T fixed. A later control changes T while retaining D, explicitly separating meal amount from delivery rate.

Why a finite processing capacity is plausible

Consider enzyme E, free substrate S, bound complex C, and product Z, all expressed as concentrations in this derivation:

E+S⇌k−k+C⟶kcE+Z.(2)

Binding occurs at rate k+ES, dissociation at k−C, and catalysis at kcC. Total enzyme ET = E + C is conserved. If complex adjustment is fast relative to substrate processing, its formation and loss approximately balance.5

Derivation · enzyme occupancyFrom elementary reaction rates to a saturation law

Substitute E = ET − C into the complex balance. Collect the terms containing C, set the fast derivative approximately to zero, and divide by its positive loss coefficient:

C˙=k+(ET−C)S−(k−+kc)C,0≈k+ETS−[k+S+k−+kc]C,C≈ETSKM+S,KM=k−+kck+.(3)

Here k+ has units of inverse concentration per time, while k− and kc have units of inverse time. Accordingly, KM is a concentration. It includes catalytic loss and is generally different from the binding equilibrium constant k−/k+.

This reduction assumes that the complex can follow its changing quasi-steady value. It does not mean C is constant throughout digestion. Substituting total substrate for free substrate further requires bound substrate to be negligible. Without adequate separation or with substantial binding depletion, the complex needs its own state or a different reduction.

Write KM = (k− + kc)/k+ for the effective half-saturation concentration. Product formation equals turnover of occupied enzyme:

v=Z˙≈VmaxSKM+S,Vmax=kcET.(4)

At low substrate, the rate is approximately proportional to S. At high substrate, most enzyme is occupied and the rate approaches its finite capacity. To express digestion using amount P in an effective fixed volume Vgut, set P ≈ VgutS, Kd = VgutKM, and Vd = VgutVmax. A finite number of transport units with finite turnover similarly motivates an effective absorption law.6 We adopt:

Jd(P)=VdPKd+P,Ja(L)=VaLKa+L.(5)

Vd and Va have units mmol/min, while Kd and Ka are half-saturation amounts in mmol. Both laws have bounded throughput. These are lumped constitutive assumptions, not a claim that whole-intestine digestion and transport are literally single binding reactions. There is no hard storage ceiling or transit loss in the model. Its finite meal amount and its finite processing rates are distinct constraints.

Accounting for material and closing the system

Input adds one unit to P, digestion transfers one unit from P to L, and absorption removes one unit from L. Place these changes in the columns of a stoichiometric matrix, then multiply by the corresponding fluxes:7

ddt(PL)=Nv,N=(1−1001−1),v=(uJdJa).(6)

Let A(t) be cumulative absorption, with Ȧ = Ja and A(0) = 0. Adding the two store equations and Ȧ cancels both internal transfers, leaving Ṗ + L̇ + Ȧ = u. Integration gives:

P(t)+L(t)+A(t)=Uin(t),Uin(t)=∫0tu(s)ds.(7)

Hence A(t) ≤ Uin(t) ≤ D for nonnegative stores. The intestine's output is an amount rate. Multiplying by a fixed systemic availability fraction f and dividing by an effective distribution volume Vb converts it to a blood concentration rate. Uptake represented by f must not be deducted again elsewhere.

Represent net incremental blood regulation by fixed linear clearance kbg. This is an extra approximation, not a result of baseline subtraction alone. It can represent a locally restoring net production-minus-utilization relation, but omits explicit insulin and time-varying endogenous production. Let the local interstitial signal relax toward g with time constant τ. The complete system is:

P˙=u(t)−Jd(P),L˙=Jd(P)−Ja(L),g˙=fJa(L)/Vb−kbg,i˙=(g−i)/τ.(8)

All parameters are positive, with 0 < f ≤ 1. At each state's zero boundary its derivative is nonnegative, so the nonnegative domain is preserved. The local observation region is too small to appreciably affect systemic glucose and is therefore driven one-way by g. It is not another whole-body glucose store. Section 6 derives the transport interpretation and qualifies the assumed unit gain.

3What finite capacity actually constrains

More substrate can accumulate without much extra throughput

Differentiation makes the diminishing local response explicit. Define ha as the relative change in absorption flux per relative change in its substrate amount:8

Ja′(L)=VaKa(Ka+L)2,ha(L)=LJadJadL=KaKa+L(L>0).(9)

At L = Ka, this local reaction order is 0.5. At L = 9Ka, it is 0.1: a small 1% rise in L produces only about a 0.1% flux increase. Faster digestion can therefore produce a larger luminal store with little extra absorption. This is a statement about an instantaneous flux. It is not yet the sensitivity of a whole blood glucose peak.

Storage can also change recovery times. As an auxiliary calculation, supply a constant rate u0 < min(Vd, Va). At equilibrium Ja(L*) = u0. Solving this equation and differentiating absorption gives:

L*=Kau0Va−u0,aa=Ja′(L*)=(Va−u0)2KaVa,ta,local=1aa.(10)

For a small local perturbation with upstream flux held at u0, δL̇ ≈ −aaδL, so the relaxation time is 1/aa. It grows as input approaches absorption capacity. This connects a weak incremental outflow to slow drainage. It describes a local constant-input equilibrium, not an assumption that the finite meal is at steady state.

A lower bound on processing time

Let tα be the first time A reaches αD, where 0 < α < 1. Absorbed material must already have entered and been digested. From conservation, A ≤ Uin ≤ Dt/T. Since the cumulative digested amount is L + A, we also have A ≤ ∫Jdds ≤ Vdt. Finally, absorption itself satisfies:

αD=A(tα)=∫0tαJa(s)ds≤∫0tαVads=Vatα.(11)

Combining these independent necessary conditions yields:

tα≥max{αT,αDVd,αDVa}.(12)

The maximum is required because stages run concurrently. Adding the three times would incorrectly assume that the entire meal completes one stage before starting the next. The bound need not be attained: rates start below capacity and decline as substrate is depleted.

At positive parameters, tα exists for every α < 1. To check this, use P ≤ D after input ends: Ṗ ≤ −[Vd/(Kd + D)]P, so P and Jd tend to zero. For any ε > 0, eventually Jd < Ja(ε)/2. Whenever L ≥ ε thereafter, L̇ < −Ja(ε)/2. Thus L eventually falls below ε and cannot cross upward through it. Hence L → 0, and conservation gives A → D. The completion statement follows from the model, rather than being assumed in the time bound.

Two upper bounds on blood glucose

A blood peak depends on the history of absorption and clearance, not just the largest instantaneous flux. The linear blood equation lets us account for that history explicitly.

Proof · blood peak boundsIntegrating the blood equation and bounding its history

Multiply ġ + kbg = fJa/Vb by exp(kbt). The left side becomes a product derivative:

ddt(ekbtg(t))=fVbekbtJa(t).(13)

Integrate from zero and use g(0) = 0:

g(t)=fVb∫0te−kb(t−s)Ja(s)ds.(14)

The exponential weight is nonnegative and at most one. Replacing Ja by Va gives one bound. Replacing the exponential weight by one and using A ≤ D gives another:

g(t)≤fVaVbkb(1−e−kbt)≤fVaVbkb,g(t)≤fVb∫0tJa(s)ds=fA(t)Vb≤fDVb.(15)

Both inequalities hold simultaneously, so:

gmax≤min{fDVb,fVaVbkb}.(16)

The finite-meal limit depends on how much glucose can enter the effective distribution volume. The capacity limit also depends on clearance. Neither increases with Vd. Nevertheless, independence of an upper bound from digestion capacity does not prove that the actual peak is insensitive or that the bound is reached. A trajectory can move substantially beneath a fixed ceiling. We must now calculate the response, then test whether absorption saturation is responsible for it.

Increasing Vd from 2 to 8 is a fourfold increase, but the blood peak rises only from 2.156 to 2.181 mmol/L, a 1.14% increase. The peak occurs about 11.0 min earlier, and 90% absorption is about 9.1 min earlier. Thus small peak changes do not mean the upstream intervention has no effect. The intermediate variables explain the result. In the reference and fast cases, the largest L/Ka ratios are about 8.90 and 16.08, respectively. Maximum absorption rises only from about 0.899 to 0.941 mmol/min despite the much faster digestion. Both cases use a large fraction of the fixed 1 mmol/min absorption capacity. These internal measurements are model calculations, not hidden physiological quantities inferred from the final peak.

The bounds are consistent with the computed times. For 90% absorption, the absorption-only lower bound is 108 min. At Vd = 0.25, digestion supplies the stronger bound of 432 min, below the computed 552.9 min. At Vd = 2 and 8, the joint bound is 108 min, below the computed 135.2 and 126.1 min. The peak upper bound is 2.667 mmol/L, which neither fast case reaches.

The reference interstitial peak retains about 99.27% of the blood peak. This simulation therefore shows only weak peak attenuation by the tissue response. Reporting that result is necessary: a delayed signal does not automatically imply a much smaller peak. Its explanation follows in Section 6, after we test the proposed upstream bottleneck.

5Does a plateau identify saturating absorption?

The reference calculation demonstrates a capacity effect under the stated equations. To determine whether the final pattern is specific to that mechanism, remove absorption saturation while matching the original law's slope at low L:

Jalin(L)=kaL,ka=Va/Ka.(17)

For this comparison, Va/Ka sets the linear slope. Va is no longer an upper bound on absorption, so the capacity-based time and peak bounds cannot be carried over unchanged. The input and digestion bounds and the finite-meal peak bound still apply. Keeping those distinctions explicit prevents the control model from being judged by an assumption it no longer has.

We perform two controls: compare the saturating and slope-matched linear laws at T = 20 min, then spread the same D over T = 240 min. A continuous sweep of Vd checks whether any observed plateau is confined to the two chosen capacities.

With 20 min delivery, the 2-to-8 increase raises the peak by 60.27% under linear absorption, compared with 1.14% under saturating absorption. Changing the high-substrate absorption behavior therefore strongly changes the effect of digestion acceleration in this controlled setting. The luminal accumulation and near-capacity flux in Section 4 support that interpretation.

However, the linear model also approaches a plateau at sufficiently large Vd. In the fast-digestion limit, little delay remains between prescribed delivery and production of absorbable glucose. Further acceleration then scarcely changes the downstream input history, even though absorption has no fixed capacity. A plateau is therefore not unique to absorption saturation.

This alternative is a mechanism test, not a claim that linear absorption is the correct physiology. Matching the low-substrate slope removes one easy source of difference, but the two constitutive laws still make deliberately different predictions at high substrate. The comparison establishes what that structural choice does within otherwise identical models. It cannot determine which law describes an unmeasured person.

The long-delivery control supplies another explanation. At T = 240 min, changing Vd from 2 to 8 raises the peak by less than 0.1% under either absorption law. Spreading the meal over a longer interval makes prescribed delivery itself a slow process. More rapid digestion cannot accelerate material that has not yet entered. This is why input duration must be controlled when interpreting a digestion intervention.

What the comparison establishes

Saturating absorption strongly reduces the response to digestion acceleration in the reference model. A peak plateau alone cannot establish that mechanism. The conclusion requires the controlled alternative and the internal flux history, not just the shape of the final curve.

6How the response appears in local interstitial glucose

To connect blood glucose to the physiological signal near a probe, consider an exchange region with volume Vi, area As, effective distance ℓ, and effective diffusivity Deff. Fick's estimate gives a transfer rate DeffAs(Gb − Gi)/ℓ. Dividing by volume yields:9

G˙i≈β(Gb−Gi),β=DeffAsViℓ,τ=1β=ViℓDeffAs.(18)

Because diffusivity has dimensions length²/time, β has dimensions inverse time. If Vi/As is of order ℓ, then τ is of order ℓ²/Deff. This supplies a physical scale for a response time. It is not a calibrated tissue model: perfusion, geometry, local utilization, and probe effects may alter both speed and gain. Neglecting local utilization and assuming fixed exchange gives the unit-gain increment equation used here.

Solving τi̇ + i = g with i(0) = 0 gives:

i(t)=∫0t1τe−(t−s)/τg(s)ds(i(0)=0).(19)

The tissue signal is a weighted history of blood glucose. The kernel is nonnegative and its integral over the available history is 1 − exp(−t/τ) ≤ 1. Therefore i(t) cannot exceed the largest earlier value of g. This proves peak attenuation for this particular observer, but not its magnitude.

For a blood increment that steps to a constant height G, direct integration gives:

i(t)=G(1−e−t/τ),i(τ)/G=1−e−1≈0.632.(20)

The response starts immediately and reaches about 63.2% of the step at t = τ. A pure time delay would remain unchanged before its delayed onset. The time constant is consequently a relaxation time, not a waiting period.

Derivation · a finite glucose pulseMatching the response before and after the input ends

Let g = G for 0 ≤ t ≤ w and zero afterward. Before w, use the step response. After w, solve i̇ = −i/τ with initial value i(w) = G[1 − exp(−w/τ)]. Continuity gives:

i(t)={G(1−e−t/τ),0≤t≤w,G(1−e−w/τ)e−(t−w)/τ,t>w.(21)

The signal rises until w and decays afterward, so its peak is exactly G[1 − exp(−w/τ)]. This peak depends on pulse duration as well as the tissue time constant.

At τ = 10 min, a 5 min pulse retains 39.35% of its height, whereas a 60 min pulse retains 99.75%. The broad meal response can likewise show little peak attenuation, explaining the 99.27% retention in Section 4. At an interior interstitial maximum, i̇ = 0 implies i = g. At a blood maximum, appearance balances clearance. These different conditions explain why their peak-time separation depends on the input history and need not equal τ.

7What has been learned, and what would test it?

The argument now separates three claims. Conservation and assumed finite throughput prove bounds without fitting. Numerical trajectories show that faster digestion can mainly increase storage and advance timing while barely increasing the peak. Controlled alternatives establish that a plateau is not unique to saturating absorption. The local observer then explains how the temporal width of blood glucose changes determines the visible attenuation.

The useful contribution is this connection between constraints, calculated mechanisms, and competing explanations. It does not establish a new universal physiological law or a validated predictor. In particular, neither the simulated 1.14% change nor the 10 min tissue constant should be treated as a human population estimate.

Each extension should address a specific assumption. Changing food structure may change gastric delivery as well as digestion, so the prescribed u(t) needs independent information. Whole-intestine uptake can involve spatial and state dependence: Nakamura and colleagues measured such differences in isolated mouse intestinal preparations, which motivates questioning a fixed lumped capacity without establishing its human value.10 Insulin feedback and endogenous production may invalidate fixed linear clearance. Local utilization can reduce observation gain, so a smaller interstitial peak need not be caused by slow exchange alone.

Useful evidence follows from those distinctions. Tracer-derived glucose appearance and delivery information can constrain the upstream input. Paired blood and local signals, with a specified probe site and device, can constrain the observation step. CGM alone combines these processes and need not identify them separately. The relevant question is which measurement distinguishes the proposed capacity mechanism from the alternatives demonstrated here.

A concrete challenge to the fixed-capacity hypothesis would be reproducible net absorption above the assumed ceiling under its defined conditions. A challenge to the reference interpretation would be evidence that intestinal delivery was already slow enough to explain the small peak change without saturation. Such observations would revise a constitutive assumption or its attribution. They would not contradict an inequality proved under a different, explicitly stated set of assumptions.

For the course-level problem, elementary reactions, a stoichiometric matrix, inequalities, local derivatives, and diffusion-based response theory are sufficient to reach a substantive conclusion: accelerating one stage has an effect determined by the entire chain, and the final curve cannot automatically name the stage that limited it.

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Params:
    D: float = 120.0       # mmol glucose equivalents; illustrative
    Tin: float = 20.0      # min; controlled entry to small intestine
    Vd: float = 2.0        # mmol/min
    Kd: float = 12.0       # mmol; effective digestion half-capacity amount
    Va: float = 1.0        # mmol/min
    Ka: float = 6.0        # mmol; effective absorption half-capacity amount
    f: float = 0.8         # systemic availability fraction, not a fitted human value
    Vb: float = 12.0       # L; effective distribution volume, not anatomical blood volume

BASE = Params()

def simulate(p=BASE, method='DOP853', rtol=2e-9, atol=2e-11):
    def rhs(u):
        def fun(t, x):
            P, L, g, i = x[:4]
            jd = p.Vd*P/(p.Kd+P)
            ja = p.Va*L/(p.Ka+L)
            return [u-jd, jd-ja, p.f*ja/p.Vb-p.kb*g, (g-i)/p.tau,
                    ja, g, i]
        return fun
    a = solve_ivp(rhs(p.D/p.Tin), [0, p.Tin], np.zeros(7),
                  method=method, rtol=rtol, atol=atol, dense_output=True)
    b = solve_ivp(rhs(0), [p.Tin, p.end], a.y[:, -1],
                  method=method, rtol=rtol, atol=atol, dense_output=True)
    assert a.success and b.success, (a.message, b.message)
    def at(t):
        scalar = np.ndim(t) == 0
        ts = np.atleast_1d(t).astype(float)
        out = np.empty((7, len(ts)))
        mask = ts <= p.Tin
        if mask.any(): out[:, mask] = a.sol(ts[mask])
        if (~mask).any(): out[:, ~mask] = b.sol(ts[~mask])
        return out[:, 0] if scalar else out
    return at

def peak(at, k, end):
    ts = np.linspace(0, end, 4801)
    j = int(np.argmax(at(ts)[k]))
    res = minimize_scalar(lambda t: -at(t)[k],
        bounds=(ts[max(j-1,0)],ts[min(j+1,len(ts)-1)]), method='bounded',
        options={'xatol': 1e-8})
    return float(res.x), float(-res.fun)

def metrics(at, p):
    tb, gb = peak(at, 2, p.end)
    ti, gi = peak(at, 3, p.end)
    t90 = brentq(lambda t: at(t)[4]-.9*p.D, 0, p.end)
    recovery = brentq(lambda t: at(t)[3]-.05*gi, ti, p.end)
    final = at(p.end)
    return {'Vd':p.Vd,'Va':p.Va,'tau':p.tau,'blood_peak':gb,
        'isf_peak':gi,'blood_peak_time':tb,'isf_peak_time':ti,
        'peak_gap':ti-tb,'peak_retention':gi/gb,'t90_absorbed':t90,
        't90_lower_bound':.9*p.D/p.Va,'isf_recovery_5pct':recovery,
        'blood_auc_180':float(at(180)[5]),'isf_auc_180':float(at(180)[6]),
        'blood_auc_complete':float(final[5]),'isf_auc_complete':float(final[6]),
        'auc_theory':p.f*p.D/(p.Vb*p.kb),
        'blood_peak_upper_bound':p.f*p.Va/(p.Vb*p.kb),
        'final_gut_amount':float(final[0]+final[1])}

def alternative(p, absorption='saturating', clearance='linear'):
    def rhs(u):
        def fun(t,x):
            P,L,g,i=x[:4]
            jd=p.Vd*P/(p.Kd+P)
            ja=p.Va*L/(p.Ka+L) if absorption=='saturating' else p.Va/p.Ka*L
            out=p.kb*g if clearance=='linear' else p.kb*g*(1+g/2.)
            return [u-jd,jd-ja,p.f*ja/p.Vb-out,(g-i)/p.tau,ja,g,i,g*g]
        return fun
    a=solve_ivp(rhs(p.D/p.Tin),[0,p.Tin],np.zeros(8),method='Radau',rtol=2e-9,atol=2e-11,dense_output=True)
    b=solve_ivp(rhs(0),[p.Tin,p.end],a.y[:,-1],method='Radau',rtol=2e-9,atol=2e-11,dense_output=True)
    assert a.success and b.success
    def at(t):
        scalar=np.ndim(t)==0;tt=np.atleast_1d(t);out=np.empty((8,len(tt)));mask=tt<=p.Tin
        if mask.any():out[:,mask]=a.sol(tt[mask])
        if (~mask).any():out[:,~mask]=b.sol(tt[~mask])
        return out[:,0] if scalar else out
    return at
