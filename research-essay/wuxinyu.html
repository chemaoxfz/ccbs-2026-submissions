Which rate constant predicts in vivo covalent capture?

Xinyu Wu · CCBS 2026 research essay · 2026-11-04

One sentence: covalent small-molecule pharmacology settled long ago that target capture is governed by the efficiency constant kinact/KI acting over an exposure, not by a rate constant measured under saturation — but the covalent-protein literature still reports a saturated kobs and compares it against a clearance half-life, and because covalent proteins bind their targets roughly four orders of magnitude more tightly than covalent small molecules do, they are the one case where neither limiting form applies cleanly.

Problem

A covalent protein drug has to form its bond before the body removes it. Fan et al. engineered IB101, a covalent anti–PD-L1 nanobody that cross-links PD-L1 with an observed rate constant kobs = 0.18 min−1, a half-life of 3.8 min [1]. They set this against the plasma half-life of 8.5 min measured in mice after intravenous dosing, and conclude that the chemistry outruns clearance, so rapid clearance of a miniprotein no longer prevents target engagement. The same framing runs through the field: the original proximity-enabled reactive therapeutics (PERx) work states that covalent binding gives a duration of action "outlasting the drug pharmacokinetics" [2].

The two half-lives are measured three orders of magnitude apart in concentration. The 3.8 min comes from an assay with IB101 at 10 µM against PD-L1 at 1 µM. In the animal, the same paper reports that plasma stayed above 5 nM for at least 4 hours. So: can a rate constant measured at 10 µM tell us what happens at 5 nM?

Challenge

For covalent small molecules this question has an established answer, and it is worth stating first because it sets the standard the protein literature should be held to. Covalent capture is two steps — reversible binding, then an irreversible reaction inside the complex [3, 4]:

D + T  ⇌KI  D·T  →kinact  D–T

When target is present in trace amounts, the fraction not yet captured, u, obeys du/dt = −k(t)·u with

k(t) = kinact · [D] / (KI + [D])

Farhan et al. build exactly this scheme into a PK/PD framework for KRAS G12C and EGFR inhibitors, and state the problem in the same terms: because recovery depends on target turnover rather than on drug clearance, "plasma exposure coverage of in vitro potency cannot be used for compound selection and human dose projections" [4]. Their practice is explicit — for their compounds they set kinact/KI = kobs/[I], justified because KI ≈ 100 µM while unbound plasma concentrations are far lower [4]. They are deliberately working in the linear limit.

Mons et al. state the convention directly: kinact/KI "is generally accepted as a more suitable measure of two-step irreversible inhibitor potency", analogous to kcat/KM for substrate turnover [3]. They also give the reported ranges by class — 105–107 M−1s−1 for kinase inhibitors, 101–105 for protease inhibitors, 102–104 for other target classes, and 10−2–102 for covalent fragments — which will let us place covalent proteins against established covalent drugs rather than only against each other.

The rate has two limits, and they are not the same kind of quantity:

RegimeConditionRateUnits
Saturated[D] ≫ KIk → kinactmin−1
Linear[D] ≪ KIk → (kinact/KI)·[D]M−1s−1 × concentration

Three obstacles remain, and they are specific to proteins rather than inherited from the small-molecule case.

First, the covalent-protein literature reports both kinds of constant without reconciling them. PERx gives second-order constants measured at equimolar concentrations — 886 ± 53 M−1min−1 for PD-1(FSY) against PD-L1, and 4188 ± 176 M−1min−1 for dZHER2(37FSY) against HER2 [2]. Fan et al. give pseudo-first-order kobs values in min−1, measured with drug in tenfold excess, and compare them with each other and with a clearance half-life [1]. Converting between the two needs KI.

Second, KI and kinact are listed as not determined for IB101 in table S4 of [1], because binding was too fast and dissociation not measurable. Only the product kinact/KI = 6.0 × 105 M−1s−1 is reported. The pseudo-first-order treatment itself requires the inhibitor in excess, conventionally [I]0 > 10[E]0 [3]; the 10 µM against 1 µM used in [1] sits exactly on that boundary.

Third — and this is why the small-molecule shortcut cannot simply be borrowed — a covalent protein inherits its reversible affinity from an antibody scaffold, so KI sits in the nanomolar range rather than near 100 µM. Therapeutic plasma concentrations are nanomolar too. The drug therefore operates at KI, where neither limiting form is valid.

Approach

Two steps, both using only numbers already published in [1], [2] and [4].

Recover KI for IB101 from the two quantities the paper does report. At 10 µM the drug is far above any plausible KI, so the measured kobs is the saturated rate, kinact = 0.18 min−1 = 3.0 × 10−3 s−1. Dividing by the reported efficiency constant,

KI = kinact / (kinact/KI) = 3.0 × 10−3 / 6.0 × 105 = 5.0 × 10−9 M = 5.0 nM

The uncertainty comes from IB201, the covalent IL-18 in the same paper and the only molecule there with both parameters resolved. Its table S3 value kobs = 0.54 min−1 and its table S4 value kinact = 4.0 × 10−3 s−1 = 0.24 min−1 differ by a factor of 2.25, although at 10 µM against KI = 11 nM the reaction is 99.89% saturated and the two should coincide. One is a direct SDS-PAGE measurement and the other an extrapolation from a two-step fit to biolayer interferometry data, so this is a method-to-method difference. Carrying it as a band gives KI = 2.2–11.3 nM for IB101.

Compute the cumulative capture. The captured fraction depends only on the dimensionless integral

I = ∫ k(t) dt, θ(∞) = 1 − e−I

Reaching 90% capture needs I = ln 10 = 2.30. To avoid assuming a pharmacokinetic model, I use only the sentence in [1] that plasma stayed above 5 nM for at least 4 hours, treating that as a rectangle of constant 5 nM for 4 h, and evaluating the full expression rather than either limit. This is a lower bound on exposure, so it is a lower bound on capture.

Proof

The recovered KI lands on top of the operating concentration. KI ≈ 5 nM is also the plasma floor quoted in [1], and also the PD-L1 concentration used in the yeast-display selection (5 nM, 37 °C, 10 min). IB101 therefore works, both in the animal and during selection, at the crossover between the linear and saturated regimes — while kobs was measured 2000-fold above it (Figure 1a).

The race between the two half-lives is close to even. At [D] = 5 nM with KI = 5 nM the complex is half formed, the effective cross-linking rate is 0.090 min−1, and its half-life is 7.7 min against a plasma half-life of 8.5 min. Taking KI = 11 nM gives 5.6 min. The 2.2-fold margin implied by comparing 3.8 min with 8.5 min shrinks to roughly 1.1–1.5-fold at the concentration that matters. By the time plasma falls to 1 nM the cross-linking half-life is 21–23 min and the chemistry is slower than clearance.

The cumulative capture, by contrast, has room to spare. Using the plasma floor alone:

KICapture integral IFraction capturedMargin over the 90% threshold
2.2 nM (lower)13.3>99.99%5.8×
5.0 nM (inferred)21.6>99.99%9.4×

The conclusion is insensitive to KI: the bound clears 90% capture for any KI above 0.27 nM (Figure 1b). Below that, kinact = (kinact/KI)·KI becomes too small for the reaction to finish; but 0.27 nM is already below the parent nanobody KN035's dissociation constant of 0.3 nM [1, table S4], so it is not a plausible value here. The observed outcome in [1] — complete cross-linking of tumour PD-L1 after a single dose at both 2 and 0.5 mg/kg — is reproduced. What reproduces it is the exposure integral, not a rate that outruns clearance.

With KI in hand, the two literatures can be put on one axis. The efficiency constant is the only quantity that compares across them, and it separates the two design strategies cleanly:

Sotorasib's chemical step is 283-fold faster than IB101's, its reversible binding 17 200-fold weaker, and the product 61-fold smaller. The two modalities arrive at comparable efficiency constants from opposite directions (Figure 2). Against the ranges in [3], both covalent proteins from [1] fall in the kinase-inhibitor band at 105–107 M−1s−1, while the two PERx proteins fall among covalent fragments at 10−2–102 — a span of four orders of magnitude within one modality over five years.

The difference in KI is what puts the two modalities in different regimes. With a biochemical KI ≈ 86 µM and unbound plasma around 1 µM, sotorasib runs at about 1% saturation, deep in the linear limit where kinact/KI × exposure is exact and the substitution in [4] is safe. IB101 at 5 nM runs at 50% saturation, where it is not.

Significance

The in vivo figure of merit is kinact/KI, not kobs. This is established practice for covalent small molecules [3, 4]; the contribution here is that the covalent-protein literature has not adopted it, and that adopting it changes the reading of the lead result. Doubling kinact while KI doubles with it leaves the product unchanged and captures no more target. Only a larger kinact/KI, or a larger exposure, improves engagement.

This explains why the selection worked. Yeast display was run with PD-L1 at 5 nM for 10 min — at KI, not above it. At that concentration the quantity under selection pressure is the product kinact/KI, which is also the quantity that governs capture in the animal. Had selection been run under saturation, pressure would have fallen on kinact alone, and a variant that sped up the chemical step by loosening the reversible complex would have scored well while performing no better in vivo. Selection concentration should be set near the intended operating concentration; this is a design rule the analysis delivers directly, and it is testable by running one round of selection at 10 µM and comparing the efficiency constants of what comes out.

Half-life extension still buys something. Since I ∝ exposure and exposure is inversely proportional to clearance, the benefit of extending a covalent drug's half-life is linear and calculable rather than unnecessary. The ninefold margin also runs the other way: it says how far the dose can be cut before capture becomes incomplete, and that a reduction beyond roughly tenfold would exhaust it.

What I would argue against

  • Target resynthesis is not in this model, and it sets the duration of effect. Farhan et al. model target turnover explicitly — a 22 h degradation half-life for KRAS G12C — and it is what determines dosing interval once capture is complete [4]. Everything above concerns how much target is captured per dose, not how long the blockade lasts. A full account of IB101's every-other-day schedule needs PD-L1 turnover, which neither [1] nor this essay supplies.
  • Plasma exposure is not exposure at the target. I have used plasma concentration as a stand-in for free drug at the tumour. Farhan et al. handle this with an unbound tumour-to-plasma ratio, Kp,uu, estimated at 1.08 for sotorasib, meaning no significant tumour retention [4]. No such measurement exists for IB101. The ninefold margin tolerates about one order of magnitude of loss, and no more.
  • The trace-target assumption may fail. The derivation assumes target concentration well below drug concentration. Farhan et al. measured KRAS G12C in their xenograft at 0.002 µM = 2 nM [4]; if tumour PD-L1 is of that order, it is comparable to the 5 nM plasma floor, both species deplete, and the integral is smaller than computed.
  • The regime comparison uses biochemical constants on both sides. Canon et al. report, for the same molecule and in the same panel, cell-based apparent constants whose half-maximal concentration is 77 nM in NCI-H358 and 440 nM in MIA PaCa-2 — 195- to 1100-fold below the biochemical KI of 86 µM [5]. Those numbers fold in permeability, protein binding and competing nucleophiles, and taking them instead would move sotorasib towards saturation as well. The robust part of the comparison is the four-order-of-magnitude gap in biochemical KI between the modalities, not the exact saturation percentage.
  • The band on KI comes from one molecule. Using IB201's method-to-method ratio as the uncertainty on IB101 is an extrapolation, not a fitted error.
  • A constant 5 nM rectangle is a floor, not the exposure. Real concentrations are far higher early on, so the true integral is larger — but the early phase is saturated, where the rate is capped at kinact, so that excess cannot be counted at the linear rate.
  • Cell-surface capture is faster than this model. Reference [1] reports above 70% cross-linking of surface PD-L1 at 1 nM within 15 min; the model gives about 36% under the same conditions with KI = 5 nM. Avidity, two-dimensional encounter at the membrane, or local concentration could account for it. The discrepancy runs in the conservative direction and does not threaten the margin, but surface reactions need their own treatment rather than the solution model used here.

Code

covalent_capture.py — regime analysis, KI recovery, capture integrals
"""Which rate constant predicts in vivo covalent target capture?

Two-step irreversible capture (standard covalent-inhibitor scheme, ref 3):
      D + T  <--KI-->  D.T  --kinact-->  D-T (covalent)
With target in trace amounts relative to drug, the fraction of target still
free, u = 1 - theta, obeys
      du/dt = -k(t) u,   k(t) = kinact * D(t) / (KI + D(t))
so the captured fraction only depends on the dimensionless capture integral
      I = int k(t) dt,   theta(inf) = 1 - exp(-I).
Limits:
      D >> KI :  k -> kinact                      (saturated; units 1/time)
      D << KI :  k -> (kinact/KI) * D             (linear; units 1/conc/time)
                 I -> (kinact/KI) * AUC
All parameters below are measured values taken from the cited papers.
"""
import json
import numpy as np

MIN = 1 / 60.0  # min^-1 -> s^-1

# ---- measured parameters -------------------------------------------------
# Fan et al., Science 392, eadv3081 (2026): tables S3, S4, fig. S12
IB101 = dict(name="IB101 (anti-PD-L1 nanobody)", kobs_min=0.18, t_half_cross=3.8,
             eff=6.0e5, kinact=None, KI=None,          # kinact, KI: N.D. in table S4
             auc_ug_ml_min=99.47, t_half_pk=8.50, MW=15144.0)
IB201 = dict(name="IB201 (covalent IL-18)", kobs_min=0.54, t_half_cross=1.3,
             eff=3.6e5, kinact=4.0e-3, KI=1.1e-8)
GLUEBODY = dict(name="Gluebody (anti-PD-L1)", kobs_min=0.0018, t_half_cross=379.7)
# Li et al., Cell 182, 85 (2020), PERx: second-order constants, equimolar conditions
PERX_PD1 = dict(name="PD-1(FSY) vs PD-L1 His69", k2_M_min=886.0, err=53.0)
PERX_HER2 = dict(name="dZHER2(37FSY) vs HER2", k2_M_min=4188.0, err=176.0)

out = {}

# ---- 1. is the reported kobs in the saturated regime? --------------------
# IB201 is the only molecule with kinact and KI both resolved.
D_assay = 10e-6
sat = D_assay / (IB201["KI"] + D_assay)
out["IB201_regime"] = dict(
    D_assay_M=D_assay, KI_M=IB201["KI"], saturation_fraction=sat,
    kinact_min=IB201["kinact"] / MIN, kobs_reported_min=IB201["kobs_min"],
    ratio=IB201["kobs_min"] / (IB201["kinact"] / MIN),
    eff_check=IB201["kinact"] / IB201["KI"])

# ---- 2. put the field on one axis ----------------------------------------
# A second-order constant and a saturated kobs differ by a factor KI.
rows = []
for d in (PERX_PD1, PERX_HER2):
    rows.append(dict(study="PERx, Cell 2020", molecule=d["name"], reported="k2",
                     k2_M_s=d["k2_M_min"] * MIN, kobs_min=None))
rows.append(dict(study="Science 2026", molecule=IB201["name"], reported="kobs + kinact/KI",
                 k2_M_s=IB201["eff"], kobs_min=IB201["kobs_min"]))
rows.append(dict(study="Science 2026", molecule=IB101["name"], reported="kobs + kinact/KI",
                 k2_M_s=IB101["eff"], kobs_min=IB101["kobs_min"]))
rows.append(dict(study="Science 2026 (table S3)", molecule=GLUEBODY["name"],
                 reported="kobs only", k2_M_s=None, kobs_min=GLUEBODY["kobs_min"]))
out["field"] = rows
out["IB101_over_PERx_PD1"] = IB101["eff"] / (PERX_PD1["k2_M_min"] * MIN)

# ---- 3. in vivo capture integral for IB101 from its own PK ---------------
AUC_M_s = IB101["auc_ug_ml_min"] * 1e-3 / IB101["MW"] * 60    # ug/mL.min -> M.s
I_linear = IB101["eff"] * AUC_M_s
need = {f: np.log(1 / (1 - f)) for f in (0.5, 0.9, 0.99)}
out["invivo"] = dict(AUC_M_s=AUC_M_s, capture_integral=I_linear,
                     AUC_needed_90_M_s=need[0.9] / IB101["eff"],
                     margin_90=I_linear / need[0.9],
                     captured_fraction=float(1 - np.exp(-I_linear)))

# ---- 4. exact integral with a decaying plasma level, scanned over KI ------
# D(t) = D0 exp(-kcl t); D0 from AUC and clearance rate so AUC is preserved.
kcl = np.log(2) / IB101["t_half_pk"] / 60          # s^-1
D0 = AUC_M_s * kcl                                  # M
t = np.linspace(0, 6 * 3600, 400001)                # 6 h
D = D0 * np.exp(-kcl * t)
scan = []
for KI in (1e-9, 5e-9, 1.1e-8, 5e-8, 2e-7, 1e-6):
    kinact = IB101["eff"] * KI                      # consistent with measured ratio
    I = np.trapezoid(kinact * D / (KI + D), t)
    scan.append(dict(KI_M=KI, kinact_min=kinact / MIN, D0_over_KI=D0 / KI,
                     capture_integral=float(I), captured=float(1 - np.exp(-I))))
out["KI_scan"] = scan
out["D0_M"] = D0

# ---- 5. the comparison the paper makes, evaluated in each regime ---------
out["half_life_comparison"] = dict(
    t_half_crosslink_at_10uM_min=IB101["t_half_cross"], t_half_plasma_min=IB101["t_half_pk"],
    note="3.8 min is measured at 10 uM, i.e. ~1000x above any plausible KI")
inst = []
for Dv in (5e-9, 1e-9):
    for KI in (5e-9, 1.1e-8):
        k = IB101["eff"] * KI * Dv / (KI + Dv)      # s^-1
        inst.append(dict(D_M=Dv, KI_M=KI, k_min=k / MIN, t_half_min=np.log(2) / (k / MIN)))
out["instantaneous"] = inst

json.dump(out, open("results.json", "w"), indent=2, default=float)

p = lambda *a: print(*a)
r = out["IB201_regime"]
p(f"IB201: at 10 uM the reaction is {100*r['saturation_fraction']:.2f}% saturated,")
p(f"  so kobs should equal kinact = {r['kinact_min']:.3f}/min; reported kobs = {r['kobs_reported_min']}/min"
  f"  (factor {r['ratio']:.2f})")
p(f"  kinact/KI check = {r['eff_check']:.2e} M-1s-1 (table S4 reports 3.6e5)")
p(f"\nIB101 kinact/KI is {out['IB101_over_PERx_PD1']:.0f}x the PERx PD-1(FSY) second-order constant"
  f" (same target, same His69)")
v = out["invivo"]
p(f"\nIB101 i.v. plasma AUC0-inf = {v['AUC_M_s']:.3e} M.s")
p(f"  capture integral (linear regime) = {v['capture_integral']:.1f}")
p(f"  needed for 90% capture = {need[0.9]:.3f}  -> margin {v['margin_90']:.0f}x")
p(f"\nExact integral with D0 = {out['D0_M']*1e9:.1f} nM decaying at t1/2 = {IB101['t_half_pk']} min:")
for s in out["KI_scan"]:
    p(f"  KI = {s['KI_M']*1e9:6.1f} nM (kinact {s['kinact_min']:.3f}/min, D0/KI {s['D0_over_KI']:5.1f}):"
      f"  I = {s['capture_integral']:7.1f}  captured = {100*s['captured']:.4f}%")
p("\nInstantaneous crosslink half-life at therapeutic plasma levels:")
for s in out["instantaneous"]:
    p(f"  D = {s['D_M']*1e9:.0f} nM, KI = {s['KI_M']*1e9:.0f} nM -> t1/2 = {s['t_half_min']:.1f} min"
      f"  (plasma t1/2 = {IB101['t_half_pk']} min)")

# ---- 6. model-free lower bound straight from the paper's own sentence -----
# "the plasma concentration of IB101 stayed above 5 nM (75 ng/ml) for at least 4 hours"
# Exact for a constant floor: no linear approximation, so it stays valid when D ~ KI.
D_fl, t_fl = 5e-9, 4 * 3600
lb = []
for KI in (5e-10, 1e-9, 1.1e-8, 1e-7, 1e-6):
    kinact = IB101["eff"] * KI
    lb.append(dict(KI_M=KI, capture_integral=float(kinact * D_fl / (KI + D_fl) * t_fl)))
I_lb = min(x["capture_integral"] for x in lb)
out["lower_bound"] = dict(floor_M=D_fl, seconds=t_fl, by_KI=lb,
                          worst_capture_integral=I_lb,
                          worst_margin_90=I_lb / np.log(10),
                          worst_captured=float(1 - np.exp(-I_lb)))
json.dump(out, open("results.json", "w"), indent=2, default=float)
print("\nModel-free bound (>=5 nM held for >=4 h; exact in KI, no PK model):")
for x in lb:
    print(f"  KI = {x['KI_M']*1e9:7.2f} nM -> I >= {x['capture_integral']:6.1f}"
          f"   capture >= {100*(1-np.exp(-x['capture_integral'])):.3f}%")
print(f"  worst case I = {I_lb:.1f} -> margin {I_lb/np.log(10):.1f}x over the 90% threshold")

# ---- 7. recover IB101's KI from the two numbers the paper does report -----
# kobs at 10 uM is the saturated rate (10 uM >> KI), so kinact = kobs, and
# KI = kinact / (kinact/KI). Table S4 lists kinact and KI as N.D. for IB101.
kinact_101 = IB101["kobs_min"] * MIN                       # s^-1
KI_101 = kinact_101 / IB101["eff"]                         # M
# IB201 shows kobs(SDS-PAGE) and kinact(BLI) differ by 2.25x, so carry that as the band.
band = out["IB201_regime"]["ratio"]
out["IB101_KI_inferred"] = dict(
    kinact_s=kinact_101, KI_M=KI_101, KI_nM=KI_101 * 1e9,
    KI_low_nM=KI_101 / band * 1e9, KI_high_nM=KI_101 * band * 1e9,
    note="band from the IB201 method-to-method ratio, not a fitted error",
    parent_KN035_KD_nM=0.3, plasma_floor_nM=5.0, selection_nM=5.0)

# capture at the inferred KI, using only the stated plasma floor
for key, KI in (("inferred", KI_101), ("low", KI_101 / band), ("high", KI_101 * band)):
    ki = IB101["eff"] * KI
    I = ki * D_fl / (KI + D_fl) * t_fl
    out["IB101_KI_inferred"][f"capture_integral_{key}"] = float(I)
    out["IB101_KI_inferred"][f"captured_{key}"] = float(1 - np.exp(-I))

# KI below which even the floor bound fails to reach 90%
KI_crit = np.log(10) / (IB101["eff"] * t_fl)
out["IB101_KI_inferred"]["KI_critical_nM"] = KI_crit * 1e9
json.dump(out, open("results.json", "w"), indent=2, default=float)

z = out["IB101_KI_inferred"]
print(f"\nIB101 KI recovered from tables S3 + S4: {z['KI_nM']:.2f} nM"
      f"  (band {z['KI_low_nM']:.2f}-{z['KI_high_nM']:.2f} nM)")
print(f"  plasma floor {z['plasma_floor_nM']} nM, selection {z['selection_nM']} nM,"
      f" parent KN035 KD {z['parent_KN035_KD_nM']} nM")
for key in ("low", "inferred", "high"):
    print(f"  {key:>8}: I = {z['capture_integral_'+key]:5.1f}"
          f"  capture = {100*z['captured_'+key]:.3f}%  margin {z['capture_integral_'+key]/np.log(10):.1f}x")
print(f"  floor bound fails 90% only if KI < {z['KI_critical_nM']:.2f} nM")
cross_literature.py — the one-axis comparison
"""Put covalent small molecules and covalent proteins on one axis.

Every entry is a measured value from a paper read in full; the efficiency
constant kinact/KI is the quantity that governs capture when free drug sits
below KI, so it is the only one that compares across the two literatures.
"""
import json
import numpy as np

H, MIN = 1 / 3600.0, 1 / 60.0

rows = [
    # name, kinact (s^-1), KI (M), eff (M^-1 s^-1) or None, source
    # Canon 2019 extended data fig. 1f, mass spectrometry, purified protein:
    # kinact 8.5e-1 +/- 7.8e-2 s^-1; KI 8.6e-5 +/- 1.4e-5 M; ratio 9.9e3 +/- 1.8e3 M^-1 s^-1
    ("Sotorasib (KRAS G12C, approved)", 8.5e-1, 8.6e-5, None,
     "Canon 2019 ED fig. 1f (biochemical); same values used by Farhan 2026 table 1"),
    ("IB101 (anti-PD-L1 nanobody)", 0.18 * MIN, None, 6.0e5,
     "Fan 2026 tables S3, S4; KI recovered here"),
    ("IB201 (covalent IL-18)", 4.0e-3, 1.1e-8, 3.6e5,
     "Fan 2026 table S4"),
    ("PD-1(FSY) vs PD-L1 His69", None, None, 886 * MIN,
     "Li 2020, second-order, equimolar"),
    ("dZHER2(37FSY) vs HER2", None, None, 4188 * MIN,
     "Li 2020, second-order, equimolar"),
]

out = []
for name, kin, KI, eff, src in rows:
    if eff is None:
        eff = kin / KI
    if KI is None and kin is not None:

                    eff_M_s=eff, source=src))

    ki = "n.d." if r["KI_nM"] is None else f"{r['KI_nM']:.4g}"
    print(f"{r['name']:34s} {km:>11s} {ki:>12s} {r['eff_M_s']:>18.3g}")

sot, ib101 = out[0], out[1]
print(f"\nSotorasib vs IB101:")
print(f"  chemistry  kinact: sotorasib is {sot['kinact_min']/ib101['kinact_min']:.0f}x faster")
print(f"  binding    KI:     sotorasib is {sot['KI_M']/ib101['KI_M']:.0f}x weaker")
print(f"  product    kinact/KI: IB101 is {ib101['eff_M_s']/sot['eff_M_s']:.1f}x larger")

# Which regime does each operate in? Free drug vs KI.
print("\nOperating regime (free drug relative to KI):")
for label, D, r in (("sotorasib, unbound plasma ~1 uM (Farhan: << KI)", 1e-6, sot),
                    ("IB101, plasma floor 5 nM", 5e-9, ib101)):
