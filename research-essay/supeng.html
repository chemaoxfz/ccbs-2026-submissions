Metabolic flexibility predicts temporal fluctuation scaling in gut bacteria

Genome-scale metabolic models connect carbon source degradation and KEGG pathway composition to species Taylor's law.

Peng Su · 3 November 2026

Thesis. Microbiota species-level temporal fluctuation scaling in the gut is better explained by modeled metabolic flexibility, particularly the ability to utilize diverse carbon sources and the completeness of specific KEGG pathways, than by genome size or taxonomic identity alone.

Problem

The unexplained variation of fluctuation scaling

Gut bacterial abundances fluctuate over days, weeks and months, yet those trajectories are not arbitrary. Across microbial communities, the temporal variance of abundance commonly scales as a power of its temporal mean, an ecological relationship known as Taylor’s law.[1][2]

log(V) =a+blog(M)

For a species-level Taylor's law, one bacterial ASV is considered at a time. Its mean temporal abundance M and temporal variance V are calculated in each host, and the slope b is estimated across hosts. The slope therefore describes how rapidly the temporal variance of that species increases as its mean abundance changes across host environments. It is not itself a direct measure of abundance, growth rate or metabolic activity.

This statistical relationship is well established, but its biological interpretation remains incomplete. Species-level slopes differ substantially among gut microbiota. Taxonomic identity explains some of that variation, and genome size provides a coarse measure of coding capacity, but neither directly states which nutrients a bacterium can convert into biomass. The unresolved question is therefore functional: do the metabolic options available to a bacterial species help explain how its abundance fluctuates?

Problem in one lineTaylor's law compresses a time series into a slope, but the biological traits that make one bacterial species' slope different from another's are not known.
Challenge

From abundance trajectories to metabolic phenotypes

Answering the question requires joining two kinds of information that are not naturally connected. Longitudinal 16S data describe when an ASV is abundant, but they do not reveal its reaction network. Conversely, a reconstructed metabolic network lists biochemical possibilities, but it does not directly describe the organism’s ecological fluctuations in a human host.

The first challenge is therefore one of correspondence. Each ASV used in the ecological analysis must be linked to a sufficiently similar, high-quality isolate genome before a genome-scale metabolic model can be built. Using the human microbiome dataset originally generated by Wastyk et al.,[7] the present study retained 215 ASVs with abundance trajectories suitable for calculating species Taylor’s law.[1] Of these, only 18 could be mapped reliably to unique gut bacterial isolate genomes and carried forward into metabolic modeling.[1]

The second challenge is confounding. A common species may have both a better-characterized genome and a more stable estimate of its Taylor’s law slope. An apparent metabolic association could therefore arise from average abundance rather than metabolism. The analysis must ask not only whether metabolic features correlate with b, but whether the association remains after abundance is controlled.

The third challenge is interpretive. A genome-scale model describes feasible reactions under stated constraints. It does not show which reactions are expressed in vivo, and flux balance analysis does not simulate the full kinetics of the gut. The resulting carbon-use phenotype must therefore be presented as a model prediction, not as a direct measurement.

The central inferential boundaryThe measured quantity is abundance over time. Carbon-source growth and pathway completeness are reconstructed model properties. Any ecological mechanism connecting them remains an interpretation unless it is tested experimentally.
Approach

Reconstructing metabolic flexibility from matched genomes

The analysis converted each of the 18 genome-matched ASVs into three increasingly specific descriptions of metabolic capacity: total reaction-network size, the number of carbon sources predicted to support growth, and the representation of reactions in individual KEGG pathways.

  1. Map ASVs to genomes. Human ASVs were matched to isolates in the CAMII collection at greater than 97% 16S similarity. Candidate genomes were required to have at least 80% completeness, less than 5% contamination and greater than 75% purity. The best qualifying isolate was retained for each ASV.
  2. Reconstruct reaction networks. ModelSEED was used to generate a genome-scale metabolic model for each selected ASV.[4] Reaction count provided a first measure of network size.
  3. Challenge each model with carbon sources. Using the COBRA Toolbox, each model was supplied with one of 24 extracellular carbon sources and biomass flux was maximized.[5] In standard FBA notation, the optimization is
maxv cTv subject to Sv=0, l≤v≤u.

Here S is the stoichiometric matrix, v is the flux vector, and the bounds l and u encode reaction directionality and environmental availability. The objective vector c selects biomass production. FBA therefore predicts the largest feasible biomass flux under the model constraints; it does not claim that the cell literally solves an optimization problem.[5]

Biomass fluxes were normalized within each model. A Gaussian mixture model fitted to independent phenotype-microarray data supplied a threshold of 37% of maximum normalized growth. A carbon source was counted as usable when the predicted normalized biomass exceeded that threshold. This generated one interpretable phenotype for each ASV: the number of carbon sources predicted to support growth.

Finally, ModelSEED reaction annotations were mapped to KEGG pathways.[6] For each pathway, the number of represented reactions was compared with species Taylor’s law slope using partial Pearson correlation while controlling for average ASV abundance. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied across pathways.

Approach in one lineThe same reconstructed network was summarized three ways—its size, its predicted carbon-use breadth and its pathway composition—so that a generic “large genome” explanation could be progressively replaced by more functional tests.
Proof · Part I

Species metabolic capacities carries information beyond genome size

Genome size provided the initial comparison. Across the 18 modeled ASVs, larger genomes were associated with lower species Taylor’s law slopes (r=−0.48, p<0.05). That association alone cannot distinguish metabolic capacity from the many other traits that accompany a larger genome.

Reaction-network size gave a stronger relationship: species represented by models containing more reactions had lower Taylor’s law slopes (r=−0.61, p<0.005). More importantly, the network-size relationship remained significant after controlling for average ASV abundance, whereas the genome-size relationship did not. The model therefore sharpened the association from total genetic content to the number of available metabolic transformations.

The carbon-source simulations then asked whether network function, rather than network size alone, carried additional information. ASVs predicted to grow on a larger number of carbon sources had lower Taylor’s law slopes (r=−0.61, p<0.01). The association remained after separately controlling for average abundance (rpartial=−0.50, p<0.05) and for metabolic network size (rpartial=−0.47, p<0.05).

This control is the decisive step. If carbon-source count were merely another name for a large reaction network, its association should disappear when reaction count is held fixed. It did not. Carbon-source breadth therefore contributed information about ecological fluctuation scaling that was not captured by network size alone.

The class-level comparison adds biological specificity. Growth on carbohydrate carbon sources was more strongly associated with lower Taylor’s law slopes than growth on amino-acid sources (Wilcoxon p<0.02). The result does not imply that amino-acid metabolism is irrelevant. It shows that, among the carbon sources tested, the relationship was concentrated more strongly in carbohydrate utilization.

Proof · Part II

Pathway composition matters, not only network size

A network-size result treats every reaction as equivalent. The next analysis instead asked which reactions, and which biological pathways, distinguished bacteria with different Taylor’s law slopes.

For each reconstructed reaction, the 18 ASVs were divided according to whether the reaction was present or absent. This screen identified 114 reactions significantly associated with species Taylor’s law scaling after Benjamini–Hochberg correction (p<0.05). Across these reactions, the estimated difference in slope between reaction-present and reaction-absent groups ranged from 24% to 39%.

Reaction annotations were then aggregated into KEGG pathways. After average ASV abundance was controlled and pathway tests were corrected for multiplicity, nine pathways showed significant associations with species Taylor’s law slope at the study’s adjusted p<0.1 threshold. The plotted effect size is the fitted difference in Taylor’s law slope between the ASV with the highest pathway representation and the ASV with the lowest.

Greater representation of the one-carbon pool by folate, sphingolipid metabolism, and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism corresponded to lower Taylor’s law slopes. Several other negatively associated pathways concerned central metabolism, cofactor metabolism or the processing of carbon-containing compounds. These pathway-level results are consistent with the broader carbon-source analysis: bacteria with more complete and diverse metabolic machinery tended to have lower slopes.

The one positive association was an anthocyanin-related pathway. Because anthocyanins are plant-derived compounds, this direction is consistent with—but does not prove—the possibility that a specialized metabolic role may accompany higher fluctuation scaling. The contrast is useful: the result is not simply “more pathway reactions always lower the slope.” The identity and ecological role of the pathway matter.

Supported claimThe relationship is carried by network composition as well as network size. Specific metabolic functions distinguish bacteria with different fluctuation scaling, which is why a pathway-resolved analysis is more informative than counting genes or reactions alone.
Significance

Metabolic flexibility links cellular capability to ecological dynamics

Taylor’s law is a statistical regularity. Genome-scale modeling gives that regularity a functional interpretation. The analysis builds a bridge from the reactions available inside a bacterial cell to a property of its abundance fluctuations across hosts:

genome→ reaction network→ carbon-use breadth→ fluctuation scaling

One ecological interpretation is that a metabolic generalist is less dependent on any single fluctuating resource. When one substrate becomes scarce, alternative substrates may remain available. A specialist, by contrast, may track the availability of a smaller set of resources more closely. Consumer–resource models have shown that resource fluctuations and overlap in resource use can reproduce macroecological patterns in microbial time series, making this interpretation mechanistically plausible.[3]

The same idea may alter competition. Species with broad but distinct resource options need not compete as strongly for one limiting substrate, whereas specialists sharing the same resource can experience synchronized limitation. This supplies a possible route from metabolic capability to abundance variance. However, neither substrate switching nor pairwise competition was directly measured here, so this route remains a hypothesis generated by the observed association.

The broader significance is methodological. Taxonomy tells which organisms are related; genome-scale metabolic models ask what transformations their genomes may support under specified environments. The present results show that this model-derived functional layer contains ecological information that is not recovered by genome size alone. That is precisely where constraint-based modeling becomes useful in microbial ecology: not as a replacement for longitudinal measurement, but as a way to turn genome content into testable phenotypes.

What changes if the thesis is right?Temporal stability cannot be understood only from who is present or how abundant each species is. The metabolic options available to each species become candidate predictors—and eventually experimental control variables—for community dynamics.
Qualification

What the metabolic models do not establish

The strongest version of the claim would be that carbon-source flexibility causes lower Taylor’s law slopes. The present evidence does not establish that version.

First, the modeled sample is small. Only 18 ASVs could be linked reliably to representative isolate genomes. Partial correlations reduce specific confounding concerns, but they cannot remove uncertainty caused by sample size or unmeasured species traits.

Second, reconstructed capability is not in vivo activity. A reaction may be present in a model but transcriptionally silent in the gut. Conversely, an incomplete genome annotation may omit a real reaction. Automated reconstruction and gap filling make large-scale comparison possible, but they also introduce model uncertainty.[4]

Third, FBA answers a conditional question. It finds a flux state that satisfies steady-state mass balance and maximizes the chosen biomass objective under specified bounds. The real gut is spatially structured, time-varying and shaped by host absorption, cross-feeding and regulation. A carbon source predicted to support growth in silico may not be accessible or preferred in vivo.

Fourth, pathway representation is not pathway flux. Counting reactions assigned to a KEGG pathway describes network content. It does not measure enzyme abundance, pathway activity or metabolite production. The pathway analysis localizes statistical associations; it does not demonstrate the biochemical route producing the ecological phenotype.

The cheapest decisive follow-up would combine controlled growth experiments with time-resolved community measurements. The 18 isolates could be tested on the same 24 carbon sources used in the simulations, validating carbon-use breadth directly. Synthetic communities containing predicted generalists and specialists could then be exposed to controlled resource fluctuations. If metabolic breadth is mechanistically important, broad-resource users should retain lower fluctuation scaling under matched mean abundance and controlled resource variance. Failure of that prediction would weaken the generalist interpretation even if the original correlation remained.

Claim after qualificationThe study shows that model-derived metabolic flexibility is a robust correlate of species Taylor’s law within the analyzed ASVs. It proposes, but does not yet prove, that resource flexibility and competition generate the difference.

Reproducibility note for the reported statistics

The numerical results in this essay are reported from the original analysis and checked against the manuscript text and figure labels. A final submission should accompany this HTML with, or point to, the original analysis code and the 18-ASV feature table so that the correlations can be rerun. The essential checks are:

# schematic R checks; column names should match the archived analysis table
cor.test(df$genome_size, df$tl_slope, method = "pearson")
cor.test(df$reaction_count, df$tl_slope, method = "pearson")
cor.test(df$carbon_source_count, df$tl_slope, method = "pearson")

# partial correlations
ppcor::pcor.test(df$carbon_source_count, df$tl_slope,
                 df$mean_abundance, method = "pearson")
ppcor::pcor.test(df$carbon_source_count, df$tl_slope,
                 df$reaction_count, method = "pearson")

The code above documents the reported tests but cannot reproduce the numerical output without the original 18-row analysis table. No claim is made that the correlations were newly rerun in this HTML draft.
