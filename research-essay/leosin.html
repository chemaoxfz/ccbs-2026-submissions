A perfect population movie can hide the wrong cell dynamics

What single-cell snapshots identify, what transport chooses, and which experiment can tell them apart.

Leo Sin (施延庆) · Westlake University
EST 5053 · Control and Computation in Biological Systems · Research essay · 5 November 2026

Suppose an AI model predicts the distribution of cell states at every developmental time, including every time that was withheld during training. Has it recovered how individual cells move, or how they would respond to an intervention? Those conclusions require more information. This essay shows why with a population that expands while its cells may also rotate. Every rotation speed produces exactly the same population movie, yet different cell trajectories and pulse responses. This is a counterexample to a general inference, not a claim that all real developmental datasets have the same ambiguity.

The underlying limitation is established in the literature. Weinreb and colleagues described symmetries of population balance that hide dynamics, and Lavenant and colleagues state assumptions under which recovery becomes possible. A recent preprint by Nguyen and Malysheva studies the divergence constraint, transport's minimum-action selection, and ways that autonomous dynamics can become identifiable.[1][2][3] My contribution here is a worked tutorial that connects this obstruction to a control experiment and to an explicit sample-size calculation. The four figures, numerical checks and interactive example use synthetic data only.

The PCAPS argument

Problem: reconstruct developmental dynamics from destructive snapshots. Challenge: density changes constrain net probability flow, leaving some motion invisible. Approach: make that invisible motion an adjustable rotation in an exactly solvable model. Proof: derive equal distributions but unequal trajectories and pulse responses, then verify the equations numerically. Significance: choose validation measurements that can distinguish the explanations the training data leave equivalent.

Reading route and evidence

Sections 1–2 define the inference problem. Section 3 is the counterexample. Sections 4–6 explain its consequences for regularization, control and sample size. Section 7 turns these into a proposed benchmark. Appendix A contains the complete executable analysis. Bracketed references identify literature claims. Equations and figures labelled as this essay's calculation are deductions under the stated model, not measurements of cells.

1A population movie is not a cell movie

The first question is what the experiment has actually measured.

In a single-cell RNA-sequencing time course, different cells are sacrificed at different times. We observe samples from a distribution of molecular states, rather than repeated measurements of each cell. This destructive sampling motivates trajectory-inference methods such as TrajectoryNet and Global Waddington-OT (gWOT).[4][2] Here a state is a vector of measured or modelled cell features. A trajectory is that vector's path through time. A marginal distribution describes the states at just one time, after forgetting which cell came from where.

A simple example makes the missing information visible. If one sample contains 50% state A and 50% state B, and a later sample has the same composition, cells could have stayed put or exchanged states. The percentages alone do not distinguish those possibilities. A coupling specifies the joint distribution of earlier and later states, including who is related to whom. Many couplings can share the same marginals. Identifiability asks whether, within a specified model class and observation scheme, distinct mechanisms can produce exactly the same observations. It is different from an optimizer returning a single answer.

Existing methods make additional structure explicit. TrajectoryNet represents continuous motion with a neural ordinary differential equation and adds energy, density, growth and optional velocity penalties. gWOT estimates a law over trajectories through an entropy-based formulation and proves recovery results under potential-driven stochastic dynamics. Simulation-free score and flow matching, [SF]2M, learns stochastic bridges from unpaired observations relative to a specified reference process.[4][2][5] These are principled ways to choose an explanation. Whether the chosen explanation is the biological mechanism depends on the assumptions and on what other evidence tests it.

Three questions to keep separate

Distribution prediction: where will the population be? Trajectory prediction: where will this cell, or this initial cohort, go? Intervention prediction: how will a specified manipulation change the response? In this essay, a mechanism is the state-space dynamical law. Identifying that law would still not, by itself, identify a causal gene-regulatory network.

2Conservation determines net flow

A density can change without revealing every component of motion.

Let ρ(x,t) be a probability density and v(x,t) a deterministic velocity field in state space. For now assume no division, death, diffusion or unobserved states. Conservation then gives the continuity equation:

∂tρ+∇·(ρv)=0.(1)

The vector j=ρv is probability current. Its divergence, ∇·j, measures net outward flow from a small region. Incoming and outgoing flows can balance even when the particles inside are moving. This population-balance viewpoint is the starting point of Weinreb et al.'s analysis, with additional terms for cell gain and loss.[1]

Suppose v explains an observed density movie. Add a velocity w. The new field explains the same movie whenever

∇·(ρw)=0.(2)

This is a condition on density-weighted current. Merely checking ∇·w=0 is insufficient, because ∇·(ρw)=ρ∇·w+w·∇ρ. A circulating current can satisfy (2) while changing individual paths. The literature calls this a gauge freedom or a weighted-solenoidal ambiguity.[1][3]

There is also a direct construction. In two dimensions, let J rotate a vector anticlockwise by 90°, and let ψ be a smooth scalar function with compact support where ρ>0. Set w=J∇ψ/ρ. Then ∇·(ρw)=∇·(J∇ψ)=0, because mixed second derivatives cancel. This calculation exhibits the freedom explicitly. Suitable smoothness and boundary conditions are required to turn a local current construction into a well-defined global flow.

What follows

Even perfect knowledge of a density's change does not generally determine the entire velocity. More model capacity or a more accurate density loss cannot supply an observation of the missing current. Additional assumptions or measurements can.

3An expanding Gaussian hides any rotation

A solvable example makes the observation-level ambiguity exact.

Take two dimensionless state coordinates, x=(x1,x2), and dimensionless time 0≤t≤1. These are abstract state coordinates, not literal transcript counts or tissue positions. Start with X0~𝒩(0,I2). Choose the population scale s(t)=1+t, and define a family of velocities with angular speed ω:

vω(x,t)=x1+t+ωJx,J=(0−110).(3)

The first term expands the cloud. The second turns each state around the origin. Linear expansion is chosen for a transparent closed-form example, not fitted as a biological growth law. If Rθ is the ordinary rotation matrix through angle θ, solving (3) gives

Xt=(1+t)RωtX0,Xt~𝒩(0,(1+t)2I2).(4)

The second result follows because rotating an isotropic Gaussian leaves its distribution unchanged. Its density is therefore

ρ(x,t)=12π(1+t)2exp[−‖x‖22(1+t)2].(5)

Equation (5) contains no ω. Equivalently, the added velocity w=ωJx has zero divergence and is perpendicular to the radial density gradient, so (2) holds. The density changes over time, but the rotational part stays hidden. This essay's expanding example illustrates the circulation obstruction described by Weinreb et al., Lavenant et al., and, especially closely, the Gaussian analysis in Nguyen and Malysheva's preprint.[1][2][3]

Counterexample, with its scope

For the deterministic family (3) with the fixed isotropic Gaussian initial law, all real values of ω produce the same one-time marginal distributions for every t∈[0,1]. The parameter ω is therefore unidentifiable from those marginals, even with infinitely many cells and continuous temporal observation of the population density.

This is stronger than a gap between two sparsely sampled times: there is no intermediate density that would separate the models. It is also narrower than saying that time-series snapshots never identify dynamics. The symmetry and model class are essential to the claim.

4Minimum transport cost selects a particular explanation

A unique optimum can be a unique answer to the wrong biological question.

Consider kinetic action over the interval:

𝒜(v)=∫01𝔼ρt[‖v(Xt,t)‖2]dt.(6)

This objective measures expected squared state-space speed integrated over time. It is not automatically physical energy expenditure or ATP use. Dynamic optimal transport uses such a quadratic action, and TrajectoryNet adds an energy penalty based on it.[4] In our family, x·Jx=0 and 𝔼‖Xt‖2=2(1+t)2. Expansion and rotation contribute independently:

𝒜(ω)=∫01[2+2(1+t)2ω2]dt=2+143ω2.(7)

Zero rotation minimizes (7), although every member fits all densities exactly. In fact, straight scaling x↦2x is globally optimal for these endpoints: for any coupling, Cauchy–Schwarz gives 𝔼[X0·X1]≤2·8=4, hence 𝔼‖X1−X0‖2≥2+8−8=2. Straight paths under scaling attain action 2 and match the entire density movie. Thus the example does not rely on a failed optimizer.

Suppose the generating model has ω=π/2, but the reconstruction chooses zero. For the same initial cell, the discrepancy at the final time is

𝔼[‖2RωX0−2X0‖2]=41−cosω.(8)

At a quarter turn this RMS error is 4, while every population discrepancy between the exact laws is zero. A particular cell starting at (1,0) ends at (0,2) in the generating model and (2,0) in the minimum-action model. Equation (8) compares a known pairing in the synthetic example. It cannot be estimated from unpaired real snapshots without additional information.

The ambiguity also changes a conditional endpoint prediction. Consider cells initially in the right half-plane, X0,1>0. Without rotation every one ends in the right half-plane. After a quarter turn, the final first coordinate is −2X0,2. Since the two initial Gaussian coordinates are independent, exactly half of that initial cohort ends on the right. The unconditional final fraction on the right is one half in both models. This is a state-defined endpoint classification, not a claim about irreversible biological commitment, but it shows precisely how an accurate population composition can hide an incorrect cohort outcome.

The general interpretation is a choice of representative among observationally equivalent flows. Nguyen and Malysheva characterize a minimum-action projection onto gradient velocities under their regularity conditions.[3] A bridge method makes an analogous choice relative to a reference path law. In [SF]2M, the bridge objective is a relative-entropy minimization, and a Brownian reference determines the associated entropic transport problem.[5] These objectives can be scientifically appropriate when their priors are justified. Perfect marginal fit alone does not justify them.

5A directional pulse makes the rotation observable

A control experiment can change the information available to inference.

How could we distinguish the models cheaply in this toy world? Prepare a second initial population with a known small mean shift, m(0)=δe1, where e1=(1,0) and δ=0.5. Keep its covariance equal to I2. Assume that the preparation changes the initial state distribution without changing the velocity law. This is an initial-condition pulse, not a gene knockout, a change of the model parameters, or a demonstrated laboratory protocol.

Because the dynamics are linear, the mean obeys the same ODE. Its response is

mω(t)=δ(1+t)(cos(ωt)sin(ωt)).(9)

At the final time the nonrotating model predicts m0(1)=(1,0), whereas the quarter-turn model predicts mπ/2(1)=(0,1). This disagreement is observable from means in the prepared population; it no longer requires tracking the same cell. Both components must be measured. If we measured only the response magnitude, ‖mω(t)‖=δ(1+t), rotation would remain invisible.

This is the course's control perspective on the inference problem: an unperturbed output can be insufficient even when its entire time course is known. Changing the preparation and measuring a directional output can distinguish models. Locally near zero rotation, ∂ωm2(1)=2δ, so this output has a first-order sensitivity that the isotropic unperturbed density lacks. That sensitivity is this essay's calculation, not a claim that a biological perturbation always leaves its underlying vector field unchanged.

Prediction is not full causal identification

One pulse separates the two specified models. It does not identify an arbitrary nonlinear high-dimensional field. Nor does a successful pulse response reveal which genes causally regulate which others. Different candidate fields, hidden variables, pulse mechanisms and measured outputs require their own identifiability analysis.

6Breaking symmetry creates information, but weakly

Real distributions need not be perfectly circular. How much does that help?

Replace the initial covariance by a known ellipse, Σ0=diag(1+ϵ,1−ϵ), with 0<ϵ<1. Keep the same dynamics. Now

Σω(t)=(1+t)2RωtΣ0RωtT.(10)

Rotation changes the ellipse's orientation. The symmetry is broken, so the final density can contain information about ω. That does not mean the information is strong. At the final time, this essay's covariance calculation gives

‖Σω(1)−Σω=0(1)‖F4=22ϵ|sinω|.(11)

Here the comparison is with the zero-rotation covariance at time 1. The Frobenius norm sums squared matrix entries before taking a square root. The signal distinguishing rotation scales linearly with anisotropy ϵ, and vanishes in the circular limit.

We can quantify the implication for sample size without fitting a neural network. Suppose we have n independent final-time samples, know the initial covariance, its orientation, the scale and the time, and estimate only ω. For a zero-mean Gaussian with covariance Σ, the expected curvature of its log likelihood gives Fisher information per sample. Substituting (10), with Σ′=∂ωΣ, yields

ℐω=12tr[(Σ−1Σ′)2]=4ϵ21−ϵ2.(12)

To check the substitution, at zero rotation Σ=4diag(1+ϵ,1−ϵ) and Σ′=8ϵ(0110). Multiplying these two small matrices gives (12). At other angular speeds the matrices are rotated together, leaving the trace unchanged.

For a locally unbiased regular estimator, the Cramér–Rao inequality therefore gives a standard-deviation lower bound

sd(ω^)≥1−ϵ24nϵ2.(13)

The inequality follows from a local score argument: the score is the derivative of log likelihood, has mean zero and variance nℐω, and its covariance with a locally unbiased estimator is 1. Cauchy–Schwarz then gives Var(ω^)nℐω≥1. This is a local statement on one identifiable parameter branch.

With n=1000, this bound is 0.157 radians per time unit at ϵ=0.1, but 1.581 at ϵ=0.01. In the weakly anisotropic regime, reducing anisotropy tenfold requires about one hundred times as many cells to maintain the same local precision. These are analytic bounds under the specified Gaussian experiment, not measured estimation errors or guarantees that a particular method attains them.

There is a second limitation: an ellipse is unchanged after a half turn. A single final-time density cannot globally distinguish ω from ω+π, even when (12) is positive. Equally spaced density observations at tk=kΔ preserve an alias between ω and ω+π/Δ. Additional timing, a justified speed range, or a directional measurement is needed. Nguyen and Malysheva explicitly separate such finite-snapshot aliasing from identifiability based on instantaneous density derivatives.[3] Thus local sensitivity, global uniqueness and attainable finite-sample precision are three different questions.

The practical insight

For this model, cells are informative about angular speed in proportion to nϵ2 when anisotropy is small. A larger circular dataset cannot reveal rotation. A weakly elliptical dataset may reveal it only imprecisely. A carefully chosen pulse changes the observation itself.

7Match the benchmark to the claim

The useful response is to state what has been learned and design a test for what has not.

The counterexample suggests a concrete evaluation protocol for models of developing cell populations. First state the object being predicted: a marginal density, a transition law, a velocity, a pulse response or a gene-level intervention. Then hold out a measurement that is sensitive to that object. This proposal follows from the observation distinctions in Sections 1–6; it is not a report of a benchmark performed on biological data.

RNA velocity illustrates why an additional data modality can matter. La Manno and colleagues estimate near-future transcriptional change by distinguishing unspliced and spliced RNA and modelling their kinetics. It supplies directional information beyond the static distribution of a single expression representation, under the kinetic assumptions of the method.[6] It should be treated as an informative, model-dependent observation rather than as a measured full long-term trajectory. In a prospective benchmark I would use independent lineage or tracking information to test transitions and a separately specified perturbation to test response.

Why adding space is useful without being an automatic solution

For spatial omics, a state could include expression z and registered tissue position r, giving a joint density q(z,r,t). My inference from (2) is conditional: space can constrain candidate paths when the joint data break their equivalence, but it does not automatically do so. For example, if q(z,r,t)=ρ(z,t)η(r,t) and the hidden velocity acts only as the rotation in the isotropic two-dimensional z-plane, then ∇z·(qωJz)=0. Merely appending an independent spatial coordinate leaves this ambiguity intact. This is a constructed mathematical example, not a characterization of any particular spatial-omics dataset or algorithm.

What the toy assumptions omit

For a raw cell number density c, division and loss introduce a source term: ∂tc+∇·(cv)=gc. For the normalized probability density, this becomes ∂tρ+∇·(ρv)=(g−g¯)ρ, with g¯=∫gρdx, assuming vanishing boundary flux. This normalization is obtained by differentiating ρ=c/∫cdx. Unknown growth can confound redistribution with changes in cell number, a problem emphasized by Weinreb et al. and addressed by growth priors in TrajectoryNet.[1][4] The expanding scale in our example describes state-space dispersion, not proliferation.

With known constant isotropic diffusion D, the probability current is j=ρb−D∇ρ, where b is the stochastic drift. Adding a drift component satisfying (2) still leaves its density contribution invisible. If diffusion is unknown as well, equal densities can hide a drift–diffusion tradeoff. Lavenant et al. demonstrate that issue and explicitly assume known diffusion in their identifiability result.[2] Their positive result matters: potential-driven drift, appropriate regularity and sufficiently informative temporal sampling make recovery possible in their specified setting. Our rotational field falls outside that potential-driven class.

A 2026 result strengthens this positive picture. Guan and colleagues establish joint identifiability of an autonomous gradient drift and constant Brownian diffusivity from a full marginal path observed outside equilibrium, under their stated regularity conditions. At equilibrium their Proposition 3.1 gives a drift–diffusion rescaling ambiguity.[7] Known diffusion is therefore not a universal prerequisite for inference. Their Theorem 3.2 concerns a restricted stochastic model class. The example in (3) instead permits rotation, an explicitly time-dependent radial term, and no diffusion. Which class is justified is a substantive part of the biological hypothesis.

A different restriction is autonomy: one time-independent field must explain all times. The main expanding example permits a time-dependent radial term. Nguyen and Malysheva show how changing marginal shapes can constrain an autonomous field more strongly, while warning that finite snapshots remain weaker observations than density-and-time-derivative constraints. Their work is a 2026 preprint under review, not an established experimental validation of recovery.[3] The lesson is to name the model class and the observation scheme together.

A falsifiable next step is to fit two marginally indistinguishable candidates, freeze them, and select a held-out directional response on which they disagree. In the worked model, the initial mean shift and the two-component measurement in Section 5 supply that test. In a biological application, specifying how the preparation affects the drift is part of the hypothesis. A failed prediction would reject that candidate and preparation model, not prove that a different candidate has recovered every mechanism.

The central claim is therefore precise: a successful population predictor becomes evidence for dynamics only to the extent that its assumptions are justified and its predictions survive observations that could have distinguished alternative dynamics. The experiment worth buying is the one on which the remaining explanations disagree.

Complete analysis and figure-generation source
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Reproduce the synthetic calculations and four figures in leosin.html.

Python 3.10+, numpy 2.2.6, matplotlib 3.10.9. No biological data, fitting,
GPU, network, or unpublished code is used. All units are dimensionless.
Run: python analysis.py --out results
Tests: python analysis.py --test
The Gaussian circulation construction illustrates established nonidentifiability
(Weinreb et al. 2018; Lavenant et al. 2023; Nguyen & Malysheva 2026).
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import argparse
import json
import math
from pathlib import Path
import unittest

import numpy as np

OMEGA = math.pi / 2
SEED = 20261105
J = np.array([[0., -1.], [1., 0.]])


def rotation(theta: float) -> np.ndarray:
    return np.array([[math.cos(theta), -math.sin(theta)],
                     [math.sin(theta), math.cos(theta)]])


def flow(x0: np.ndarray, t: float, omega: float) -> np.ndarray:
    return (1 + t) * (np.asarray(x0) @ rotation(omega * t).T)


def velocity(x: np.ndarray, t: float, omega: float) -> np.ndarray:
    return np.asarray(x) / (1 + t) + omega * (np.asarray(x) @ J.T)


def density(x: np.ndarray, t: float) -> np.ndarray:
    s = 1 + t
    return np.exp(-np.sum(np.asarray(x) ** 2, axis=-1) / (2 * s * s)) / (2 * math.pi * s * s)


def action(omega: float) -> float:
    return 2 + (14 / 3) * omega ** 2


def endpoint_rms(omega: float) -> float:
    return 4 * math.sqrt(max(0., 1 - math.cos(omega)))


def covariance(t: float, omega: float, epsilon: float) -> np.ndarray:
    if not 0 <= epsilon < 1:
        raise ValueError('epsilon must be in [0, 1)')
    r = rotation(omega * t)
    return (1 + t) ** 2 * r @ np.diag([1 + epsilon, 1 - epsilon]) @ r.T


def fisher(omega: float, epsilon: float, t: float = 1.) -> float:
    del omega  # The information is invariant under rotating the covariance.
    if not 0 <= epsilon < 1:
        raise ValueError('epsilon must be in [0, 1)')
    return 4 * epsilon ** 2 * t ** 2 / (1 - epsilon ** 2)


def crlb_se(epsilon: float, n: int, t: float = 1.) -> float:
    if n <= 0 or t <= 0:
        raise ValueError('n and t must be positive')
    i = fisher(0., epsilon, t)
    return math.inf if i == 0 else 1 / math.sqrt(n * i)


def rk4(x0: np.ndarray, tmax: float, omega: float, steps: int = 1000) -> np.ndarray:
    x = np.asarray(x0, dtype=float).copy()
    h = tmax / steps
    for k in range(steps):
        t = k * h
        k1 = velocity(x, t, omega)
        k2 = velocity(x + h * k1 / 2, t + h / 2, omega)
        k3 = velocity(x + h * k2 / 2, t + h / 2, omega)
        k4 = velocity(x + h * k3, t + h, omega)
        x += h * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4) / 6
    return x


def continuity_residual(points: np.ndarray, t: float, omega: float) -> float:
    h = 1e-5
    dt = (density(points, t + h) - density(points, t - h)) / (2 * h)
    div = np.zeros(len(points))
    for d in range(2):
        dx = np.zeros(2)
        dx[d] = h
        plus = density(points + dx, t) * velocity(points + dx, t, omega)[:, d]
        minus = density(points - dx, t) * velocity(points - dx, t, omega)[:, d]
        div += (plus - minus) / (2 * h)
    return float(np.max(np.abs(dt + div)))


class ModelChecks(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_independent_ode_integration(self) -> None:
        x = np.random.default_rng(SEED).normal(size=(24, 2))
        for omega in [-math.pi, 0., OMEGA, math.pi]:
            np.testing.assert_allclose(rk4(x, 1., omega), flow(x, 1., omega), atol=2e-10)

    def test_marginal_and_current(self) -> None:
        x = np.random.default_rng(SEED).normal(size=(100, 2))
        for t in [0., .3, 1.]:
            for omega in [-2., 0., 2.]:
                y = flow(x, t, omega)
                np.testing.assert_allclose(density(y, t), density(x, 0.) / (1 + t) ** 2, atol=1e-14)
                np.testing.assert_allclose(covariance(t, omega, 0.), (1 + t) ** 2 * np.eye(2), atol=1e-14)
                self.assertLess(continuity_residual(x, t, omega), 2e-9)

    def test_action_and_endpoint(self) -> None:
        ts = np.linspace(0., 1., 10001)
        numerical = np.trapezoid(2 + 2 * (1 + ts) ** 2 * OMEGA ** 2, ts)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(float(numerical), action(OMEGA), places=7)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(endpoint_rms(OMEGA), 4.)
        np.testing.assert_allclose(flow(np.array([.5, 0.]), 1., OMEGA), [0., 1.], atol=1e-14)

    def test_fisher_and_alias(self) -> None:
        h = 1e-5
        for epsilon in [0., .01, .1, .6]:
            c = covariance(1., .4, epsilon)
            dc = (covariance(1., .4 + h, epsilon) - covariance(1., .4 - h, epsilon)) / (2 * h)
            a = np.linalg.solve(c, dc)
            self.assertAlmostEqual(.5 * float(np.trace(a @ a)), fisher(.4, epsilon), places=8)
            np.testing.assert_allclose(covariance(1., .4 + math.pi, epsilon), c, atol=1e-13)
        self.assertTrue(math.isinf(crlb_se(0., 1000)))
        for epsilon in [-.1, 1., 1.1]:
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError):
                covariance(1., .2, epsilon)


def metrics() -> dict:
    points = np.random.default_rng(SEED).normal(size=(100, 2))
    return {
        'seed': SEED, 'omega_reference': OMEGA,
        'marginal_density_difference_exact': 0.,
        'action_zero_rotation': action(0.),
        'action_quarter_turn': action(OMEGA),
        'paired_endpoint_rms_quarter_turn': endpoint_rms(OMEGA),
        'pulse_initial_mean': [.5, 0.],
        'pulse_final_mean_zero_rotation': flow(np.array([.5, 0.]), 1., 0.).tolist(),
        'pulse_final_mean_quarter_turn': flow(np.array([.5, 0.]), 1., OMEGA).tolist(),
        'crlb_se_n1000_epsilon_0.1': crlb_se(.1, 1000),
        'crlb_se_n1000_epsilon_0.01': crlb_se(.01, 1000),
        'max_rk4_absolute_error': float(np.max(np.abs(rk4(points, 1., OMEGA) - flow(points, 1., OMEGA)))),
        'finite_difference_continuity_residual': continuity_residual(points, .4, OMEGA),
    }
