Learning Plant Growth Backward

A dynamical-systems view of GrowFlow: why short trajectories help only when their boundaries carry reliable information.

Weihan Luo · 罗维翰 · CCBS research essay · 6 November 2026

A time-lapse shows a plant getting larger. Reconstructing that growth in three dimensions asks a harder question: which part of yesterday’s plant became which part of today’s? My coauthored paper, Grow with the Flow, represents a plant using persistent geometric elements and learns how they move and deform. It starts from the mature plant and reconstructs earlier stages backward in time.1

This essay asks why training through short reverse trajectories might help. The answer requires a condition: short segments limit local error amplification when their starting states are independently kept accurate. Dividing an uninterrupted trajectory into named pieces supplies no such protection. A second condition follows: locally accurate segments must still agree with one complete trajectory.

The argument in five steps

Problem: recover coherent plant geometry through time. Challenge: reversing a trajectory can amplify errors. Approach: connect local linearization from CCBS to neural ODEs and boundary-based training. Proof: derive exact gains and test a small model with a no-reset control. Significance: separate local stability, global consistency, and biological interpretation.

1What is actually flowing?

The state is a geometric description of a plant.

A Gaussian primitive is a small, soft ellipsoid used to render an image. A collection of these elements approximates visible plant surfaces. GrowFlow evolves each primitive’s position, orientation, and scale, while keeping its identity fixed. Its neural network predicts their rates of change from position and time.1 Collect the evolving geometry into a state vector z. In general neural-ODE notation,

dzdt=fφ(z,t),z(t)=z(t0)+∫t0tfφ(z(s),s)ds(1)

The vector field fφ is a rule assigning a velocity to a state at a time. The parameters φ are learned network weights. Integrating that rule produces a trajectory. Chen and colleagues’ neural ODE framework makes this distinction explicit: a network can specify a derivative rather than directly output the next state.2

The images are observations of the state, not the state itself. Write the observation rule as I≈R(z), where R is the renderer. Hidden surfaces or alternative primitive arrangements can make geometry uncertain even when rendered images look similar. Consequently, low image error alone does not establish low state error.

Here, “growth” means reconstructed shape change. Equation (1) does not identify hormones, cell division, or a biochemical feedback law. That distinction keeps the biological connection precise: the class supplies tools to analyze a dynamical model, while additional experiments would be needed to interpret its learned field as a growth mechanism.

2A reversible equation can have an unreliable inverse

Uniqueness answers whether a trajectory exists, not how accurately we can recover it.

Imagine a relaxing deformation: different initial displacements become almost indistinguishable later. Recovering the original displacement requires magnifying their tiny remaining difference. Under suitable regularity and finite-interval existence assumptions, an ODE can have a unique reversible flow and still make this recovery numerically sensitive. ANODE illustrates this distinction with exponentially contracting forward dynamics whose reverse evolution amplifies perturbations.3

Several ML methods address related difficulties, but their operations differ. An adjoint is an auxiliary differential equation used to compute training gradients. Adaptive Checkpoint Adjoint saves accepted forward states and recomputes local steps, avoiding reliance on a single backward reconstruction of those states.4 GrowFlow’s reverse trajectory instead describes geometric evolution. Checkpointing a gradient calculation and anchoring a geometric reconstruction share an interest in short propagation intervals, but neither is automatically an implementation of the other.

Multiple shooting introduces starting states for several short intervals and then enforces agreement between their endpoints. Differentiable Multiple Shooting Layers formulates this agreement as a root-finding problem.5 This supplies two questions for reconstruction: how much does each segment amplify error, and do the segments actually join?

3Measure separation along a trajectory

Local linearization connects the course to the learned flow.

CCBS Lecture 4 uses a Jacobian to analyze nearby dynamics.6 We can apply the same first-order expansion along a moving trajectory, without assuming the plant is at a fixed point. Let δz be a small difference between two states. Assuming the field is differentiable along the trajectory,

d(δz)dt≈J(t)δz,J(t)=∂fφ∂z(z(t),t)(2)

The Jacobian records how changing each state coordinate changes each velocity. Its effect accumulates through time. Define the flow map Φt,s as the state at time t obtained by starting at time s. Its derivative M=DzΦt,s maps small initial perturbations to final perturbations. The largest singular value g=σmax(M) is the worst directional gain in the chosen Euclidean norm.

Coordinate scaling matters: one unit of position and one unit of orientation are not automatically comparable. We must scale coordinates before interpreting a gain. For non-smooth network activations, this derivative calculation is local where derivatives exist. Finite perturbation tests can check the behavior directly.

To reverse time, introduce the clock τ=T−t and reverse trajectory u(τ)=z(T−τ). The chain rule gives

dudτ=−fφ(u,T−τ).(3)

A forward contracting direction can therefore become a reverse expanding direction. This is a statement about sensitivity to perturbations. It does not imply that every reverse trajectory is unstable or that growth is a relaxation process.

4A model with two directions gives an exact test

One direction expands forward; the other relaxes.

Consider two scaled deformation coordinates x and y. Choose a diagonal linear system so its solution can be checked exactly:

dxdt=rx,dydt=−ky,r=0.2,k=2.(4)

These rates use normalized time. They are teaching choices, not fitted plant measurements. Forward perturbations have gains erτ and e−kτ. Reversing the clock changes them to e−rτ and ekτ. The diagonal structure makes these gains exact even for finite differences.

The direction matters as much as the time direction. After four normalized time units, reverse evolution reduces the first component’s perturbation to about 0.449 times its initial size, while multiplying the second by about 2,981. Calling the entire process simply “backward and unstable” would conceal that difference.

5Subdivision needs an information source

A no-reset control distinguishes a shorter dependency from a renamed long trajectory.

Start reverse integration with an error of ε=10−4 entirely in the amplifying direction. Compare three protocols. One runs for the whole duration. Another runs successive half-unit segments, passing each endpoint directly into the next. The third independently replaces each segment’s initial estimate with an anchor having the same prescribed error ε.

The first two must agree. For a fixed field, exact flow maps compose: applying two consecutive maps gives the same trajectory as integrating over their combined interval. The numerical experiment also uses the same fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps in both protocols, so their final values agree exactly in this implementation. Anchoring is different because it adds an independently bounded state estimate.

In this model, the local peak error for interval length Δ is exactly

For a general differentiable flow, the first-order counterpart is Elocal≲gΔε. This describes propagated initial error. Errors in the learned field and numerical integration add other contributions. Shortening an interval cannot compensate for an arbitrarily poor anchor, and image supervision does not directly guarantee the state-error assumption used here.

The calculation was checked against the exponential solution. Halving the RK4 step repeatedly reduced its endpoint error by approximately the expected factor of sixteen. At step 0.0025, the relative discrepancy was below 10−10. Thus the large separation between protocols comes from the stated anchoring operation, rather than a coarse solver.

6Local agreement does not certify a full rollout

A shared field needs a separate boundary-consistency check.

Suppose training stores boundary estimates bj. Let Ψj denote reverse integration through segment j. A boundary defect measures whether integration from one stored state reaches the next:

dj=bj+1−Ψj(bj).(6)

If a full rollout differs from boundary bj by ej, linearization gives ej+1≈Mjej−dj, where Mj is the segment’s state derivative. Each defect can therefore enter later segments and be amplified.

A scalar example makes this accumulation visible. Store exact boundary values for the reverse relaxation mode with true rate k=2, but integrate using k^=2.05. From each exact starting boundary, a half-unit prediction overshoots the next by a relative amount e0.025−1≈2.53%. One uninterrupted four-unit rollout accumulates e0.2−1≈22.14% relative error.

This example does not say a 2.53% local defect is acceptable. It shows why local fit and full-trajectory fit answer different questions. Reliable anchors control each local prediction only while those anchors are actually used.

7What does this establish about GrowFlow?

The published ablation motivates the explanation; it does not isolate its cause.

GrowFlow progressively reconstructs short reverse intervals and caches boundary states. Its later training phase initializes a new shared field and trains from those cached boundaries. Appendix E’s algorithm does not list an explicit equality constraint matching every integrated endpoint to the next cache. On the reported clematis experiment, removing boundary reconstruction lowers PSNR, an image-fidelity score for which larger is better, from 33.05 to 28.52 dB.1

That result supports the value of the boundary stage in the reported setup. It does not show that perturbation amplification alone caused the difference: initialization, optimization, supervision, and rollout lengths change together. Nor does the absence of an explicit matching constraint prove that the trained method has a large defect. The actual gains, state uncertainties, and cache-to-rollout discrepancies have not been measured here.

The literature suggests a constructive extension. In a 2026 preprint, Persson and colleagues combine multiple shooting with a curriculum that merges short windows until the final objective uses one complete trajectory. Their continuity penalty and final full-window stage address the relationship between easier local training and the eventual global task.7 This is a candidate experiment for reconstruction, not an established improvement to GrowFlow.

8The next experiment should measure both conditions

Choose intervals using measured sensitivity and check the trajectory they produce.

A direct test would first freeze a trained field and perturb selected boundary states in scaled coordinates. Integrating perturbed and unperturbed states over several interval lengths estimates directional gains. Several perturbation magnitudes would check whether the first-order approximation holds. A credible state-uncertainty estimate would additionally need a reconstruction ensemble, independent geometry, or another stated measurement model.

Next, measure the boundary defects in equation (6) and compare a full rollout against the stored states and held-out views. Repeat training with several interval lengths under a matched training budget and comparable supervision. If shorter intervals fail to reduce measured propagation error when anchor accuracy is comparable, the proposed explanation weakens. If local fits improve while full-rollout disagreement grows, boundary consistency needs attention. Held-out geometry and plant-trait measurements would then test usefulness for biological study.

The result

A neural growth field is a dynamical system, so the course’s local-linearization tools apply directly. The new calculation identifies a conditional benefit of short, re-anchored trajectories and a separate obligation to check their global agreement. Smooth geometry is a useful representation; measuring its sensitivity and consistency makes the reconstruction claim more informative.

Reproduce the calculations

The three figures and all synthetic numbers are generated by the Python program below. Save it as analysis/run_analysis.py inside an essay folder, install NumPy and Matplotlib with Python 3, and run that file. It writes a figures folder and analysis/results.json. No trained GrowFlow model or plant measurements are used. The numerical checks compare RK4 to analytic solutions and include the unanchored segmented control.

from pathlib import Path
import json
import numpy as np
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

ROOT = Path(__file__).resolve().parents[1]
FIGURES = ROOT / "figures"
FIGURES.mkdir(exist_ok=True)
GROWTH = 0.2
RELAXATION = 2.0
HORIZON = 4.0
EPSILON = 1e-4
STEP = 0.005
INK = "#20201E"
GRAY = "#877F77"
GRID = "#EDE7E0"
CORAL = "#EE8168"
PERIWINKLE = "#5A73CE"
LIME = "#8FB043"

TEAL = "#1F9A8E"
ROSE = "#D75567"

def integrate_error(initial, duration, step=STEP):
    count = int(round(duration / step))
    assert np.isclose(count * step, duration)
    matrix = np.diag([-GROWTH, RELAXATION])
    values = np.empty((count + 1, 2))
    values[0] = initial
    for index in range(count):
        current = values[index]
        first = matrix @ current
        second = matrix @ (current + step * first / 2)
        third = matrix @ (current + step * second / 2)
        fourth = matrix @ (current + step * third)
        values[index + 1] = current + step * (first + 2 * second + 2 * third + fourth) / 6
    return np.linspace(0, duration, count + 1), values

def segmented_error(interval, reset):
    times = []
    values = []
    initial = np.array([0.0, EPSILON])
    for segment in range(int(round(HORIZON / interval))):
        if reset:
            initial = np.array([0.0, EPSILON])
        local_times, local_values = integrate_error(initial, interval)
        times.extend(local_times + segment * interval)
        values.extend(local_values[:, 1])
        initial = local_values[-1].copy()
    return np.array(times), np.array(values)

def prepare(axis):
    axis.grid(alpha=0.9)
    axis.legend(loc="lower left", bbox_to_anchor=(0, 1.02),
                frameon=False, fontsize=9, borderaxespad=0)

def save(figure, name, description):
    figure.savefig(FIGURES / f"{name}.svg", bbox_inches="tight", transparent=True, metadata={"Date": None, "Description": description})
    figure.savefig(FIGURES / f"{name}.pdf", bbox_inches="tight", metadata={"CreationDate": None, "ModDate": None})
    figure.savefig(FIGURES / f"{name}.png", dpi=150, bbox_inches="tight")
    plt.close(figure)

durations = np.linspace(0, HORIZON, 321)
figure, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.8, 4.1), layout="constrained")
axes[0].semilogy(durations, np.exp(GROWTH * durations), color=CORAL, lw=2.4,
                 label="Forward expansion")
axes[0].semilogy(durations, np.exp(-GROWTH * durations), color=PERIWINKLE,
                 lw=2.4, ls="--", label="Reverse contraction")
axes[1].semilogy(durations, np.exp(-RELAXATION * durations), color=TEAL,
                 lw=2.4, label="Forward relaxation")
axes[1].semilogy(durations, np.exp(RELAXATION * durations), color=ROSE,
                 lw=2.4, ls="--", label="Reverse amplification")
for axis in axes:
    axis.set(xlabel="Elapsed integration time (normalized)", ylabel="Perturbation gain")
    prepare(axis)
figure.suptitle("Time reversal contracts one mode and amplifies the other\n"
               r"Diagonal example: $r=0.2,\ k=2$", fontsize=12)
save(figure, "fig-modes", "Exact gains for a diagonal two-mode toy model, not measured plant rates.")

times, errors = integrate_error(np.array([0.0, EPSILON]), HORIZON)
free_times, free_errors = segmented_error(0.5, reset=False)
np.testing.assert_allclose(free_errors[-1], errors[-1, 1], rtol=0, atol=0)
rows = []
for interval in (1.0, 0.5, 0.25):
    anchor_times, anchor_errors = segmented_error(interval, reset=True)
    peak = float(anchor_errors.max())
    expected = EPSILON * np.exp(RELAXATION * interval)
    np.testing.assert_allclose(peak, expected, rtol=2e-9)
    rows.append({"interval": interval, "gain": float(np.exp(RELAXATION * interval)),
                 "anchored_peak_error": peak})

anchor_times, anchor_errors = segmented_error(0.5, reset=True)
figure, axis = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.4, 4.5), layout="constrained")
axis.semilogy(times, errors[:, 1], color=ROSE, lw=2.8, label="One long reverse rollout")
axis.semilogy(free_times, free_errors, color=PERIWINKLE, lw=2.2, ls="--",
              label="Split rollout, no re-anchoring (same curve)")
axis.semilogy(anchor_times, anchor_errors, color=TEAL, lw=2.2,
              label="Reset to a noisy anchor every 0.5 time units")
axis.set(xlabel="Time traveled backward (normalized)", ylabel="Absolute error in scaled state units",
         xlim=(0, HORIZON), ylim=(7e-5, 0.6))
prepare(axis)
figure.suptitle("Independent anchors limit local error; splitting alone does not\n"
               r"Known anchor error: $\epsilon=10^{-4}$", fontsize=12)
save(figure, "fig-anchors", "RK4 propagation with identical time step. Anchored curve assumes bounded state error at every reset.")

boundaries = np.arange(0, HORIZON + 0.25, 0.5)
rate_bias = 0.05
relative_global_error = np.exp(rate_bias * boundaries) - 1
relative_local_error = np.expm1(rate_bias * 0.5)
truth = np.exp(-RELAXATION * (HORIZON - boundaries))
local_predictions = np.exp((RELAXATION + rate_bias) * 0.5) * truth[:-1]
np.testing.assert_allclose((local_predictions - truth[1:]) / truth[1:], relative_local_error)
full_rollout = truth[0] * np.exp((RELAXATION + rate_bias) * boundaries)
np.testing.assert_allclose((full_rollout - truth) / truth, relative_global_error, atol=1e-14)
figure, axis = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.4, 4.2), layout="constrained")
axis.plot(boundaries, 100 * relative_global_error, "o-", color=ROSE, lw=2.4,
          markeredgecolor="white", markeredgewidth=1.3,
          label="Relative error of one full rollout")
axis.plot(boundaries[1:], np.full(len(boundaries) - 1, 100 * relative_local_error),
          "s--", color=TEAL, lw=2.2, markeredgecolor="white", markeredgewidth=1.3,
          label="Relative mismatch of each cached-boundary step")
axis.set(xlabel="Time traveled backward (normalized)", ylabel="Relative state mismatch (%)",
         xlim=(0, HORIZON), ylim=(0, 24))
prepare(axis)
figure.suptitle("Small local defects can hide a larger full-rollout error\n"
               r"Exact rate-bias example: $k=2,\ \widehat{k}=2.05$", fontsize=12)
save(figure, "fig-defects", "Exact scalar rate-bias example with true rate 2 and interval 0.5. This is not a measured GrowFlow cache error.")

step_checks = []
for step in (0.02, 0.01, 0.005, 0.0025):
    _, numerical = integrate_error(np.array([0.0, EPSILON]), HORIZON, step=step)
    exact = EPSILON * np.exp(RELAXATION * HORIZON)
    step_checks.append({"step": step, "relative_error": float(abs(numerical[-1, 1] / exact - 1))})
assert step_checks[-1]["relative_error"] < 1e-10
for previous, refined in zip(step_checks, step_checks[1:]):
    assert refined["relative_error"] < previous["relative_error"] / 14

results = {
    "parameters": {"expansion_rate": GROWTH, "relaxation_rate": RELAXATION,
                   "horizon": HORIZON, "anchor_error": EPSILON, "rk4_step": STEP},
    "reverse_worst_gain": float(np.exp(RELAXATION * HORIZON)),
    "reverse_expansion_mode_gain": float(np.exp(-GROWTH * HORIZON)),
    "long_rollout_error": float(errors[-1, 1]),
    "unanchored_split_error": float(free_errors[-1]),
    "anchored_results": rows,
    "model_rate_bias": rate_bias,
    "local_relative_defect": float(relative_local_error),
    "full_relative_defect": float(relative_global_error[-1]),
    "rk4_refinement": step_checks,
}
(ROOT / "analysis" / "results.json").write_text(json.dumps(results, indent=2) + "\n")
print(json.dumps(results, indent=2))
