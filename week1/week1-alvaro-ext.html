Week 1 — Extension: The space and the weight of genetic information

Author: Álvaro Bátrez · Week 1 (lectures 1–2, 2026-09-01/03) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: lecture 2 left two numbers open — how much space the genome fills, and whether a messenger RNA is bigger than the protein it describes — and both answers say the same thing: genetic information takes little space but is heavy to copy, so the cell reuses every copy about 150 times to make it cheap.

The point I am adding

Lecture 2 mentioned, almost in passing, that the DNA of an E. coli bacterium fills only 0.5% of the cell, and its first exercise asked: which is bigger, an mRNA or the protein it encodes? I answer both questions here. Neither answer needs any deep biology — just the size of a DNA thread, the weight of the code's letters, and division. And the two answers turn out to make one point together: the cell's information is cheap in space, expensive in weight, and the cell fixes that by reuse.

Work it

Question 1 — how much space does the genome take? DNA is a thread 2 nanometres (millionths of a millimetre) thick; each "letter" — one base pair — advances the thread 0.34 nm. So one letter occupies a tiny cylinder of π × 1² × 0.34 ≈ 1.07 nm³. That single number answers the question for anything alive, once you know the genome size and the container. An E. coli: 4.6 million letters → a thread 1.5 mm long rolled into 0.005 µm³ of thread-volume, inside a cell of 1 µm³ (a millionth of a cubic millimetre) → 0.5%, exactly the lecture's number. Then I checked three more organisms, same arithmetic:

From a bacterium to a salamander the genome grows 34,000 times, but the space it fills stays under a few percent (Figure 2). Why so similar? Not because DNA packs better in big cells — because the container grows with the content: species with bigger genomes have proportionally bigger nuclei [5]. The salamander shows what that costs: it has 25 times more DNA than a human, roughly the same number of genes, and pays for it with bigger cells and slower development [6]. And one more thing the lecture mentioned without dwelling on: the proteins that wrap DNA (histones) weigh almost as much as the DNA itself — the shelf costs as much as the books.

Question 2 — which is bigger, the mRNA or the protein? A protein is a chain of small pieces (amino acids); the exercise's protein has 300 pieces. Weight scale for molecules: the dalton (Da); a water molecule is 18 Da. One amino-acid piece is about 110 Da, so the protein weighs 300 × 110 ≈ 33,000 Da. To build it, the cell first makes a copy of the gene — the messenger RNA (mRNA) — which spells the recipe in letters of about 330 Da each. The recipe needs three letters per piece: 3 × 330 = 990 Da just to name one piece of 110 Da. With the full message (900 coding letters plus ~100 extra at the ends), the copy weighs about 330,000 Da — ten times the protein it describes. That ratio is not a coincidence of this gene; it is the genetic code itself (three heavy letters per light piece), so no gene can escape it.

In size the story is the same (Figure 1). The protein folds into a ball about 4 nm wide. The mRNA is a thread 600 nm long that coils loosely into a ball about 28 nm across — some 300 times the protein's volume, though almost all of that is water; stripped of water, the RNA would fit in a ball just 8 nm wide. The machine that reads the message, the ribosome, is bigger than both and 70 times heavier than the protein.

So why doesn't the heavy copy bankrupt the cell? Because it is reused. A messenger has to be read about 10 times before it has produced its own weight in protein. In reality it is read far more: about ten ribosomes work on one message at the same time, each finishes a protein in 18 seconds (16.5 pieces per second, lecture 2), and the message survives about 5 minutes before decaying (also lecture 2). That comes to ~150 proteins per messenger. The numbers close from another side too: the cell holds ~3.3 million proteins and only ~3,000 messengers — about 1,000 proteins per message, exactly what 150 readings predict once you account for proteins living longer than messages. So each protein carries only ~6% of extra weight from its recipe. That is the exercise's "cost of a message against a machine": the recipe is heavy, the copying is not — information is expensive to make and cheap to reuse, and the cell reuses everything: the archive every generation, each messenger ~150 times, the reader for its whole life.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

The coil size is my roughest estimate. The 28 nm ball assumes simple physics for a floppy thread; real mRNAs fold, so the honest range is 20–40 nm. The weight ratio — the main claim — does not depend on it at all.

The "same few percent" is approximate, and one input is guessed. The salamander's nucleus size I estimated from its huge red blood cells (good to a factor of two), and I compared a bacterium's whole cell with eukaryotes' nuclei, which is not quite the same container.

150 readings is an average that hides the interesting part. Some messages are read only ~10 times — sitting right at break-even — and others thousands of times. That spread is how the cell regulates its genes, and it is exactly where lectures 4 and 9 will pick up.

Weight is not what the cell really pays. It pays in energy and time. I used weight because it is the one any classmate can re-check with a calculator in one line.

The code behind every number and both figures 
import math

# ---- Question 1: space ----
v_bp = math.pi * (1.0e-9)**2 * 0.34e-9      # volume of one base pair: cylinder, d = 2 nm
for name, bp, vol in [("E. coli", 4.6e6, 1.0), ("yeast", 1.21e7, 2.9),
                      ("human, 2 copies", 6.4e9, 300.0),
                      ("salamander, 2 copies", 2*80*0.978e9, 5000.0)]:
    print(f"{name:20s} {100*bp*v_bp*1e18/vol:5.2f}% of {vol} um^3")
print("histones vs DNA on 200 letters:", 109e3 / (200*660))   # 0.83: shelf = books

# ---- Question 2: weight and size ----
prot_da = 300 * 110                          # the protein
rna_nt  = 3*300 + 100                        # 3 letters per piece + ends
rna_da  = rna_nt * 330                       # the messenger
print("mRNA / protein =", rna_da/prot_da)    # 10.0
Rg = math.sqrt((rna_nt*0.6)*1.0/3)           # floppy-thread coil radius, nm
print("coil diameter ~", 2*Rg, "nm; protein ball ~ 4.3 nm")

# ---- reuse: how many proteins per messenger ----
trips = (10 / (300/16.5)) * (5*60)           # 10 ribosomes, 18 s each, 5 min lifetime
print("readings per mRNA ~", trips)          # ~165 (break-even: 10)
print("recipe overhead per protein =", 100*rna_da/trips/prot_da, "%")
print("check: cell holds", 3.3e6, "proteins /", 3e3, "mRNAs =", 3.3e6/3e3,
      "proteins per message")
