Week 1 — Why Stronger Binding Does Not Always Make a Promoter More Sensitive

Author: Lang Kehan · Week 1 (2026-09-07) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: A promoter distinguishes two transcription-factor concentrations best when its response midpoint is centered on that range; stronger binding can instead saturate the promoter, while cooperative binding can sharpen the distinction.

The point I am adding

Lecture 2 warns that a Hill function is not the rate law of one elementary reaction: it compresses several molecular steps into an effective response.1 That warning raises a useful design question. If a transcription factor binds its promoter more tightly, does the promoter always become a better sensor of that factor?

No. A promoter that is almost always occupied cannot tell a high concentration from a slightly lower one, just as a promoter that is almost always empty cannot do so. The useful question is therefore not “how strong should binding be?” in isolation, but “which response midpoint gives the greatest contrast over the concentration range the cell needs to distinguish?” I answer that question first for one binding site, then ask what an effective Hill coefficient changes.

Work it

From concentration to occupancy contrast

For one equilibrium binding site, the probability of promoter occupancy is the familiar Langmuir form

p(C) = C/(C + Kd).

Suppose a promoter must distinguish C1 = 10 nM from C2 = 20 nM. Its useful signal is not occupancy alone but the difference

Δp = p(C2) − p(C1) = Kd(C2 − C1)/[(C1 + Kd)(C2 + Kd)].

Differentiating this expression with respect to Kd gives one maximum:

Kd* = √(C1C2) = √(10 × 20) nM ≈ 14 nM.

This is not Lecture 1's geometric-mean guessing rule. Under this deliberately simple equilibrium model, it is an exact optimum for response contrast. At Kd = 1 nM, both inputs nearly saturate the promoter (0.91 versus 0.95 occupancy), so Δp = 0.04. At Kd = 100 nM, both inputs leave it mostly empty (0.09 versus 0.17), so Δp = 0.08. At the intermediate optimum, occupancy changes from 0.41 to 0.59, giving the larger Δp ≈ 0.17.

Cooperativity sharpens a matched response

A real promoter may use several interacting factors or other hidden steps. A standard effective description is the Hill response2

p(C) = Ch/(Ch + Kh).

Here K is the half-occupancy concentration and h is an effective Hill coefficient. For h = 1, this reduces to the single-site expression above. For h > 1, it is not evidence for one elementary reaction involving h molecules at once; it is a compact description of a composite mechanism, exactly as Lecture 2 emphasizes.

The same contrast calculation is maximized at K = √(C1C2). With that matched value, the contrast for a twofold input change becomes

Δpmax = [2h/2 − 1]/[2h/2 + 1].

Thus cooperativity does not merely make binding “stronger.” When its midpoint is matched to the relevant input range, it can make a modest concentration fold-change produce a much clearer change in promoter occupancy. What I would argue against / where this breaks

The calculation optimizes only equilibrium occupancy contrast between two chosen concentrations. A living cell may instead need a broad dynamic range, rapid switching, low output noise, or a particular response to a changing signal. Those goals need not select the same affinity or Hill coefficient. In addition, h above one is an effective parameter: it can arise from cooperative DNA binding, promoter states, chromatin effects, or a downstream network, rather than a literal one-step reaction.3 The conclusion is therefore narrow but useful: “stronger binding” is not a self-contained design objective. Sensitivity depends on how the response curve is positioned and shaped relative to the signal the cell must read.
