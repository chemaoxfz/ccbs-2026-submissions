Week 1 — Extension: Why Does Hypoxia Inhibit the TCA Cycle?

Author: Runzi Yan · Week 1 (2026-09-07) · CCBS 2026

Why Does Low O₂ Stall Respiration?

Aerobic respiration is the main route by which most cells extract energy from organic carbon. Glycolysis and the TCA cycle break glucose down to CO2 while loading electrons onto NADH (and FADH2):

C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 10 NADH + 2 FADH2 + other products

NADH then hands those electrons to the respiratory chain complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Oxidative phosphorylation uses them to consume O2, make H2O and ATP, and regenerate NAD+:

NADH + H+ + ½ O2 → NAD+ + H2O

Although the TCA cycle itself does not directly require oxygen, it is nevertheless inhibited under hypoxic conditions. This is often attributed to the inability of NADH to be efficiently oxidized back to NAD⁺ under oxygen-limited conditions, resulting in insufficient NAD⁺ to sustain the TCA cycle. This raises a basic question: under normal conditions, how fast is NADH produced and consumed within a cell, and is there a substantial difference between the two rates?

The Calculation

Start from a measured CO2 production rate for a single, well-oxygenated HeLa cell, reported from a live-cell fluorescent CO2 probe, which gives JCO2 = 6.40 × 10−6 μg h−1 cell−1 [1], which converts to:

JCO2 ≈ 2.4 × 107 CO2 molecules s−1 cell−1

Taking glucose as the dominant carbon source and reading the stoichiometry off the equation above (6 CO2 produced alongside 10 NADH per glucose), the implied NADH production rate is:

JNADH, produced ≈ (10/6) × 2.4 × 107 ≈ 4 × 107 NADH molecules s−1 cell−1

On the consumption side, an independent, classic measurement of cellular O2 uptake under normoxia gives an O2 consumption rate of JO2 = 26.9 amol O2 cell−1 s−1 [2], which converts to:

JO2 ≈ 1.62 × 107 O2 molecules s−1 cell−1

So, production (~4 × 107 s−1) and consumption (~3.2 × 107 s−1) sit within roughly a factor of two of each other — under normoxia, the NADH books are close to balanced, not wildly mismatched.

So under normoxia the balance holds. The literature answer to "how low does O2 have to fall" comes from yeast respiration studies: O2 utilization stays high while dissolved O2 is above roughly 30 μM, but falls off once O2 drops below that [3]. Tumor microenvironments routinely sit well under 30 μM O2 [4] — which is exactly the regime where, by the numbers above, NADH production keeps running while its reoxidation route starts to choke, tipping the NADH/NAD+ balance this section computed.Then, the TCA cycle can be inhibited, and more NADH is diverted toward lactate fermentation.

Do Cells Only Downregulate the TCA Cycle Under Anaerobic Conditions?

This picture is too clean, and tumor biology is the place that shows why: the Warburg effect. Many cancer cells run high rates of aerobic glycolysis — fermenting glucose to lactate — even when O2 is plentiful, not only when it is scarce. So why would a well-oxygenated tumor cell still choose fermentation? This remains an open question.
