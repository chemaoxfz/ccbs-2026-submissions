WEEK 1 — Extension: What ML scaling laws have in common with biology

Author: Weihan Luo · Week 1 (2026-09-07) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: Biological scaling laws are often derived from physical constraints, while machine-learning scaling laws are fitted empirical trends; both are useful because they turn “make it bigger” into a quantitative prediction, but only one comes with a mechanism automatically attached.

The point I am adding

Lecture 1 uses scaling to estimate what changes when a physical system gets larger. A central example is diffusion: if a molecule must travel a distance x with diffusion coefficient D, the characteristic time is t ≈ x2/D. Doubling the distance therefore makes the time about four times longer. The exponent 2 is not just a curve fit; it follows from the units and from how random motion accumulates.

Large language models also show scaling laws. A simplified form is L(N) ≈ L∞ + aN−α, where N is model size and L is prediction loss. Increasing N usually lowers the excess loss, but with diminishing returns. Kaplan and colleagues measured this relationship across many language-model experiments and found approximate power laws for model size, data size and training compute.

These laws are related at the level of reasoning: both ask how an output changes when an input is multiplied. On a log–log plot, a power law becomes a straight line whose slope is the exponent. That makes exponents easy to compare. But the analogy has a boundary. Diffusion’s exponent can be derived before collecting new data, whereas α in an ML scaling law must be estimated from experiments and may change when the architecture, data distribution, optimizer or evaluation task changes.

Work it

The figure normalizes both examples at a one-times baseline. The blue curve is the diffusion time when distance grows from 1× to 16×. The red curve is the excess ML loss predicted by a toy exponent α = 0.3. They are not plotted in the same physical units; normalization only lets us compare the shape of the scaling.

This comparison changes how I read an ML scaling plot. A straight line on log–log axes is evidence for a useful range of behavior, not proof that the same exponent will hold forever. It is more like a calibration curve than a conservation law. The biological calculation tells us why the curve has its exponent; the ML experiment tells us that an exponent is a good approximation in the range that was measured.

Reproduce the figure 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

scale = 2.0 ** np.arange(5)
diffusion_time = scale ** 2
excess_loss = scale ** (-0.3)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8, 3))
axes[0].loglog(scale, diffusion_time, "o-")
axes[0].set_title("Diffusion")
axes[1].loglog(scale, excess_loss, "o-", color="tomato")
axes[1].set_title("ML scaling toy law")
for axis in axes:
    axis.set_xlabel("input scale")
    axis.set_ylabel("normalized output")
    axis.grid(True, which="both", alpha=0.25)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

What I would argue against / where this breaks

It would be misleading to say that ML scaling laws are simply biological scaling laws applied to computers. A physical law must respect units and usually survives a change of measurement convention. Model size, data size and compute are choices in an experiment, and their fitted exponents can depend on what is held fixed. A larger model can also fail to help if the data are limited, noisy or mismatched to the task.

Even the ML literature revises its advice. Kaplan et al. emphasized one allocation of model size, data and compute; later work by Hoffmann and colleagues showed that many large language models were undertrained and that compute-optimal training requires scaling data along with model size. The useful extension is therefore not “all scaling laws are the same.” It is: before trusting a power law, ask whether its exponent comes from a mechanism or from a finite experimental regime.
