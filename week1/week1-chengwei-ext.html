Week 1 Extension: What Is the Radius of a DNA Target?

Chengwei Jin · Week 1 · CCBS 2026

One sentence: The familiar 108 M−1s−1 estimate for three-dimensional capture of the lac operator is a useful anchor, but not a geometry-free bound: its 1 nm target radius is an assumed effective capture scale, not a length derived from “20 base pairs.”

A striking story in Lecture 2 begins with a rate that looks impossible. A simple three-dimensional estimate gives about 1.2 × 108 M−1s−1, while LacI was reported to find its operator much faster. The gap motivates facilitated diffusion: LacI can bind nonspecific DNA first, then slide or hop until it finds the operator. The mechanism is real and important. My narrower question is whether both numbers in this comparison have been specified carefully enough.

1. Correct the experimental anchor first

The lecture exposition describes the 1970 measurement as greater than 1010 M−1s−1 and therefore one hundred times its 1.2 × 108 benchmark.1 The original paper says something slightly different. Riggs, Bourgeois and Cohn report an association constant of

kexp = 7 × 109 M−1s−1

in their standard buffer.2 Relative to the stated benchmark, the measured rate is

(7 × 109) / (1.2 × 108) = 58.
A correction to the record. The original measurement is not greater than 1010, and the ratio is not literally 100. “Roughly two orders of magnitude” remains a fair order-of-magnitude description. The correction changes the size of the anomaly, not its existence.

This matters methodologically. A surprising result should make us inspect the measurement and the bound separately. Correcting the numerator is the first step. The denominator hides a more interesting geometric assumption.

2. Recovering the Berg–Purcell result

Start with a perfectly absorbing sphere of radius a in a bath whose far-field concentration is c∞. At steady state the concentration outside the sphere obeys ∇²c = 0, with c(a) = 0 and c(∞) = c∞. The radial solution is c(r) = c∞(1 − a/r). Integrating the diffusive flux over the surface gives the Smoluchowski rate:

J = 4πDa c∞,    so    ksphere = 4πDa.

Berg and Purcell then considered a mostly reflecting cell carrying N small, absorbing receptor patches. Each patch has radius s, while the cell has radius a. Their capture probability for a molecule that reaches the cell is Ns/(Ns + πa).3 For one patch,

k = 4πDa · s/(s + πa)   →   4Ds   when s ≪ a.

The limiting result is memorable because a disappears. A tiny target captures according to its own linear size, not its area. With D = 50 μm²/s and s = 1 nm, conversion from m³/s to M−1s−1 gives

4DsNA × 10³ = 1.20 × 108 M−1s−1.

The arithmetic is sound. The question is what physical object each symbol represents when this cell-receptor model is transferred to LacI and DNA.

3. Twenty base pairs do not determine a capture radius

A B-form DNA segment rises about 0.34 nm per base pair and is about 2 nm across. Thus a 20 bp operator is roughly 6.8 nm long and 2 nm wide.1 Neither number is automatically s. The operator length describes a molecular structure. The capture radius describes the boundary condition seen by a diffusing protein.

Successful recognition may require LacI to approach the correct groove, orientation and sequence. Those requirements can make the effective absorbing target smaller than the visible DNA segment. Electrostatic attraction and nonspecific DNA binding can make the effective target larger. Equal physical area is not enough either. Diffusive capture depends on shape and linear dimensions, so replacing a DNA cylinder by an equal-area disc is an exploratory analogy, not a corrected bound.

The hidden assumption. “A 20 bp operator” does not imply “s = 1 nm.” The 1 nm value is defensible as a local transverse molecular scale, but it must be labelled an assumed effective capture radius.

4. Does that ambiguity destroy the anomaly?

It does not, at least within the same small-patch relation. I varied the assumed s while keeping the classroom value D = 50 μm²/s. This is a sensitivity analysis, not a claim that every value on the axis is geometrically valid.

The calculation behind the figure is short enough to inspect:

NA = 6.02214076e23
D = 50e-12                 # 50 μm²/s in m²/s
def molar_rate(s_nm):
    return 4 * D * (s_nm * 1e-9) * 1e3 * NA

k_1nm = molar_rate(1)      # 1.20e8 M⁻¹ s⁻¹
s_needed = 7e9 / k_1nm     # 58.1 nm

At s = 4 nm, already several times the stated patch radius, the model gives 4.8 × 108 M−1s−1. Riggs remains about fifteen-fold faster. If the formula is extended mechanically until it meets the experiment, it asks for an effective target radius of about 58 nm. That value is not a new estimate of the operator. It is a diagnostic: the naive three-dimensional picture would need an antenna far larger than the sequence itself.

5. What the comparison can and cannot establish

The experiment itself already points beyond unguided diffusion. Riggs and colleagues found that increasing ionic strength reduced association, implicating electrostatic attraction.2 Nonspecific DNA binding can enlarge the antenna further. A protein can land away from the operator, then reach it by sliding, hopping or reassociation. Halford and Marko review evidence for all of these routes and warn against treating continuous one-dimensional sliding as universal.4

Therefore the comparison does not uniquely prove one mechanism. It does show where the simple model runs out of explanatory capacity. Geometry contributes uncertainty to the size of the gap, while electrostatics and mixed-dimensional search supply plausible missing physics.

Conclusion: The anomaly survives, but its magnitude is model-dependent. The useful research habit is to keep a numerical anchor, expose the geometry that produced it, and test whether the biological conclusion survives when the hidden parameter moves.
