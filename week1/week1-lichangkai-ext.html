Week 1 — Extension: When does E. coli stop being well mixed?

Author: Li Changkai · Week 1 (lectures 1–2, 2026-09) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: Lecture 2 licenses a well-mixed model for a 1-µm bacterium from its short diffusion time; comparing that time directly with a diffusion-limited binding time gives a sharper condition, and for two ~2-nm proteins the boundary is only ≈33 nM — inside the concentration range of low-copy regulatory proteins — although real binding is usually slower than this worst-case ceiling.

The point I am adding

Lecture 2 makes two statements that are individually clear: a protein crosses a 1-µm E. coli in roughly a tenth of a second, so the cell is often treated as well mixed; and mass action requires independent encounter partners, which can fail if they are not well mixed [1]. I want to connect those statements quantitatively. “Mixing is fast” is not an absolute claim: it should mean mixing is faster than the reaction whose rate law assumes mixing.

For a binding step A + B → C, let the partner B have concentration C. I define a cell-scale reaction–diffusion ratio

Dacell = τmix / τbind = (L²/Drel) · (konC).

If Dacell ≪ 1, relative positions randomize before binding and the well-mixed approximation is comfortable. If Dacell ≳ 1, a molecule can be captured before it has explored the cell, so spatial organization has a chance to matter. This is a deliberately crude Damköhler-like test, not a theorem.

Work it

Step 1, use the same diffusion physics for both times. Elowitz et al. measured GFP diffusion in E. coli as 7.7 ± 2.5 µm²/s [2], the value also used in Lecture 2. For two similar freely diffusing proteins the relative coordinate diffuses with Drel = DA + DB ≈ 15.4 µm²/s. Across L = 1 µm,

τmix ≈ L²/Drel = 1/15.4 s ≈ 65 ms.

Step 2, push binding all the way to the diffusion limit. Lecture 2 derives the Smoluchowski encounter rate kon = 4πDrela [1]. For two proteins with radius ≈2 nm each, the contact distance is a ≈4 nm. Converting the swept volume to molar units gives

kon ≈ 4.7e+08 M−1s−1.

At 10 nM partner concentration, τbind ≈ 215 ms, so Dacell ≈ 0.30. At 100 nM, τbind falls to 21 ms, so Dacell ≈ 3.0. The ordering has reversed: the encounter can be faster than cell-scale mixing.

Step 3, solve for the boundary. Substituting the diffusion-limited rate into the ratio makes Drel cancel:

Dacell = 4πaL²NA(1000)C.

That cancellation is the result I did not expect. If crowding slows local encounters and cell-scale diffusion by the same factor, it does not move this particular boundary. Setting Dacell = 1 gives

C* = 1 / [4πaL²NA(1000)] ≈ 33 nM   (a = 4 nm, L = 1 µm).

Lecture 2 places transcription factors around 10–100 nM [1], so the boundary is not at an absurd concentration: it cuts through a biologically familiar regime. The cell-size dependence is even stronger, C* ∝ L−2: the same calculation gives about 132 nM at 0.5 µm but only 8.3 nM at 2 µm.

What the number means

The result does not overturn Lecture 2's “E. coli is well mixed” rule. It puts a condition on it. The calculation intentionally uses the fastest physically allowed unguided association. Lecture 2 immediately notes why real molecular recognition is usually slower: only a small surface patch may be reactive, orientation matters, and conformational steps can intervene [1]. Reducing kon by even 100-fold reduces Dacell by 100-fold; then a 100 nM partner gives Dacell ≈ 0.03 and the well-mixed model is safe again.

So I would phrase the extension this way: small size licenses well-mixed models only relative to a reaction timescale. For ordinary reaction-limited binding in a bacterium, diffusion wins easily. But a fast, high-concentration, near-diffusion-limited capture process can approach the spatial boundary even in a 1-µm cell. That is exactly where localization, tethering, membranes or source–sink geometry should stop being decorative details and enter the model.

reproduce.py — full calculation and Figure 1 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

NA = 6.02214076e23        # mol^-1
a = 4e-9                  # m; contact distance of two ~2-nm-radius proteins
D_single = 7.7            # um^2/s; Elowitz et al. (1999)
D_rel = 2 * D_single      # relative diffusion of two similar proteins

# 1-um cell: numerical check using the measured in-cell diffusion coefficient
tau_mix = 1.0**2 / D_rel
D_rel_m2s = D_rel * 1e-12
kon = 4*np.pi*D_rel_m2s*a*NA*1000  # M^-1 s^-1

for C_nM in [10, 100]:
    tau_bind = 1/(kon*C_nM*1e-9)
    print(C_nM, "nM:",
          "tau_mix =", tau_mix,
          "tau_bind =", tau_bind,
          "Da =", tau_mix/tau_bind)

# At the diffusion limit, D_rel cancels:
# Da = (L^2/D_rel) * (4*pi*D_rel*a*NA*1000*C)
#    = 4*pi*a*L^2*NA*1000*C
C_nM = np.logspace(0, 3, 300)

for L_um in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    L = L_um * 1e-6
    C_M = C_nM * 1e-9
    Da = 4*np.pi*a*L**2*NA*1000*C_M
    plt.plot(C_nM, Da, label=f"L = {L_um:g} µm")

plt.axhline(1, ls="--", label="Da = 1")
plt.xscale("log")
plt.yscale("log")
plt.xlabel("Partner concentration (nM)")
plt.ylabel("Da_cell = tau_mix / tau_bind")
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

L = 1e-6
Ccrit = 1/(4*np.pi*a*L**2*NA*1000)
print("critical concentration =", Ccrit*1e9, "nM")

What I would argue against / where this breaks

“Da ≳ 1 means mass action is wrong” is too strong. My ratio compares a global exploration time with a mean capture time. A spatially uniform population can still obey a useful mass-action law even when an individual encounter happens before one molecule literally visits every part of the cell. The ratio is therefore a warning light, not a proof of spatial failure.

The cancellation of D is conditional. It works because I used the same normal-diffusion coefficient for local encounter and cell-scale mixing and assumed a perfectly absorbing contact. Elowitz et al. themselves found that mobility depends on protein size, concentration and even small tags [2]. If the cytoplasm is anomalous on one scale, or if molecules bind transient obstacles, local and global mobility need not slow by the same factor and D will no longer cancel.

Localization can break the null model earlier. A membrane-bound partner, a tethered complex, or production from one pole violates the independence assumption directly. Lecture 2 already flags tethering, membrane confinement and poor mixing as reasons the [A][B] product can fail [1]. My calculation concerns the cleaner case: two freely diffusing soluble proteins.
