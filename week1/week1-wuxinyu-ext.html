Week 1 — Extension: When faster antibody capture makes shallower ADC delivery

Author: Wuxinyu · Week 1 (due 2026-09-07) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: In a minimal reaction–diffusion model, a tumor-targeting antibody penetrates only ℓ = √(D/k) before binding and removal, so faster capture can improve local engagement while starving cells farther from a vessel—and can therefore make ADC delivery shallower.

The point I am adding

High affinity sounds unconditionally desirable for an ADC: bind HER2 tightly, internalize, release payload. The missing spatial question is where that first binding happens. If molecules entering from a vessel are captured by the first antigen-rich cells they meet, high local engagement creates a low-concentration region behind them—the binding-site barrier.

My quantitative null model compresses binding, internalization and irreversible loss into one spatially uniform first-order sink k. It assumes one-dimensional tissue, constant diffusivity D, a fixed concentration at the vessel and steady state:

0 = Dd²C/dx² − kC   ⇒   C(x)/C0 = exp(−x/ℓ),   ℓ = √(D/k).

The parameter ℓ is a penetration length: after one ℓ the free concentration is 1/e of its vessel value. Doubling D increases ℓ only √2-fold; doubling the effective sink shortens ℓ by √2.

Work it

Bordeau, Yang and Balthasar tested this logic without altering trastuzumab itself. A short-lived anti-trastuzumab nanobody, 1HE, temporarily competed with HER2 binding. In SKOV3 xenografts, coadministration increased mean trastuzumab penetration from 41.30 ± 6.70 μm (mean ± SD) to 58.24 ± 5.40 μm (mean ± SD), and positive tumor area from 26.52 ± 8.11% to 43.32 ± 11.42% (mean ± SD). Penetration was measured for trastuzumab; T-DM1 was used in the subsequent efficacy experiment.

For an order-of-magnitude calculation, I deliberately equate those reported mean distances with ℓ and borrow D = 4.5 μm² s−1, measured by FRAP for nonbinding IgG in ovarian-cancer spheroids. Then k = D/ℓ²:

2.64 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹control effective sink
1.33 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹with transient inhibition
0.503×inferred sink ratio

The absolute k values depend on the borrowed D, but their ratio does not within this model: k1HE/kcontrol = (41.30/58.24)² = 0.503. Under the imposed exponential null model, the distance ratio corresponds to an effective-sink ratio of 0.503. This is a model translation of the observed 41% increase in distance, not a direct measurement of binding or internalization kinetics.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

The numerical k is an effective sink, not the HER2 association rate. Real binding is second order, saturable and reversible; internalization follows bound antibody; antigen varies cell to cell; delivery is pulsed by plasma pharmacokinetics; vessels are irregular; and ADC-bound payload can kill neighboring cells through a bystander effect. Most importantly, the experimental “penetration distance” is a mean image-analysis distance, not an exponential decay constant, and my D came from a different spheroid system. Thus, under the imposed exponential null model, the distance ratio corresponds to an effective-sink ratio of 0.503; that number should not be read as an experimentally measured twofold change in any single kinetic rate. The scaling explains why affinity, antigen density, dose and internalization must be designed together—not why weaker binding is always better.

Reproducible calculation embedded in this file
# Python 3; no packages
from math import log, exp
D = 4.5                    # um^2/s; stated cross-system assumption
ell0, ell1 = 41.30, 58.24 # um; Bordeau et al. means
k0, k1 = D/ell0**2, D/ell1**2
print(k0, k1, k1/k0)      # 0.002638, 0.001327, 0.503
print(log(2)/k0/60, log(2)/k1/60)  # 4.38 and 8.71 min
for x in range(0,121,20):
    print(x, exp(-x/ell0), exp(-x/ell1))
