Week 1 — Extension: the 30-cm jump runs out of power below a few grams

Author: Zheng Haobo · Week 1 (2026-09-07) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: the lecture's universal 30-cm jump is an energy argument; adding the power budget shows that direct muscle cannot deliver that jump below a crossover mass of a few grams, which is exactly where real animals switch to spring-loaded catapults.

The point I am adding

Lecture 1 argued that jump height does not depend on body mass. Usable muscle energy scales with muscle volume, muscle volume scales with body mass, and h = E/(mg) then gives h ∝ m0. A 30-cm jump should hold from rats to fleas, and it roughly does. The lecture added, almost in passing, that some insects store the energy in their shell like a spring. This essay takes that one sentence seriously and asks why the spring has to appear at all.

The energy argument has a gap: energy says nothing about how fast it is delivered. The push happens over a distance s, and under geometric similarity s = ℓ m1/3. The constant fits the big end: a 70 kg human extends its legs by about 0.4 m in take-off, so ℓ ≈ 0.1 m·kg−1/3. Reaching h = 30 cm fixes the take-off speed at v = √(2gh) ≈ 2.4 m/s whatever the mass. Delivering v over s at constant acceleration costs a peak specific power p = v3/(2s) = v3/(2ℓm1/3) ∝ m−1/3. Each tenfold drop in mass multiplies the watts per kilogram by about 2.2.

Muscle cannot follow that curve. Work-loop measurements on striated muscle give 76 W/kg as a controlled cyclic maximum (Josephson 1985), and the highest in-vivo estimates during take-off reach roughly 390-530 W/kg (Askew et al., 2001, 2002). Setting p equal to a burst ceiling P gives a crossover mass m* = (v3/2ℓP)3. For P = 500 W/kg, m* ≈ 2.9 g; across the plausible 250-1000 W/kg it spans 0.4-23 g.

Nature splits there, and the split is measured. The froghopper Philaenus spumarius (12.3 ± 0.7 mg) extends both hind legs in under 1 ms and leaves the ground at 2.8 ± 0.1 m/s on average; its paper reports peak outputs of 36-74 W per gram of jumping muscle, that is 3.6-7.4 × 104 W/kg, and states that no jumping muscle could supply this (Burrows 2003). My model asks 3.1 kW per kg of body mass for a 30-cm jump at that mass, and the paper's own energy and duration (49-100 μJ in about 1 ms over 12.3 mg) give a measured 4-8 kW/kg. Model and measurement agree without a fit. Above the band, a human jumps at 17 W/kg, fifteen to sixty times below any ceiling in that range, which is why we need no spring. At the boundary sits the 2 g locust, the mixed case.

So the spring is a power transformer, not an energy source. It charges over about 100 ms at muscle-grade power and releases in under 1 ms at two orders of magnitude above muscle's burst ceiling. The energy budget, and with it h ∝ m0, is untouched. What changes below m* is only who delivers the power, so the lecture's universal 30 cm survives with its mechanism swapped.

Work it

The script computes preq for the lecture's animals and the crossover mass; the figure plots it. Reading the figure: a human jumps thirty to sixty times below the plotted 0.5-1 kW/kg band. A flea insisting on 30 cm would need ≈9 kW/kg, an order of magnitude beyond any burst ceiling. At its measured 2.8 m/s take-off the froghopper needs ≈4.8 kW/kg, which is what its spring delivers. The crossover mass is my own derivation; every number in the table and figure reproduces from the embedded script.

import math
g, h = 9.8, 0.30                  # lecture's universal jump height (m)
v = math.sqrt(2 * g * h)          # take-off speed ~2.42 m/s, mass-independent
ell = 0.10                        # push distance per kg^(1/3)  (m/kg^(1/3))
P_burst = 500.0                   # burst specific-power ceiling (W/kg)
p_req  = lambda m: v**3 / (2*ell*m**(1/3))
m_star = (v**3 / (2*ell*P_burst))**3
for name, m in [("human",70),("cat",4),("rat",.3),
                ("locust",2e-3),("froghopper",12.3e-6),("flea",.45e-6)]:
    print(f"{name:11s} p_req = {p_req(m):8.0f} W/kg")
print(f"v = {v:.2f} m/s   m* = {m_star*1e3:.1f} g")
for P in (250, 500, 1000):        # crossover sensitivity, the softest input
    print(f"ceiling {P:5d} W/kg -> m* = {(v**3/(2*ell*P))**3*1e3:5.1f} g")
print("froghopper at its measured 2.8 m/s:",
      f"{2.8**3/(2*ell*12.3e-6**(1/3)):.0f} W/kg")

What I would argue against / where this breaks

  • m* scales as P−3, and the ceiling is the softest input: 250 W/kg gives m* ≈ 23 g, 1 kW/kg gives 0.4 g. The claim is an order of magnitude, not a line, and the flea and froghopper sit far enough below the whole band to be safe; a muscle-driven 1 g jumper at 30 cm would break it.
  • The 1/3 exponent in s = ℓm1/3 is assumed geometric similarity, not measured. Since m* ∝ ℓ−3, relatively longer legs rescue direct muscle: legs twice as long, eight times smaller m*.
  • The argument forces a spring only if the animal insists on ≈30 cm. It could instead jump lower, since p ∝ h3/2: 3 cm costs thirty times less power. That small animals keep h roughly constant and switch mechanism is a regularity this scaling explains but does not predict.
  • Drag is ignored. At flea scale it raises the required power further, which helps the argument, but it would also bend the h ∝ m0 universality at the small end.
