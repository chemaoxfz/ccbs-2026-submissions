Week 1 — Extension: Same Overall Reaction, Different Kinetics

Author: Sirui Cheng · Week 1 (2026-09-03) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: The overall chemical equation tells us what changes, but for a complex reaction it does not by itself tell us how fast the change occurs—the mechanism matters.

The point I am adding

The lectures distinguish an elementary reaction from a complex reaction built from multiple elementary steps. I want to push this distinction one step further: can two reactions have the same overall equation but different rates? Yes. The reason is that the overall equation hides the intermediate steps.

Consider the overall reaction A + B → P. If it is a single elementary bimolecular step, its rate law can be written directly as rate = k[A][B]. But suppose the same overall change happens through an intermediate:

A + B → I     and     I → P.

Here I is produced and then consumed, so it disappears when the two steps are added together. The net equation is still A + B → P, but product formation is now controlled by two successive processes. The rate of P formation initially starts at zero because no intermediate I exists yet. Thus, the same net equation does not uniquely determine the instantaneous rate law. This is why reaction mechanisms have to be checked against kinetic evidence rather than inferred from the balanced overall equation alone. [1–3]

Work it

I made a small numerical experiment. I started both systems with [A] = [B] = 1 and [I] = [P] = 0. For the elementary reference I used k = 1. For the two-step mechanism I used k1 = 1 and k2 = 0.2. The graph shows that the elementary model produces P immediately, whereas the two-step model first accumulates I and produces P more slowly.

Code used to make the figure 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

dt, t_end = 0.002, 10
k1, k2 = 1.0, 0.2

# Two-step mechanism: A + B -> I -> P
t = np.arange(0, t_end + dt, dt)
A = np.zeros_like(t); B = A.copy()
I = A.copy(); P = A.copy()
A[0] = B[0] = 1.0

for n in range(len(t) - 1):
    r1 = k1*A[n]*B[n]
    r2 = k2*I[n]
    A[n+1] = A[n] - dt*r1
    B[n+1] = B[n] - dt*r1
    I[n+1] = I[n] + dt*(r1-r2)
    P[n+1] = P[n] + dt*r2

# Elementary reference: A + B -> P
Ae = np.zeros_like(t); Be = np.zeros_like(t)
Pe = np.zeros_like(t)
Ae[0] = Be[0] = 1.0

for n in range(len(t) - 1):
    r = k1*Ae[n]*Be[n]
    Ae[n+1] = Ae[n] - dt*r
    Be[n+1] = Be[n] - dt*r
    Pe[n+1] = Pe[n] + dt*r

plt.plot(t, Pe, label="elementary: P")
plt.plot(t, P, label="two-step: P")
plt.plot(t, I, "--", label="two-step: I")
plt.xlabel("time"); plt.ylabel("concentration")
plt.legend(); plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The simulation is an illustrative comparison, not a fit to a particular chemical system. Its purpose is to make the mechanistic point visible.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

It would be too strong to conclude that a complex reaction can never have a simple rate law. Under special conditions, such as a fast pre-equilibrium or a clearly rate-limiting step, a multistep mechanism can reduce to a compact expression. In fact, deriving such an expression is one way to test whether a proposed mechanism is consistent with experiment. [1] The safer rule is therefore: do not derive an overall rate law from the net stoichiometric equation unless the reaction is known to be elementary.
