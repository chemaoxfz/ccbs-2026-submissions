WEEK 1 — Extension: The third stage of "height" limit: Geometry after storage

Author: Peng Su · Week 1 (2026-9-6) · CCBS 2026

Transmission ratio as a second, orthogonal lever past the power limit

The main claim

In Lecture 1 we watched a small jumper fall off the flat height line. The reason was introduced as a second constraint, the time. Fleas cannot accelerate to the ~3 m/s that an energy-balanced jump “should” give, because their legs only have microseconds of muscle time, so they use a resilin spring and a latch to release stored energy in a millisecond. The lecture presents that spring as the escape from the power wall. My extension is that this solves only one part of the problem. There is a second, orthogonal tool whose job is not to change the work through release in time, but to re-route force versus speed: the lever geometry, or more generally the four-bar linkage. It does not add energy or power. Instead, it redistributes a given mechanical input between force and speed. The spring controls when energy is released; the linkage controls how that motion is transmitted. Although not directly related to the "jump" height, mantis shrimp make this distinction especially clear because their strike combines both a spring-latch system and a four-bar linkage.

Rereading the power limit

Lecture 1 displays that the jump height may represent as

$$ h = \frac{f_m w}{\rho g} $$

where the size cancels. At this level, a small and a large animal could in principle reach similar jump heights. The problem appears when we ask how quickly that energy must be delivered. A jump of fixed height requires a roughly fixed take-off speed,

$$ v \sim \sqrt{2gh} $$

But a small animal has only a short distance over which to accelerate, roughly one body length, so the available time scales approximately as

$$ \tau \sim \frac{L}{v} $$

For a flea, this is only on the order of a millisecond. Muscle cannot shorten fast enough to deliver all of the required work directly within that time, which formed the time/power wall. The lecture's escape is the spring: store muscle work slowly, release it fast, so power is amplified while energy stays conserved. But after the spring releases its energy, another question remains:

How is the motion transmitted to the part of the animal that actually moves (the tip of the leg for jump)?

That is where geometry enters.

The third stage: transmission geometry

Consider the simplest case: a lever.
If the input point of the lever moves slowly through a short distance, the distal end can move through a larger distance in the same amount of time.
In a simplified description,

$$ v_{\mathrm{out}} = v_{\mathrm{in}} \times R $$

where \(R\) is a velocity ratio: the ratio of the output lever arm to the input lever arm. Energy conservation forces the force trade-off,

$$ v_{\mathrm{out}} / v_{\mathrm{in}} = F_{\mathrm{out}} / F_{\mathrm{in}} = R $$

This shows the key trade-off: more speed <=> less force, with no additional energy being created. This makes geometry fundamentally different from a spring. A spring stores work and releases it over a shorter time, increasing the available instantaneous power. While the lever decides how the available motion is divided between force and velocity.

A toy calculation: how much can geometry change tip speed?

Here I ask the extreme question: if a system only had a lever, how fast could the tip move in the release window? Using vout = vin* R with a muscle-driven input of order 1 m/s and a few-millisecond window (the flea's own release time). The table and figure below are the whole calculation, while the detailed input information is listed below

# v_out = v_in * R ; a_peak(acceleration) ~ v_out / t_release ;  P = F.v conserved
v_muscle = 1.0        # m/s of lever input (muscle-limited, ms release)
t_release = 2e-3      # s  (flea-order release window)
for R in (1, 2, 5, 10, 20):
    v_out = v_muscle * R
    a = v_out / t_release
    print(R, round(v_out,1), 'm/s', round(a,0), 'm/s^2', round(a/9.81,0), 'g')

At R=20, the toy model gives a tip speed of 20 m/s, close to the measured mantis-shrimp range of 14-23 m/s. Its estimated mean acceleration is about 10000 m/s², or ~10³ g, which roughly within one order of magnitude of the measured mantis-shrimp acceleration (6.6 * 10³ ~ 1.1 * 10⁴ g). (Patek et al. 2004). Compare the flea’s ~98 g from Lecture 1.

In this case, the simulated calculation shows that the spring is what allows the strike to finish in <1 ms, but the speed-ratio that dominates the number is the lever.

Reality beyond estimation

A real mantis shrimp, of course, does not operate as a pure lever, it pairs the four-bar geometry with a saddle-shaped spring and a latch, exactly because the spring handles the sub-millisecond timing that a lever cannot. So my claim is not “geometry replaces the spring”, instead, it is that the two are independent controls, and the lecture, by teaching only the spring, under-sells how much of the speed-up is geometry at the third stage. The shrimps and the trap-jaw ants are the proof that evolution turns both dials.

The weakest inputs in my little table: the 1 m/s muscle input is a loose order-of-magnitude, the 2 ms window is borrowed from the flea, and real four-bar linkages have a R that changes through the stroke rather than being a single number.

However, the test example above still indicates that even without adding energy, changing transmission geometry can move distal velocity by an order of magnitude.

In other words,elastic storage determines how rapidly energy becomes available; linkage geometry determines what force-speed output that energy produces.
