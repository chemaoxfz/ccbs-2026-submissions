Can an energy-consuming cycle change the accuracy–throughput frontier?

Equilibrium binding versus dissipative proofreading

CCBS · EST 5053 · extension mini-essay · Lectures 7–8 · Week 4 · 2026-09-28

The claim

An equilibrium binding baseline fixes selectivity through state free energies. A fuel-driven proofreading cycle can move the accuracy–throughput frontier only when it has a non-zero cycle affinity and state-selective rejection. Multiplying every rate by the same factor makes the reaction faster but does not change equilibrium selectivity.

1Problem

“More ATP” can mean more turnover, more discrimination, or both; these are different claims.

I compare a correct substrate R and an incorrect substrate W competing for the same enzyme. The word equilibrium below refers to the binding/occupancy baseline; product removal and fuel turnover are explicit non-equilibrium boundaries. The assay asks whether a fuel-coupled reset step adds selectivity rather than merely changing the clock.

2Thermodynamic claim and assumptions

  • R and W concentrations are held fixed and enzyme amount is fixed.
  • Every reversible transition has a reverse path. Fuel coupling obeys local detailed balance.
  • The fuel chemical potential is ΔμF = μF − μF′; cycle affinity is Acycle = ΔμF/(RT) = ln ∏k+/∏k−.
  • Accuracy is ε = JW/(JR+JW), and fuel cost is JF/JR.

A proofreading advantage is predicted only if W is more likely than R to visit a reset/rejection branch. Fuel itself is not a magic selectivity parameter.

3Minimal networks

The equilibrium baseline is

E + Si ⇌ Ci → E + Pi,   i ∈ {R,W}.

The dissipative network adds a reversible fuel-coupled transition and a rejection path:

Ci + F ⇌ Ci* + F′;   Ci* → Pi;   Ci* → E + Si + F′.

For a transparent numerical benchmark, use binding entry probabilities pR=0.476 and pW=0.0476. A non-selective equilibrium commit would preserve the binding error εeq=pW/(pR+pW)=0.0909. With fuel-driven, state-selective commit probabilities qR=0.889 and qW=0.050, the illustrative fluxes are JR=0.4233 and JW=0.00238, giving ε=0.00559 and 1.24 fuel turnovers per correct product. These values are illustrative, not a fit to a named enzyme.

4Computational assay and controls

For each parameter point, solve the continuous-time Markov network to steady state and then simulate at least 1,000 finite-copy trajectories. Scan the discrimination ratio, ΔμF, rejection dwell time and commit rates. Compare at both fixed total throughput and fixed fuel consumption.

5Illustrative result

The decisive comparison is not “ATP makes product faster.” It is whether, at a matched throughput, ε is lower and whether the reduction is paid for by a measurable fuel flux. A non-zero affinity without selective rejection should not move the frontier.

6Falsification, limits and next test

Falsifiable prediction

At ΔμF=0, with no reset branch, or with identical R/W reset probabilities, the accuracy–throughput curve must overlap the equilibrium/rate-scaling baseline within numerical uncertainty. If the model predicts improved accuracy under those controls, its reverse paths or detailed-balance bookkeeping are wrong.

The network is a minimal proofreading model; it omits ribosome conformational states, crowding, multiple fuels and proteome allocation. Finite trajectories have transient and boundary effects. A future cell-free assay could use purified enzyme, paired correct/wrong substrates and an ATP/GTP regeneration system, measuring products and fuel consumption by LC–MS or calibrated fluorescence. That experiment is proposed, not performed.
