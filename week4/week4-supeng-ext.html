Week 4 Extension: A steep binding curve is paid for by occupancy fluctuations

Author: Peng Su · Week 4 (2026-09-28) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: For any equilibrium binding model whose state weight scales as cn, the response of the mean number of bound ligands to log concentration is exactly the variance in that number, so cooperativity steepens a “dose response” curve by moving probability toward more widely separated occupancy states.

What makes an equilibrum binding curve steep?

Lecture 7 built equilibrium probabilities from molecular states and weights. Lecture 8 then used those probabilities to calculate activity and showed how the winning state can set the apparent Hill degree. Took both results as background, one narrower question was asked: what is a steep equilibrium response physically made of? And the answer may not merely depend on “a large exponent.” At equilibrium, the steepness of the mean response is exactly tied to fluctuations across the allowed binding states.

Consider one receptor, promoter or allosteric complex with equilibrium states indexed by i. State i contains ni bound ligands. At controlled free-ligand concentration c, write its weight as

wi(c) = ai(c/c0)ni,   pi = wi/Z,   Z = Σiwi.

The constants ai contain binding energies, degeneracies and interactions that independent from the concentration. The exponent counts how many ligands the state draws from the controlled reservoir. Differentiating a normalized weight with respect to log concentration gives

dpi/d ln c = (ni − ⟨n⟩)pi.

Multiplying by ni and summing produces the extension in one line:

d⟨n⟩/d ln c = ⟨n2⟩ − ⟨n⟩2 = Var(n).

This is a static fluctuation–response relation. “Fluctuation” here means variation across an equilibrium ensemble of molecular states, not measurement error and not necessarily time-dependent gene-expression noise. If every likely state carries nearly the same number of ligands, Var(n) is small and the mean occupancy cannot change sharply. A steep transition requires substantial probability on states with different ligand counts.

A four site comparison: same mean, fourfold different steepness

Take a four-site receptor and define x = c/K. First suppose that the four sites bind independently. Its binding polynomial is Zind = (1 + x)4, and the fractional occupancy is θind = x/(1 + x). At x = 1, the number bound is binomial: the probabilities for n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 are (1, 4, 6, 4, 1)/16. Thus ⟨n⟩ = 2 and Var(n) = 1.

Now take an idealized concerted receptor that occupies only the empty and fully bound states. Its binding polynomial is Zall = 1 + x4, and θall = x4/(1 + x4). At the same midpoint, x = 1, half the receptors have n = 0 and half have n = 4. The mean is still 2, but the variance is now 4.

Because θ = ⟨n⟩/4, the identity predicts dθ/d ln x = Var(n)/4. The independent receptor therefore has midpoint slope 1/4, whereas the concerted receptor has slope 1: exactly four times steeper. The usual Hill-plot slope at half occupancy, [θ(1−θ)]−1dθ/d ln x, is correspondingly 1 and 4. Figure 1 shows why. Both systems have the same mean occupancy at the midpoint, but the concerted model puts probability at opposite ends rather than near the mean.

What the identity does and does not guarntee

I would argue against the statement that a steep mean response automatically makes a better sensor. The identity says that equilibrium gain and equilibrium occupancy variance rise together; it does not say how quickly independent samples are obtained. Positive cooperativity can also slow collective switching, so a cell may see a large instantaneous variance but only a few statistically independent transitions during its decision time. Diffusive arrival noise supplies another limit that cooperativity cannot remove. A sharp mean curve is therefore an amplifier, not by itself proof of precise concentration measurement.

The derivation also needs a controlled free concentration and equilibrium-compatible weights proportional to cn. With a finite ligand pool, changing total ligand also changes the free concentration through conservation, so d⟨n⟩/d ln qligand is not simply Var(n). In a driven receptor with broken detailed balance, a stationary distribution may exist but need not be generated by one binding polynomial, so the identity is not guaranteed. Finally, an experimental output may depend on state-specific activities rather than on ligand number alone. The fluctuation relation constrains occupancy; the activity map can sharpen, flatten or even invert the observed output.
