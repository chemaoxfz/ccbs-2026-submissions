When does a Hill curve stop being a Hill curve?

A finite binding pool can move a response threshold from affinity to stoichiometry.

Extension mini-essay · liyibing · Lecture 8 · CCBS 2026

1The claim

Lecture 8 replaces an assumed Hill law with explicit binding, conservation and activity.1 I push that idea one step further by asking when the familiar one-site curve remains accurate if the experimentally controlled input is total ligand rather than free ligand.

The reservoir Hill curve is reliable only when binding removes a negligible fraction of the ligand. Once the receptor pool is comparable with its dissociation constant, total ligand is no longer free ligand; at strong binding, the response turns near the stoichiometric point qS ≈ qE instead of qS ≈ Kd.

2The finite-pool model

For one binding pair, let qE and qS be receptor and ligand totals, and let C be the complex. Fast equilibrium gives three equations:

qE = E + C,   qS = S + C,   KdC = ES.

Substitution gives a quadratic. Its physical root is

C = 2qEqS / [qE+qS+Kd+√((qE+qS+Kd)2−4qEqS)].

If qE is negligible, then S ≈ qS and the bound fraction reduces to qS/(Kd+qS). If not, the conservation equations must be retained. This is the same finite-pool mechanism that underlies molecular titration and ultrasensitivity without cooperative binding.2

3My calculation

The calculation supplies a practical check. In this model, using total ligand in the reservoir formula stays within five percentage points of the exact bound fraction for every ligand total only when

qE/Kd ≲ 0.36.
