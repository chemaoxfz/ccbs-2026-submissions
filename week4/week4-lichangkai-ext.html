Week 4 Extension: A Delay Cannot Be an Equilibrium Property

Author: Li Changkai · CCBS 2026 Fall · Week 4 · Lectures 7–8

Central claim. Kinetic proofreading can outperform equilibrium recognition because a fuel-driven step creates a directional delay. In the course's binding-catalysis framework, this step belongs to the driven catalytic layer rather than the equilibrium binding layer.

1. The Point I Am Adding

Lecture 7 distinguishes stationarity from detailed balance: at equilibrium, each reversible edge has equal forward and reverse probability flux. Lecture 8 then classifies fast, undriven reversible transitions as the binding layer, while driven or chemistry-changing steps belong to catalysis.

Kinetic proofreading is a useful boundary test for this distinction. A reversible binding equilibrium can prefer the correct substrate, but equilibrium binding alone cannot sustain a directional, fuel-consuming cycle that provides additional opportunities for incorrect substrates to dissociate.

Hopfield's central idea was to couple an intermediate transition to a fuel-consuming reaction, such as nucleotide-triphosphate hydrolysis. This driven transition breaks detailed balance and allows additional discrimination beyond what a single equilibrium recognition step can provide. The key is not merely waiting longer; it is using a nonequilibrium reaction network to make the waiting period selective.

2. Work It: A Minimal Quantitative Model

Consider a simplified model in which a correct substrate (C) or wrong substrate (W) has already bound. Product formation occurs at a common rate \(k_p\), while dissociation occurs at substrate-specific rates \(k_{\mathrm{off,C}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{off,W}}\).

For two competing exponential processes, the probability that product forms before dissociation is

\[ P_i = \frac{k_p}{k_p+k_{\mathrm{off},i}} \]

Assuming equal incoming amounts of correct and wrong substrates, the wrong-to-correct product ratio for one selection stage is

\[ f_1 = \frac{P_W}{P_C} = \frac{k_p+k_{\mathrm{off,C}}} {k_p+k_{\mathrm{off,W}}}. \]

Choose illustrative rates \(k_{\mathrm{off,C}}=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{off,W}}=10\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). These values are chosen to demonstrate the model and are not fitted ribosome measurements.

At \(k_p=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\), the correct complex passes with probability \(1/2\), whereas the wrong complex passes with probability \(1/11\). Therefore,

\[ f_1 = \frac{1/11}{1/2} = \frac{2}{11} \approx 0.1818. \]

In an idealized model with two independent, identical filters, the wrong-to-correct ratio becomes

\[ f_2=f_1^2=\frac{4}{121}\approx 0.0331. \]

This is about a 5.5-fold reduction in the wrong-to-correct ratio relative to one stage. The wrong fraction among all products falls from approximately 15.4% to 3.2%. However, the probability that a correct complex completes both stages falls from 50% to 25%. Thus, improved discrimination comes with a cost in successful product formation.

3. The Speed–Discrimination Trade-off

When \(k_p\) becomes large, product commitment outruns dissociation. Both correct and wrong complexes pass readily, so \(f_1\) approaches 1 and discrimination disappears. When \(k_p\) becomes small, the one-stage ratio approaches \(k_{\mathrm{off,C}}/k_{\mathrm{off,W}}=0.1\), and the idealized two-stage ratio approaches 0.01. However, successful product formation becomes slow and rare.

4. What I Would Argue Against: Limitations

This calculation is not a complete ribosome model. It assumes equal incoming substrate fluxes, a common product formation rate, fixed dissociation rates, and two independent identical filters. Real proofreading networks can contain branching, backtracking, fuel-coupled cycles, and constraints on reaction rates. Their energy cost depends on how many futile attempts occur before a correct product forms.

The calculation establishes a qualitative trade-off, not a biological parameter estimate. It also does not imply that every driven reaction is proofreading: additional specificity requires an appropriate network topology and kinetic ordering. The central point is narrower: a model claiming proofreading beyond equilibrium discrimination must represent the fuel-driven transitions and nonequilibrium currents rather than hide them inside equilibrium binding weights.

5. Conclusion

The minimal calculation shows how serial kinetic selection can lower the wrong-to-correct product ratio, while also reducing the probability of completing the correct pathway. This makes the specificity–speed trade-off explicit. In the language of CCBS Lectures 7 and 8, equilibrium weights describe reversible binding preferences, whereas fuel-driven catalytic transitions can sustain nonequilibrium cycles. Kinetic proofreading depends on this distinction: the extra discrimination is a property of the driven network, not simply a property of equilibrium binding.

Appendix: Reproduce the Figure

The following Python code reproduces the model curves with NumPy and Matplotlib. It is included for reproducibility; the HTML figure itself is drawn with embedded JavaScript and needs no external library.

Show Python source 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

k_off_correct = 1.0
k_off_wrong = 10.0

kp = np.logspace(-1, 2, 300)

f_one = (kp + k_off_correct) / (kp + k_off_wrong)
f_two = f_one ** 2

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.0))

ax.loglog(kp, f_one, linewidth=2.4,
          label="One-stage selection: f1")
ax.loglog(kp, f_two, linewidth=2.4,
          label="Two-stage idealized filter: f2 = f1^2")

ax.axhline(k_off_correct / k_off_wrong,
           linestyle="--", linewidth=1.4,
           label="One-stage low-rate limit: 0.1")

kp0 = 1.0
f1 = (kp0 + k_off_correct) / (kp0 + k_off_wrong)
f2 = f1 ** 2

ax.scatter([kp0, kp0], [f1, f2], zorder=5)
ax.set_xlabel("Product formation rate kp (s^-1)")
ax.set_ylabel("Wrong : correct product ratio")
ax.set_xlim(0.1, 100)
ax.set_ylim(0.008, 1.1)
ax.grid(True, which="both", alpha=0.22)
ax.legend(frameon=False)
ax.set_title("Faster commitment reduces discrimination")

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("week4-proofreading.svg", format="svg")
plt.show()
