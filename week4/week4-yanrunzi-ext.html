Molecular titration: a switch that needs no cooperativity

Author: Yan Runzi · Week 4 (2026-09-28) · CCBS 2026

Lecture 8 §10 reads one binding pair and finds four responses on a single map; among them, on the tight cut, the free enzyme reports a titration switch — free E jumps a hundredfold in a thin strip of totals. The lecture notes the punchline in a sentence: this "rising threshold of molecular titration needs no cooperativity," and cites DnaA. This is an under-sold point. A switch is usually the signature of cooperative binding or of a nonlinearity; here it emerges from nothing but conservation. This essay takes that one sentence and pushes it: I write down the free-enzyme law, compute the depth of the switch, show how a second binding partner (Lecture 8 A2's sponge) turns a graded response into a switch and moves its threshold, and land on the bacterial example — replication starting once ~300 high-affinity DnaA boxes fill up. Molecular titration, I argue, is an underappreciated computational primitive: a counting and gating device the cell builds with binding alone.

The assignment · what to hand in

One point taken beyond the lecture pair and pushed. Here the point is Lecture 8 §10's closing claim about molecular titration, extended into a computational primitive via A2's sponge and the DnaA counting threshold. It is a "prepend the wrong / under-sold" opinion angle: the steepness of the response comes from conservation, not cooperativity. Structure follows Lectures 7–8: equilibrium, one-pair map, leading-order laws, singular ray.

Part I

The claim, in one binding pair

Steep responses can be bought with inventories, not affinities.

1A switch with reagent hungry, not cooperative

Conservation can generate a switch where the equilibrium law contains no Hill term at all.

The model class of Lecture 8 is a single BINDING pair E + S ⇌ CES with dissociation constant Kd = ES/CES. The two totals are conserved, qE = E + CES and qS = S + CES. Each total is a tiny number in the sense of the course — an inventory — and the whole equilibrium is decided by who wins each of the two totals. Lecture 8 §8 delivers the leading-order complex as the smallest of three candidates,

(1) CES  ≈  min(qE,  qS,  qEqS/Kd).

Three of the four regimes are "one total each": the complex beats one loop, or the free partner beats it. The fourth is singular — CES wins both totals, forcing qE ≈ qS on a thin diagonal ray. It is that ray, with a tight binder qE ≫ Kd, that holds the switch. My claim:

The claim

Tight-binding molecular titration is a switch built from conservation alone: one enzyme and one ligand, a single reversible binding pair, no Hill coefficients, no cooperativity, no feedback. The response can be arbitrarily steep in the inventories near the singular ray qE ≈ qS, and its threshold is where the totals match.

2The one-pair map, and where the switch hides

One map, four responses; only the tight cut shows a switch, and only on a single line.

Lecture 8 §10 runs the pipeline twice: a weak cut qE = 0.01Kd and a tight cut qE = 100Kd, sweeping the substrate total qS and reading two outputs — the bound-complex fraction CES/qE and the free-enzyme fraction E/qE. The four responses are summarized in Table 1, and the qualitative difference is this: on the weak cut only one total ever changes winner, so both readouts are graded (slope 1, 0, −1). On the tight cut, the path crosses the singular ray, both totals change winner at once, and the free-enzyme readout breaks.

The switch breaks at qS = qE — where the inventories match — not at Kd. That is the whole point: the threshold is set by a conservation bookkeeping, and Kd only sets how tight the switch is. On log axes every regular piece is a straight line of integer slope; a change of regime is a change of integer gain. The discontinuity is a one-line feature — literally the singular ray of Figure 12. Nothing in any of these lines is a Hill function.

Part II

The mechanism

Why the free enzyme can jump, and how a sponge sharpens it.

3Titration by a tight sponge

A second binding partner raises the matching point and reprograms the threshold.

Lecture 8 §10 phrases the switch a second way: hold a sequestering total qS ≫ Kd, sweep qE (the "input" is now the enzyme), and read the free enzyme vertically through the map. "Little free enzyme appears until the sequestering pool fills, and then all extra enzyme is free." That sentence is titration made literal: nearly every enzyme is bound until the pool is full, after which each added enzyme stays free. Appendix A2 makes a deeper point: the sequestering partner need not even read out the enzyme — it can be a sponge for a regulator R, sitting ahead of a promoter G. The promoter senses free repressor,

(2) G/qG  =  KGR/(KGR + R),

and half-repression always needs R = KGR — that never changes. What changes is how much total repressor that takes: the sponge T (total qT) swallows repressor with a much tighter constant, KRT ≪ KGR < qT. The free remainder stays tiny, R ≈ KRTqR/qT, while qR < qT. At qR = qT the sponge's own singular ring is crossed; past it every extra repressor stays free, R ≈ qR > KGR, and the promoter shuts. Leading order gives the threshold

(3) threshold  ≈  max(KGR,  qT).
The worked number (made for this essay)

Take promoter KGR = 1 nM, sponge total qT = 100 nM, and a tight sponge KRT = 1 pM. Before the ray, R ≈ KRTqR/qT = 104 · qR, so even qR = 100 nM leaves R = 1 pM ≪ KGR: fully unrepressed. Past qT = 100 nM, R > KGR: shut. The switching interval is set by KRT/KGR = 10−3 — a thousand-fold tighter than the promoter's own response width. Without the sponge the same promoter turns on over ~ 1–10 nM of repressor (graded); with it, the transition is crammed into a few pM near 100 nM. That is a graded response re-cast as a switch, using no cooperativity.

4A worked number: the free pool on the ray

The switch is finite, not a hard cliff — its depth is a square root.

An exact step function is impossible with continuous binding; the switch's real depth is set by the singular balance. On the ray qE ≈ qS > Kd, the difference of the two conservation equations is exact, qE − qS = E − S, and binding gives ES = KdCES ≈ KdqE. Lecture 8 §8 finds the small free pool that survives even when everything is bound:

(4) E  ≈  S  ≈  √(KdqE)    ⇒    E/qE  ≈  √(Kd/qE).

On the tight cut qE = 100Kd, the free-enzyme fraction is 1 far from the ray, drops to √(Kd/qE) = 0.1 on the ray, and arrives at Kd/qS past it — the "hundredfold jump" of §10. The switch is not an instruction to be flat forever; it is a finite well whose floor is the geometric mean of Kd and the tank qE. Tighter binding (smaller Kd/qE) deepens the well and sharpens the switch, but the switch never needs a Hill n > 1.

Part III

Consequences

One place the cell actually counts with titration.

5DnaA counts replication in DnaA boxes

A bacterium turns "the initiator pool is depleted" into "it is time to copy the chromosome."

Lecture 8 §10's own example is bacteria timing DNA replication. The initiator protein DnaA binds ~300 high-affinity DnaA boxes clustered near the single replication origin; the DnaA boxes act as the sponge T, DnaA as the repressor R, and the weak, high-Kd origin sites as the promoter G. Initiation is licensed only once free DnaA crosses a threshold — in the language of (3), once free DnaA exceeds KGR, which happens as qR passes qT ≈ the total DnaA-box capacity. Replication is a switch disciplined by a counting threshold.

What makes this a counting device rather than an analog response is the same conservation arithmetic as Table 1. The tight KRT holds free DnaA minuscule while boxes have spare capacity, so the total DnaA level is read only through the tally of filled boxes — the system effectively counts how many molecules accumulate before any spillover into the origin pools. Initiation is gated on whether that count has reached the box capacity, the singular ray qR ≈ qT. The refractory, all-or-nothing character of bacterial replication initiation is close to this switch's fingerprint.

Why it is a primitive

Titration turns a flux into a threshold with no enzyme and no energy: binding formally performs arithmetic on inventories. It is the same operation in three places — sequestration of an enzyme, a sponge ahead of a promoter, DnaA-box counting — and in each it is the difference of two conserved totals that is being compared to zero. That is why I call it a computational primitive: one conserved operation, many readouts, zero cooperativity.

Part IV

Situating and testing

Where the switch breaks, and what it buys over a real cliff.

6Where this could be wrong, and what a sponge buys

The switch is local in totals, and it competes with noise and slow filling.

The titration switch is not a global cliff, and it is honest to say what would falsify or limit the reading.

  • It lives on a thin strip of totals. The switch requires qE ≫ Kd and a matched qS ≈ qE; beat the inventories far out of balance and the response returns to graded. The steepness is a local feature of the singular ray, not a global law. A system that must be robust to ratio drift gets less from titration than one that can hold the totals near match.
  • Tightness is bounded by Kd. The well depth is √(Kd/qE); get the ligand too tight and the free precursor becomes so scarce that the equilibrium assumption of fast binding itself starts to fray — the switch then becomes a problem of how fast the sponge fills, not merely of how tight it is.
  • Noise smears the threshold. If the initiator count fluctuates (Lecture 5's burst noise), the all-or-nothing point is reached stochastically, and replication timing inherits that jitter. Titration sets a mean threshold; it does not by itself make the threshold sharp against molecular noise.

That last point is the honest boundary of the claim. Yet the value of the sponge over a Hill cliff is precisely conservation: a switch you can tune by writing a number — add or delete DnaA boxes, raise or lower qT — rather than by improving an affinity or engineering a cooperativity that nature may not allow. That is why the lecture's one-line turn of phrase under-sells it: molecular titration is not a curiosity of the tight cut, it is a general recipe for steeping a response, and one the cell demonstrably uses to count.

One number, made and checked

Compute the fold-pool depth on Lecture 8's own tight cut: √(Kd/qE) = √(1/100) = 0.1, a ten-fold dip on the ray and a hundred-fold from the high plateau — matching §10's "hundredfold jump at leading order." This is a unitless ratio, independent of any biological parameter I did not choose. A measured free-DnaA-versus-total-DnaA curve whose midpoint sits at a different multiple of the box capacity, or whose dip is far shallower than √(Kd/qE) at known Kd, would directly test the claim.
