Week 4 — Extension: the binding-catalysis model as a nonlinear feedback loop

Author: Álvaro Bátrez · Week 4 (lectures 7–8) · 2026-09-29 · CCBS 2026

One sentence: lecture 8's binding-catalysis model is a nonlinear feedback loop — the binding layer senses the totals and the catalysis layer closes the loop on them — and asking only what the loop buys, with no linearity anywhere, gives the two classic payoffs: the negative wiring turns a 30% production disturbance into a 10–14% output drift, and the positive wiring manufactures bistability out of an n = 1 response that, without sequestration, can never switch.

The point I am adding

The lecture gives the cell its model class: a fast binding layer that redistributes inside conserved totals, and a slow catalysis layer that moves them. My claim is that this two-layer machine is a feedback loop by construction, and that the feedback reading needs no linearity. When binding is at quasi-equilibrium, the reaction rates do not see the totals q — they see the free amounts, a static nonlinear function h(q). So the slow layer always has the form

q̇ = Γ·v(h(q))

Plant (totals, slow), sensor (binding equilibrium h), actuator (production), and the returned signal is the plant's own state. What survives without linearity is everything that matters: the loop topology, the local loop gain — the susceptibility dh/dq, which the regime map schedules between K/qS ½ and 1 as the totals cross the dominance regimes — and stability by the slope condition of my lecture-4 exposition. What honestly does not survive: superposition, pole placement, transfer functions. The cell has no setpoint and nobody chose its gains; feedback here is a structure of analysis, not of synthesis. What nonlinearity adds is the one dichotomy that linear theory nearly hides: the sign of the loop — negative buffers, positive switches. Both predictions are testable on one and the same binding module.

Work it — one module, two wirings

Module (identical in both tests, Kd = K = 1): one binder and one sequestered partner, free amount x = ½[(qx−qy−K) + √((qx−qy−K)² + 4Kqx)].

Negative wiring (output E sequesters its own activator A, totals qA = 50, Ka = 5, δ = 1): q̇E = α·g(A_free) − qE, Hill n = 1 in the free activator. At α = 100 the output settles at qE* = 52.5 with A_free = 5.5, where the loop gain is L = 2.0 — the sensitivity formula predicts the closed loop passes only 1/(1+L) = 33% of a disturbance. Measured, by re-solving the fixed point:

production capacityopen-loop outputclosed-loop output
α: 100 → 70 (−30%)52.5 → 36.8 (−30.0%) 52.5 → 45.3 (−13.8%)
α: 100 → 130 (+30%)52.5 → 68.3 (+30.0%) 52.5 → 57.6 (+9.7%)

Positive wiring (free E activates its own production, sequestrator qS = 50, Ka = 7, β = 150, δ = 1): q̇E = α + β·g(E_free) − qE, again n = 1. At α = 2 there are three fixed points — 3.6 (stable), 35.3 (unstable), 141.4 (stable) — a genuine bistable switch with no cooperativity anywhere. The control: remove sequestration (qS = 0) and the same self-activator is monostable for every α tested (0.01–100). The mechanism is the shape, not the slope: a Hill-1 production curve is concave from the origin and can never produce the three crossings of my lecture-4 slide 19; sequestration convexifies the foot of the response (the E-wins regime compresses the low end), supplying the S-shape that cooperativity usually provides. Sweeping α, the low and middle branches annihilate at a saddle-node, found exactly by solving f = 0, f′ = 0: αfold = 7.66 at q ≈ 22; for 0 < α < 7.66 the switch is bistable, above it only the high state remains.

The code

Self-written (NumPy/SciPy). Core: the closed-form binding layer, its analytic susceptibility, and root-finding for fixed points and the fold.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

K = 1.0
def free_of(qx, qy):                 # binding layer, closed form
    a = qx - qy - K
    return 0.5*(a + np.sqrt(a*a + 4*K*qx))
def dfree_dqx(qx, qy):               # susceptibility = local loop gain schedule
    a = qx - qy - K; D = np.sqrt(a*a + 4*K*qx)
    return 0.5*(1 + (qx - qy + K)/D)

g  = lambda e, ka: e/(ka + e)        # activation, Hill n = 1
dg = lambda e, ka: ka/(ka + e)**2

# ---- negative wiring: E sequesters activator A (q_A = 50) ----
qA, ka, delta = 50.0, 5.0, 1.0
qstar = lambda a: brentq(lambda q: a*g(free_of(qA, q), ka) - delta*q, 1e-9, 300)
q0 = qstar(100.0)                    # 52.51
L  = (q0/g(free_of(qA,q0), ka)) * dg(free_of(qA,q0), ka) * dfree_dqx(qA, q0)
print(q0, L, qstar(70.0), qstar(130.0))   # 52.51, 2.02, 45.29, 57.59

# ---- positive wiring: free E activates own production (q_S = 50) ----
qS, kap, beta = 50.0, 7.0, 150.0
fnet = lambda q, a: a + beta*g(free_of(q, qS), kap) - q
def roots(a):
    xs = np.linspace(1e-6, 400, 40001); v = fnet(xs, a)
    i = np.where(np.sign(v[:-1])*np.sign(v[1:]) < 0)[0]
    return [brentq(lambda q: fnet(q, a), xs[j], xs[j+1]) for j in i]
print(roots(2.0))                    # [3.61, 35.33, 141.39]
print(roots(8.0))                    # [148.07]  - past the fold at 7.66

What I would argue against / where this breaks

First, the feedback reading is structural and local: loop gain, stability by slope, disturbance rejection — no superposition, no pole placement, and every gain statement is a statement about one operating point (Figure 2 is the honest map of how it moves). Second, the wiring is my choice, not the module's: the same binding layer buffers under the negative wiring and switches under the positive one, so "feedback" predicts nothing until the loop's sign is fixed — and in a real promoter both loops coexist, making the net behavior an empirical matter. Third, well-posedness: the fast layer is an algebraic loop, and in networks with competitors h(q) is multivalued (lecture 8's cubic); I used the one-binding-site case where the physical root is unique, but the feedback framing inherits that root choice silently. Fourth, the quasi-equilibrium assumption: binding fast relative to dilution, the same separation my week-2 essay showed can fail and delay the loop's response. Fifth, no noise: with small molecule numbers the region between the folds is exactly where spontaneous switching should live, and the deterministic diagram promises a memory the cell may not be able to keep.
