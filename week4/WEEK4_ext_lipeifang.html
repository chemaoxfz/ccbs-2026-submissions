Week 4 — Extension: Kinetic proofreading spends energy to buy specificity

Author: lipeifang · Student ID: 20261203497 · Week 4 (2026-09-28) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: Equilibrium binding can discriminate only through state weights, whereas a driven kinetic proofreading step can add a second, rate-based discrimination, but the improvement is paid for in yield and fuel consumption.

The point I am adding

The lectures develop a useful equilibrium shortcut: enumerate molecular states, assign compatible weights, normalize them, and attach an activity to each state. They also warn that a stationary distribution is not automatically equilibrium, because a stationary enzyme can keep circulating and dissipating energy. My extension is a concrete consequence of that distinction: kinetic proofreading.

Suppose an initial selection has already made the incoming wrong:right ratio f0 = 0.01. A second selection sends every candidate into an intermediate. A right intermediate commits to product at rate W and is discarded at rate f0ℓ; a wrong intermediate commits at the same rate W but is discarded at rate ℓ. The asymmetry is deliberate: the wrong intermediate has a much larger rejection hazard. A fuel-coupled reset is what makes this extra kinetic branch physically possible without equilibrium cancellation.

For competing exponential clocks, the probability of commitment is the commitment hazard divided by the sum of exit hazards:

sright = W / (W + f0ℓ),    swrong = W / (W + ℓ).

Therefore the final wrong:right product ratio is

f = f0(W + f0ℓ) / (W + ℓ).

When W ≪ f0ℓ, this approaches f02: a second stage approximately squares the pre-existing error. That is qualitatively different from changing an equilibrium affinity. The new discrimination comes from waiting long enough for the two candidate types to be rejected at different rates.

Work it

I evaluated the model with f0=0.01 and ℓ=1 s-1. The yield per intermediate arrival is

Y = [sright + f0swrong] / (1 + f0).

I also used the explicit accounting rule that every intermediate arrival consumes one fuel conversion, including successful ones. Thus fuel conversions per product are 1/Y. The following values are my calculation, not experimental measurements.

The low-W point gives nearly the squared error (f02 = 10-4), but only about 1% of attempts produce product. At W = 0.001 s-1, the error is still about 1.1 × 10-4, while yield is 9.0%.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

It would be wrong to read the squared-error formula as a universal law saying that every two-step biological pathway squares its error. First, the model starts after an initial selection and assumes a fixed incoming ratio f0. Second, it is a limiting attempt model: it specifies exit hazards but not every reverse microscopic reaction. A thermodynamically complete model must include the fuel reservoir, reverse routes, and the chemical potentials needed to compute finite dissipation. Third, I held the attempt flux fixed. A slower commitment can reduce throughput because intermediates occupy enzymes longer; yield is not the same thing as speed.

The equilibrium lecture therefore remains useful rather than being replaced. It predicts state populations when the fast layer has compatible weights and no unresolved current. Proofreading is the complementary case: energy-driven cycling creates a current and uses time-dependent rejection to improve fidelity. The experimental question is not simply “does the response curve look sharp?” but “does the proposed mechanism account for its fuel use, reverse paths, output rate, and error simultaneously?”
