Week 5 — Extension: A fixed free repressor does not fix total transcription

Author: Kehan Lang · Week 5 (2026-10-19) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: If an adaptive controller pins the free repressor concentration, it also pins promoter occupancy per gene copy, but it does not protect the total transcriptional flux from a change in promoter copy number.

The point I am adding

Lecture 9 builds gene regulation from the allocation of finite binding pools: a repressor occupies a promoter, and the free promoter is the transcriptionally active state. It also stresses that changing a total, such as promoter copy number, is a physical perturbation rather than a cosmetic rescaling. [1] Lecture 10 asks a complementary question for adaptation: which observable does an identity actually pin, and which changes to the model are outside its robustness claim? [2]

I combine those two questions. Imagine that an upstream adaptive module has already done its job: after a persistent disturbance it restores a free repressor concentration R to a set value R*. A natural but too-fast conclusion is then that the repressed gene is protected. That conclusion is true for one observable—the fraction of each promoter copy that remains free—but false for another—the total number of free promoters and hence the total transcriptional flux. Gene dosage is not only a hypothetical perturbation: replication-associated copy-number changes can alter expression in bacteria. [3]

The distinction matters because “the output adapts” is incomplete until the output has been named. This essay does not claim that promoter duplication is an ordinary input to the upstream controller. It is a change of the network boundary. The calculation shows exactly what remains invariant under that change and what does not.

Work it

Take one repressor and one promoter state. Free repressor R binds a free promoter D to make a repressed complex C. Let the promoter total DT count all copies, whether free or occupied. Binding is assumed fast relative to transcription and to the adaptive controller:

R + D ⇌ C,    KD = RD/C,    DT = D + C.

At binding equilibrium, C = RD/KD. Substitute this into the promoter balance, then solve for the two quantities that an experiment might call “the output”:

D = DT KD/(KD + R),    ffree ≡ D/DT = KD/(KD + R).

The fraction ffree is an intensive observable: it says what share of copies is open. The amount D is extensive: it counts open copies. If every free promoter initiates transcription at rate α, then total transcript production is

J = αD = αDTKD/(KD + R).

What the adaptive controller fixes

Now impose the stated controller result: at its resting state it fixes the free repressor at R = R*. It follows directly that

ffree* = KD/(KD + R*)    is independent of DT,
J* = αDTKD/(KD + R*)    is proportional to DT.

For a concrete normalization, choose R* = KD. Every promoter copy is then free half of the time in the equilibrium ensemble: ffree* = 1/2. A duplication changes DT to 2DT, leaves that fraction at 1/2, and changes J* to 2J*. The controller has protected the per-copy regulatory state; it has not protected the total flux from additional copies.

Putting a downstream protein on the same accounting

Suppose transcription produces a downstream product X, with first-order removal δX. Under the same boundary, its balance is ẋX = J − δX. At rest,

X* = αDTKD/[δ(KD + R*)].

Thus the conclusion is not confined to an abstract flux. If the measured output is a total mRNA or protein made from these copies, that output changes with dosage even while free repressor and fractional occupancy return perfectly. This is the same observable discipline as Lecture 10's warning that a pinned free species need not imply that every pool containing it has the same invariance.

The assumption that makes Figure 1 possible

Figure 1 intentionally gives the controller enough authority to restore R after duplication. A finite pool gives a useful contrast. If what is fixed is instead the total repressor constituent RT = R + C, the free repressor must solve

RT = R + DTR/(KD + R).

Here extra promoters titrate repressor into C. The same duplication lowers free R, raises the free-promoter fraction, and can increase flux by more than the simple twofold gene-dose factor. This is not a contradiction of Figure 1: it is a different boundary condition. It distinguishes an adaptive controller that pins a free concentration from a finite repressor reservoir that only conserves a total. Experiments on transcription-factor titration likewise show why copies and binding-site demand cannot be ignored when interpreting repression. [4]

Interpretation. “Free repressor adapts” and “transcription adapts” are equivalent only after specifying which transcriptional quantity is meant. In this model, adaptation of R protects the per-copy occupancy D/DT. It does not make a new promoter copy disappear, nor does it hold the total production rate αD fixed. The proposed distinction is exact for the stated rapid-equilibrium and fixed-free-repressor model.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

I would argue against calling gene duplication a failure of the controller's original disturbance-rejection claim without first naming that claim. If the controller was designed to restore free R against an input disturbance, Figure 1 says it can still do exactly that after dosage changes. The conclusion that fails is stronger and different: that every downstream quantity is thereby held fixed. Conversely, Figure 2 should not be presented as a failure of an ideal controller; it deliberately replaces that controller's free-concentration constraint with conservation of a finite total.

The calculation assumes one noncooperative binding site per promoter, fast binding equilibrium, identical promoter copies, a well-mixed compartment, constant α, and no resource limitation by RNA polymerase or ribosomes. Real duplication can also change growth, chromosome position, dosage through the cell cycle, and the controller itself. At very low copy number, promoter occupancy is stochastic rather than the continuous fraction written here. Those cases require a kinetic or stochastic model. The claim here is narrower: within the model, promoter fraction and total transcription are distinct observables with different invariances.

const KD = 1, Rstar = 1, alpha = 1;
const freeFraction = R => KD / (KD + R);
const fixedFreeFlux = DT => alpha * DT * freeFraction(Rstar);

// Positive root of RT = R + DT*R/(KD + R), for Figure 2.
function freeRfromTotal(DT, RT = 2) {
  const b = KD + DT - RT;
  return (-b + Math.sqrt(b*b + 4*RT*KD)) / 2;
}
