Week 5 — Extension: the most basic biological MPC collapses into the lecturer's PI

Author: Álvaro Bátrez · Week 5 (lectures 9–10) · 2026-10-13 · CCBS 2026

One sentence: one-step constrained model predictive control run on the lecturer's own discrete plant is algebraically identical to a PI controller with anti-windup (verified to machine zero), explicit-MPC theory says constrained MPC is always a piecewise-affine feedback — the same piecewise machinery the course's regime maps already provide — and what keeps MPC out of the cell is not the impossibility of feedback but the cost of wiring its partition: the integral controller of Briat, Gupta & Khammash is the MPC whose entire partition has one region.

The point I am adding

Lecture 10 built a controller-to-molecules ladder: proportional action leaves an offset, integral action removes it, and chemistry can implement the integral (saturated enzyme, antithetic sequestration, multiplicative growth). The paper the lecturer highlighted — Briat, Gupta & Khammash's antithetic integral feedback (Cell Systems 2:15–26, 2016) — sits on rung two. My question: what would it take to climb to the rung control engineers consider the most powerful — model predictive control — inside a cell? MPC decides at each step by predicting the future over a horizon and optimizing, which sounds hopeless for chemistry: no molecule solves quadratic programs. But a theorem most control courses never reach changes the picture. Bemporad, Morari, Dua & Pistikopoulos (Automatica 38:3–20, 2002) proved that for a linear plant with input constraints and quadratic cost, the entire receding-horizon optimization collapses offline into a piecewise-affine static feedback: the state space splits into polyhedral regions, and inside each region the optimal law is just u = Kᵢx + kᵢ. At run time MPC is not an optimizer at all — it is: find your region, evaluate an affine law.

That is a stunning fit with this course. The regime map of lecture 8 is exactly a partition of state space into regions with local monomial-affine laws; sequestration builds steep region boundaries (the titration switch); saturation builds the ceilings. A small explicit MPC is, in principle, compilable into the course's own primitives — each region boundary one more titration threshold, each gain one more dominance regime. So "biological MPC" is not a category error; it is a budget question. The essay measures that budget twice: once at the bottom of the ladder, once in the middle.

Work it — measurement 1: the bottom rung, on the lecturer's own plant

Lecture 10's discrete plant is y[t+1] = y[t] + w − u[t], with actuator ceiling 0 ≤ u ≤ uₘₐₓ and unknown constant inflow w. The most basic MPC there is — horizon N = 1, cost y[t+1]² — has a closed form. Minimizing (y[t] + w − u)² over the interval gives u* = y[t] + w; with w unknown, estimate it from the data the plant itself provides, ŵ[t] = y[t] − y[t⁻₁] + u[t⁻₁]:

u[t] = clip( y[t] + ŵ[t],  0, uₘₐₓ )

Expand the estimator: u[t] = u[t⁻₁] + (y[t] − y[t⁻₁]) + y[t] — a PI controller in velocity (incremental) form with kₖ = kᵢ = 1, whose integral state is the actually applied input u[t⁻₁], which is why it cannot wind up: the integral is clamped to the actuator range by construction. I verified the identity numerically: the MPC law and the velocity-form PI produce bit-identical input and error trajectories (maximum difference 0.0 — machine zero). The most basic biological MPC is the lecturer's PI, wearing anti-windup.

Then the lecture's own windup pathology becomes the experiment (w = 1.2, ceiling uₘₐₓ = 1.5, initial error y₀ = 6):

controllerbehavior
position-form PI (4:1, gain ½), integrates while saturated winds up: error undershoots to −4.65, oscillates for ~50 steps before settling
same PI + conditional anti-windup minimal undershoot (−0.22), settles
MPC-1 (= velocity-form PI) deadbeat: error reaches 0 at t = 20 and stays there exactly; input plan stays inside the ceiling throughout

And in the infeasible case (w = 1.8 > uₘₐₓ = 1.5, the lecture's "unreachable set point"), the two structures diverge in state, not behavior: the naive integrator state grows without bound (S → 1428 by t = 80) while MPC's implicit integral — the applied input — stays pinned at the ceiling, with ŵ⁰ still converging to the true 1.8. The estimator knows the disturbance is infeasible; the exploding integrator is the controller's version of denial.

Work it — measurement 2: the price of climbing

One region up: a two-state plant (discretized double integrator), one actuator with an asymmetric box 0 ≤ u ≤ 1.2, quadratic cost — and I enumerated the explicit-MPC partition on a 90×90 grid of initial states, solving each tiny quadratic program exactly (SLSQP, unconstrained closed form when interior). Counting distinct saturation patterns — each pattern is one affine region of the controller — gives:

horizon N123456
regions24691215

Three more precisely-placed region boundaries per extra step of foresight, for the simplest plant with a single actuator. Compile that to molecules and every row of that table is a parts list: each region boundary is another sequestration threshold that must sit at the right place, each affine gain another dominance regime that must hold the right slope — and the partition of real MPC problems is known from the literature to grow combinatorially with states, inputs and constraints (surveys document explicit solutions with thousands of regions). This is the honest sense in which biological MPC is "possible": the run-time law is static and piecewise, the course's chemistry builds exactly such maps — and the budget explodes. Evolution's answer was to stop at rung two: don't predict the partition, integrate the error. The antithetic controller is the MPC whose entire partition has one region.

The code

Self-written (NumPy/SciPy). Part 1: the three controllers on the lecture's plant and the machine-zero equivalence check. Part 2: exact grid enumeration of the explicit-MPC partition.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# ---- Part 1: MPC-1 on the lecturer's plant y[t+1] = y[t] + w - u[t] ----
w, umax, y0, T = 1.2, 1.5, 6.0, 80
def mpc1(w, umax, y0, T):                 # u = clip(y + w_hat)
    y = np.zeros(T+1); u = np.zeros(T); y[0] = y0
    for t in range(T):
        what = (y[t]-y[t-1]+u[t-1]) if t else 0.0
        u[t] = np.clip(y[t] + what, 0, umax)
        y[t+1] = y[t] + w - u[t]
    return y, u
def velocity_pi(w, umax, y0, T):          # u = clip(u[-1] + dy + y)
    y = np.zeros(T+1); u = np.zeros(T); y[0] = y0
    for t in range(T):
        u[t] = np.clip((u[t-1]+y[t]-y[t-1]+y[t]) if t else y[t], 0, umax)
        y[t+1] = y[t] + w - u[t]
    return y, u
def position_pi(w, umax, y0, T, kP=0.4, kI=0.1, aw=False):
    y = np.zeros(T+1); u = np.zeros(T); S = np.zeros(T+1); y[0] = y0
    for t in range(T):
        u_raw = kP*y[t] + kI*S[t]
        u[t] = np.clip(u_raw, 0, umax)
        halt = aw and ((u_raw > umax and y[t] > 0) or (u_raw < 0 and y[t] < 0))
        S[t+1] = S[t] + (0.0 if halt else y[t])
        y[t+1] = y[t] + w - u[t]
    return y, u, S
ym, um = mpc1(w, umax, y0, T)
yi, ui = velocity_pi(w, umax, y0, T)
print(np.max(np.abs(um-ui)))              # 0.0 : the collapse, machine zero

# ---- Part 2: explicit-MPC regions (2 states, 1 input, 0 <= u <= 1.2) ----
A = np.array([[1,.1],[0,1]]); B = np.array([[.005],[.1]])
Q = np.diag([2.0, 1.0]); R = .3; ul, ub = 0.0, 1.2
def qp(x0, N):
    H = np.zeros((N,N)); F = np.zeros((N,2))
    for k in range(N):
        for j in range(k+1):
            Mj = np.linalg.matrix_power(A,k-j) @ B
            for i in range(k+1):
                Mi = np.linalg.matrix_power(A,k-i) @ B
                H[j,i] += (Mj.T @ Q @ Mi)[0,0]
            F[j] += (Mj.T @ Q @ np.linalg.matrix_power(A,k+1)).ravel()
        H[k,k] += R
    H = .5*(H+H.T)
    z = -.5*np.linalg.solve(H, F@x0)
    if not (np.all(z >= ul) and np.all(z <= ub)):
        J  = lambda z: float(z@H@z + (F@x0)@z)
        dJ = lambda z: 2*H@z + F@x0
        z = minimize(J, np.clip(z,ul,ub), jac=dJ, bounds=[(ul,ub)]*N,
                     method='SLSQP', options={'ftol':1e-12}).x
    return z
for N in range(1, 7):
    pats = {tuple(0 if v<1e-4 else (2 if v>1.2-1e-4 else 1)
                  for v in qp(np.array([a,b]), N))
            for a in np.linspace(-2,2,90) for b in np.linspace(-2,2,90)}
    print(N, len(pats))                   # 2 4 6 9 12 15

What I would argue against / where this breaks

The collapse result is exactly as deep as N = 1: it says the first rung of MPC is PI-with-anti-windup, not that MPC is "just" PI — longer horizons and known future references buy genuinely new behavior (preview, constraint-aware planning) that no feedback of current error can imitate, and my measurement 2 is precisely the price tag of that novelty. The "compilable to molecules" claim is theoretical: nobody has wired a multi-region biomolecular MPC, and the compilation assumes each affine gain and each boundary is implementable and fast relative to the plant — the same timescale-separation bill my week-2 essay watched the toggle pay. My region counts are a grid-based lower bound from one plant, not the exact partition, and the exponential worst cases belong to bigger problems than mine (single-input double integrators grow roughly linearly, which I report as such). Part 1's plant is deterministic and noise-free; lecture 10 itself warns the discrete clock is a fiction, and an MPC whose ŵ⁰ estimates w from single steps would be fragile under molecular noise — the stochastic setting where the antithetic controller's guarantees were actually proved. And the deepest objection is the one the lecture already taught me: cells do not have objectives. MPC optimizes a cost somebody wrote; evolution wrote no such function, so the region budget was never there to spend.
