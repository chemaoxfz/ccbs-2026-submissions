Week 5 — When zero-mean input noise moves the mean output

Author: Su Peng · Week 5 (2026-10-17) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: For a nonlinear biochemical rate, zero mean input fluctuations shift the mean output by approximately ½[H2−H+dH/d ln x]CVx2, so the reaction order geometry used to describe switches and adaptation also predicts where noise creates a systematic bias.

Why symmetric input noise can shift the mean response

Lecture 9 showed how binding and catalysis generate graded responses, thresholds and adaptation. Lecture 10 asked how an adaptive circuit returns its output after a constant input step. Lecture 11 then defined the local reaction order

H = d ln v / d ln x,

so that a one-percent change in input x produces approximately an H-percent change in rate v. I take these results as background and ask a narrower question that the lectures did not answer: if the input fluctuates symmetrically around the same mean, must the mean rate remain unchanged?

No. Let the fluctuating input be

X = x(1 + η),    ⟨η⟩ = 0,    ⟨η2⟩ = CVx2,

where x is the mean input and η is its relative fluctuation. Expanding the rate around x gives

v(X) ≈ v(x) + v′(x)(X−x) + ½v″(x)(X−x)2.

Averaging over the input fluctuations removes the first-order term because ⟨X−x⟩ = 0:

⟨v(X)⟩ − v(x) ≈ ½v″(x) Var(X).

Since Var(X) = x2CVx2,

[⟨v(X)⟩ − v(x] / v(x) ≈ ½[ x2v″(x) / v(x) ] CVx2.

The normalized curvature can be written using the reaction order:

x2v″(x) / v(x) = H2 − H + dH / d ln x.

Therefore,

[⟨v(X)⟩ − v(x)] / v(x) ≈ ½[ H2 − H + dH / d ln x ] CVx2.

The reaction order H describes ordinary local gain, while its change with concentration helps describe curvature. Equal upward and downward input fluctuations cancel at first order, but after passing through a curved response they can produce unequal output changes. The resulting shift is therefore a real consequence of nonlinear biochemical processing, not measurement error.

Steep Hill responses magnify noise induced mean shifts

Consider the Hill response v(x)=Vmaxxm/(Km+xm). Its reaction order is H=m(1−v/Vmax). At the midpoint x=K, the quantity in square brackets is −m/2. The fractional shift is consequently −(m/4)CVx2.

Suppose the input coefficient of variation is 20%. For a noncooperative response with m=1, the predicted mean output at the midpoint is shifted downward by approximately 1%. For a steeper response with m=4, it is shifted downward by approximately 4%. Both inputs have the same mean and variance. The fourfold difference is created only by the shape of the response. Below the threshold, the m=4 curve can instead have positive curvature, so noise may raise the mean output there. Noise can therefore move an apparent switching boundary rather than merely broaden observations around it.

This matters for adaptation because a constant-input experiment tests whether final output depends on the fixed input, whereas a fluctuating environment tests whether ⟨v(X)⟩ equals v(⟨X⟩). A feedforward adaptive design may match its opposing arms for constant inputs yet acquire an average offset if those arms have different curvatures. Matching reaction orders at one point controls first-order sensitivity; avoiding this noise bias additionally requires the two curvature combinations to match.

Limits of the curvature based noise test

One cannot draw the general conclusion that "noise will definitely destroy all adaptive systems" just because the formula predicts a noise induced mean shift. It is a weak noise, local approximation. Large or skewed fluctuations require averaging the full nonlinear rate over the actual input distribution. The calculation also assumes that the rate responds instantaneously. If fluctuations are faster than binding or downstream turnover, dynamical filtering changes the result. Intrinsic reaction noise and correlations between the two adaptive arms are not included.

The distinction between adaptive architectures also matters. A stable integral feedback mechanism may remove a mean offset that remains in a feedforward design, provided that its molecular memory does not leak, saturate or wind up. The formula is therefore a diagnostic for nonlinear noise transmission, not a universal failure theorem for adaptation. Its useful prediction is narrower: deterministic matching of local slopes does not by itself guarantee matching of mean responses under fluctuating inputs.
