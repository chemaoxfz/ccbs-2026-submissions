Week 5 — Extension: A clock can oscillate but lose the time

Author: Li Changkai · Week 5 · CCBS 2026

One sentence: A stable deterministic limit cycle is not automatically a precise biological clock: independent molecular noise diffuses phase, so a population can lose synchrony even while individual cells keep oscillating.

The point I am adding

Lecture 9 uses the repressilator, a ring of mutually repressing genes, to illustrate how a closed biochemical circuit can generate oscillation. But a clock must do more than oscillate: it must also keep track of time reliably. Two cells can both oscillate with similar periods while reaching the same phase at different times.

The distinction comes from the geometry of a stable limit cycle. A deterministic perturbation away from the cycle can decay, restoring the oscillation's amplitude. However, motion along the cycle corresponds to a shift in phase. There is no preferred phase at which an autonomous oscillator must remain. Molecular fluctuations can therefore accumulate as timing errors even when the oscillation itself persists.

A minimal model isolates this effect. Let θ be the oscillator's phase, ω its mean angular frequency, and D its phase-diffusion coefficient. Represent the accumulated random timing error by

dθ = ω dt + √(2D) dW(t)

Var[θ(t) − ωt] = 2Dt

Here W(t) is a standard Wiener process. The first equation says that phase advances on average at a constant rate but receives random increments. The second follows because independent Wiener increments accumulate variance linearly with time.

For cells synchronized at time zero, define the population's phase coherence as

R(t) = |⟨exp(i[θ(t) − ωt])⟩| = exp(−Dt).

The last equality follows from the characteristic function of a Gaussian random variable with variance 2Dt. R = 1 means perfect phase alignment; R near zero means that phases are broadly dispersed. Importantly, R is not the fraction of cells that are still oscillating. It measures how closely their phases agree.

Work it: how fast does synchrony disappear?

I calculated R(t) for two illustrative phase-diffusion coefficients: D = 0.020 h⁻¹ and D = 0.005 h⁻¹. These values are chosen to demonstrate the model; they are not estimates fitted to a biological experiment. The predicted coherence half-time is obtained by setting R(t) = 1/2:

t1/2 = ln(2)/D

For D = 0.020 h⁻¹, the half-time is 34.7 hours. For D = 0.005 h⁻¹, it is 138.6 hours. At 20 hours, the corresponding coherence values are 0.670 and 0.905. Thus, reducing D fourfold increases the coherence half-time fourfold, without changing the model's mean frequency ω.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

This calculation does not prove that a particular genetic circuit will have either diffusion coefficient. It starts by assuming that a stable oscillation already exists and then models only its phase. Near the boundary between oscillation and a stable resting state, molecular noise can also change amplitude, period, or whether oscillation persists at all. Stochastic simulations of the repressilator show why a deterministic rate equation alone may miss these effects [3].

Nor must every population lose synchrony at the same rate. Reducing important noise sources can improve clock precision: a redesigned repressilator demonstrated long-term oscillations and phase coherence under the tested conditions [4]. Coupling between cells or an external periodic signal can also alter phase dispersion. The useful conclusion is narrower: oscillation, phase precision, and synchronization are different performance properties and should be measured separately.

Reproduce the calculation

The following Python code generates the plotted curves and prints the two numerical comparisons. It evaluates the phase model directly; it is not a Gillespie simulation and does not infer noise strength from molecular reaction rates. 

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

D_values = [0.020, 0.005]  # illustrative values, per hour
t = np.linspace(0, 140, 500)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 3.2))

for D in D_values:
    R = np.exp(-D * t)
    ax.plot(t, R, label=f"D = {D:.3f} h^-1")
    R20 = math.exp(-D * 20)
    half_time = math.log(2) / D
    print(
        f"D={D:.3f}: R(20 h)={R20:.3f}; "
        f"coherence half-time={half_time:.1f} h"
    )

ax.set_xlabel("Time after synchronization (h)")
ax.set_ylabel("Phase coherence R(t)")
ax.set_xlim(0, 140)
ax.set_ylim(0, 1.02)
ax.grid(alpha=0.25)
ax.legend(frameon=False)
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("phase_coherence.svg", bbox_inches="tight")
plt.show()
