WEEK 5 — Extension: Can negative cooperativity create a sharp insulin response?

Author: Fei Wang · Week 5 (2026-10-18) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: An insulin-inspired model can generate a steep response from negative cooperativity only when ligand depletion and an occupancy-dependent activation rule are added, so its threshold is a testable hypothesis rather than an established property of the insulin receptor.

The point I am adding

The supplied figure suggests a counterintuitive possibility: a receptor with negative binding cooperativity can still produce a sharp response to total ligand. Insulin receptor (IR) provides a useful biological starting point. IR is a preassembled, disulfide-linked (αβ)₂ complex. Its two α-containing receptor halves provide interacting insulin-binding surfaces, and classic ligand-dissociation experiments support negative cooperativity. [1]

However, “one simple binding site per α subunit” is an approximation: structural studies identify two pairs of insulin-binding sites and up to four bound insulins. They also identify asymmetric, incompletely occupied active states. [2] [3] Thus, only double occupancy activates the receptor must be introduced as a model assumption, not a universal biological fact.

My proposed mechanism is conditional: if the first insulin binds tightly but leaves the receptor effectively silent, low total insulin is absorbed into singly occupied complexes. Once that pool is largely filled, additional insulin creates doubly occupied, signaling complexes. This could make a total-dose response appear unusually steep. The calculation below tests that mechanism and then relaxes its crucial “silent first occupancy” assumption.

Work it

A two-occupancy approximation. Let R, RI and RI₂ denote an unoccupied receptor complex and complexes carrying one or two insulin molecules. This is a coarse-grained comparison model, not the complete IR binding architecture. With free insulin concentration I, retain the figure’s statistical rate factors:

R + I ⇌ RI   (forward 2k₊₁; reverse k₋₁)
RI + I ⇌ RI₂   (forward k₊₂; reverse 2k₋₂)
K₁ = k₋₁/k₊₁,   K₂ = k₋₂/k₊₂
[RI]/[R] = 2I/K₁,   [RI₂]/[RI] = I/(2K₂)

Define w₁ = 2I/K₁, w₂ = I²/(K₁K₂) and Z = 1 + w₁ + w₂. The receptor fractions are f₀ = 1/Z, f₁ = w₁/Z and f₂ = w₂/Z. The factor of two must not be dropped when comparing these per-site constants with macroscopic stepwise constants.

qR = [R] + [RI] + [RI₂]
qI = I + [RI] + 2[RI₂] = I + qR(f₁ + 2f₂)
Sε = εf₁ + f₂,   0 ≤ ε ≤ 1

Here qR counts receptor complexes, not individual α or β chains. S is a normalized, baseline-subtracted model output: the doubly occupied state has unit efficacy, and ε is the relative efficacy of the singly occupied state. The lecture uses ε = 0. I compare it with ε = 0.5 as an illustrative alternative; this value is not an experimental estimate of IR activity.

Parameter choice. Set K₁ = 0.01K₂ and qR = 100K₂. This implements the figure’s approximately 100-fold affinity contrast and a receptor-rich, tight-binding regime. All concentrations are expressed in units of K₂; these are deliberately chosen teaching parameters, not measured insulin affinities or physiological receptor concentrations.

For each total insulin level, I solved the mass-balance equation for free I. At qI = qR, the results are I/K₂ ≈ 0.099, f₁ ≈ 0.909 and f₂ ≈ 0.045. Thus:

S₀ ≈ 0.045   versus   S₀.₅ ≈ 0.5 × 0.909 + 0.045 ≈ 0.500.

The same binding distribution therefore gives approximately 4.5% or 50.0% normalized output, depending only on the signaling rule.

How steep is the response? For S₀ = f₂, define the response-width index n10–90 = ln(81)/ln(D₉₀/D₁₀), where D₁₀ and D₉₀ give 10% and 90% of the asymptotic maximum. This equals the Hill exponent for an exact Hill curve; here it is a descriptive index, not a count of binding sites.

Identical receptors therefore look graded against free insulin but steep against total insulin. The difference comes from ligand sequestration and the output rule, not positive binding cooperativity. At qI/qR = 0.5, S₀ is only 0.00123, whereas S₀.₅ is 0.250.

The 10% and 90% points were calculated directly: for f₂ = y, I = [yK₂ + √(y²K₂² + y(1 − y)K₁K₂)]/(1 − y); substitute I into the same mass balance. Doses are normalized separately on each axis.

Reproduction: Set K₂ = 1, K₁ = 0.01, qR = 100. Sweep x = qI/qR from 0 to 2.5 in steps of 0.01. For each x, bisect I ∈ [0, 100x] for 100 iterations to solve I + 100(f₁ + 2f₂) = 100x; then evaluate S₀ and S₀.₅. At x = 0, use I = 0. Calculations were performed in Python and plotted as embedded SVG.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

Binding evidence does not establish the activation rule. An upward-curving Scatchard plot (bound/free versus bound insulin) is compatible with negative cooperativity, but binding-site heterogeneity can also produce such curvature. The classic accelerated-dissociation experiment is more discriminating. [1] Neither observation proves that singly occupied IR is silent. Partially occupied active structures challenge that assumption; their precise signaling efficacy is not fixed by the structures alone. [2] [3]

Receptor activation and downstream output are separate measurements. Insulin-induced β-subunit autophosphorylation can initiate IRS-1/PI3K–Akt signaling. [3] [4] However, this model contains no phosphorylation, phosphatase or downstream reaction kinetics. S therefore cannot be equated directly with measured phospho-Akt or glucose uptake. It also omits the third and fourth insulin-binding events.

The steepness is conditional, not a general explanation of insulin dose–response curves. The value 6.732 requires the chosen receptor-rich regime and a total-ligand input. If free insulin is imposed by a large reservoir, the same f₂ curve has n10–90 ≈ 1.005. For surface receptors, qR must be calculated from receptor number and an explicitly defined assay volume. A steep measured curve alone cannot establish this mechanism.

I would test the hypothesis by measuring free and total insulin, occupancy, receptor autophosphorylation and phospho-Akt while varying receptor abundance. A depletion-driven threshold should shift with receptor amount under the assumed model. Detectable signaling before substantial double occupancy would instead motivate ε > 0. The distinction is between a mathematically sufficient mechanism and a biologically demonstrated one.
