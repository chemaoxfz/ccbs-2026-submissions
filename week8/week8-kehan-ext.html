Week 8 — Extension: A strong binder can lose its target in a crowded mixture

Author: Kehan Lang · Week 8 (2026-11-09) · CCBS 2026

One sentence: In a single-site equilibrium model, a designed binder's target affinity alone cannot predict its target occupancy when a competing molecule is present: the competitor's free concentration relative to its affinity determines the loss.

The point I am adding

Lecture 15 distinguishes a designed interface from evidence that the protein performs its intended job, and asks a reporter to distinguish a target from a competitor [1]. Lecture 16 asks how molecular components can be assembled into a mechanistic virtual cell [2]. I consider a specific missing link between those questions: if a binder is characterized against its target alone, what changes when the binder encounters a second molecule that uses the same site?

My claim is conditional and testable: when both molecules bind independently to the same site and free concentrations are fixed, target occupancy falls as the competitor's concentration divided by its dissociation constant increases. A strong target affinity can be insufficient at the operating concentration. This is a calculation for a hypothetical binder, not evidence that any particular designed protein has an off-target interaction.

Work it

Let B be free binder, T free target, C free competitor, and BT and BC their complexes. Concentrations and dissociation constants are in µM. Assume one site per binder, mutually exclusive binding, equilibrium, and free T and C held fixed by large reservoirs. For B + T ⇌ BT and B + C ⇌ BC, mass action gives

KT = [B][T]/[BT],   KC = [B][C]/[BC].

The total binder concentration is Btot = [B] + [BT] + [BC]. Substituting [BT] = [B][T]/KT and [BC] = [B][C]/KC gives Btot = [B](1 + [T]/KT + [C]/KC). Dividing [BT] by this total yields the target-bound fraction:

θT = ([T]/KT) / (1 + [T]/KT + [C]/KC).   (1)

The three terms in the denominator represent free binder, target-bound binder, and competitor-bound binder relative to free binder. Equation 1 is the classical single-site competition balance [3]; the example and calculations below apply it to a hypothetical protein-design specification. At zero competitor it reduces to [T]/(KT + [T]), the familiar single-ligand result. Increasing [C] reduces θT because it increases the share of binder in BC; the statement holds at fixed free target and fixed positive constants.

Take [T] = 1 µM and KT = 0.1 µM, so the target-to-affinity ratio is 10. Without competitor, θT = 10/11 = 0.909. If KC = 1 µM and free competitor rises to 10 µM, θT = 10/21 = 0.476. The target affinity is ten times stronger than the competitor affinity, yet fewer than half of binder molecules carry target in this example: the competitor is ten times as concentrated as the target.

How much competitor changes the answer?

The relevant quantity is not competitor concentration by itself, but the dimensionless ratio [C]/KC. To see what this ratio does, write a = [T]/KT and b = [C]/KC. With no competitor, the target-bound fraction is θ0 = a/(1 + a). Dividing Equation 1 by that baseline gives

θT/θ0 = (1 + a)/(1 + a + b).   (2)

Thus the competitor reduces target occupancy to half its competitor-free value when b = 1 + a, or [C] = KC(1 + [T]/KT). This is a relative half-occupancy condition: it is not necessarily the concentration at which half of all binder is target-bound. In the example, a = 10 and KC = 1 µM, so 11 µM competitor reduces target occupancy from 0.909 to 0.455. A higher free target concentration raises this particular half-displacement concentration, because target molecules also compete for the available site.

The target can therefore protect its own occupancy, but the amount required may be incompatible with the application. Solving Equation 1 for [T] at a desired occupancy q, where 0 < q < 1, gives

[T] ≥ KT · q/(1 − q) · (1 + [C]/KC).   (3)

For an 80% requirement, KT = 0.1 µM and [C]/KC = 10, Equation 3 requires at least 4.4 µM free target. Without competitor the same threshold needs 0.4 µM. This elevenfold increase is an equilibrium prediction under fixed free concentrations, not a prediction that adding target to a cell will necessarily restore its signal.

Interpretation

Suppose a proposed application requires at least 80% of binder to carry target at equilibrium. Writing a = [T]/KT, Equation 1 gives a/(1 + a + [C]/KC) ≥ 0.8, or [C] ≤ KC(a/4 − 1). This criterion is feasible at nonnegative competitor concentration only if a ≥ 4. For the example, a = 10 and KC = 1 µM, so the maximum free competitor concentration is 1.5 µM. The threshold is a model-derived design requirement, not a measured tolerance.

A useful component specification would therefore include both target and competitor affinities and their expected free concentrations. One way to test the assumption is to measure target binding over a competitor titration, then compare the measured curve with Equation 1. A systematic mismatch could motivate a different binding model rather than an adjustment to the target affinity alone. This is a small illustration of the composition problem: a molecular parameter becomes predictive only when the relevant interaction and environment are specified.

The distinction also matters when comparing designs. Consider binder A with KT = 0.1 µM and KC = 1 µM, and binder B with the weaker target affinity KT = 0.2 µM but KC = 10 µM. At [T] = 1 µM without competitor, A binds target more often: 0.909 versus 0.833. At [C] = 10 µM, the fractions are 10/21 ≈ 0.476 for A and 5/7 ≈ 0.714 for B. The ranking reverses because B is less susceptible to this competitor. These two hypothetical binders illustrate why a design decision should include the environment and the intended readout.

An informative assay would first estimate each affinity separately under matched conditions, then measure target-bound binder across a range of known free competitor concentrations. Measuring only total fluorescence would be insufficient if target-bound and competitor-bound binder produce indistinguishable signals. The experiment needs a target-specific readout and either negligible depletion or an independent estimate of free concentrations. Agreement with the curve would be consistent with the model; it would not prove that every other interaction has been excluded.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

I would argue against ranking designs solely by target KT in a mixture. A low target KT is useful, but the ranking of target occupancy can change if the candidates also have different competitor affinities. Equation 1 describes equilibrium, not the time required to reach it; slow binding, transport, turnover or transient exposure require an explicit kinetic model. Occupancy is also not automatically reporter output or cellular function.

The free-concentration assumption matters. If binder sequesters an appreciable fraction of either molecule, total concentrations cannot simply replace [T] or [C]: all three mass balances must be solved together. Multiple sites, simultaneous binding, allostery or other off-target molecules likewise change the denominator. Finally, 80% is a chosen threshold; the appropriate threshold and candidate competitors must come from the intended assay or application.

Code used for the figure

Show or hide the Python code

The following Python 3 code generates the SVG and prints values from the same equation. It uses only the standard library.

T,KT=1.0,0.1
KCS=(0.1,1.0,10.0)
def occupancy(c,kc): return (T/KT)/(1+T/KT+c/kc)
def xy(c,v): return 70+500*c/20, 245-200*v
svg=['<svg class="fig" viewBox="0 0 640 310" role="img" aria-labelledby="plot-title plot-desc" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg">','<title id="plot-title">Competition reduces target occupancy</title>','<desc id="plot-desc">Target occupancy against free competitor concentration for three competitor dissociation constants.</desc>','<rect width="640" height="310" fill="var(--soft)"/>']
for v in (0,.2,.4,.6,.8,1):
 x,y=xy(0,v); svg += [f'<line x1="70" y1="{y}" x2="570" y2="{y}" stroke="var(--line)"/>',f'<text x="60" y="{y+5}" text-anchor="end" fill="var(--ink)" font-size="13">{v:.1f}</text>']
for c in (0,5,10,15,20):
 x,y=xy(c,0); svg.append(f'<text x="{x}" y="268" text-anchor="middle" fill="var(--ink)" font-size="13">{c}</text>')
svg+=['<path d="M70 45 V245 H570" fill="none" stroke="var(--ink)"/>','<text x="320" y="297" text-anchor="middle" fill="var(--ink)" font-size="14">Free competitor concentration C (µM)</text>','<text transform="translate(19 145) rotate(-90)" text-anchor="middle" fill="var(--ink)" font-size="14">Target-bound fraction</text>']
for kc,color,dash,label in [(0.1,'#cc684c','', 'Kc = 0.1 µM'),(1,'#397bc0','7 3','Kc = 1 µM'),(10,'#338260','2 3','Kc = 10 µM')]:
 pts=' '.join(f'{x:.2f},{y:.2f}' for x,y in (xy(20*i/300,occupancy(20*i/300,kc)) for i in range(301)))
 svg.append(f'<polyline points="{pts}" fill="none" stroke="{color}" stroke-width="3" stroke-dasharray="{dash}"/>')
svg+=['<text x="95" y="65" fill="var(--ink)" font-size="13">Kc: <tspan fill="#cc684c">0.1</tspan> · <tspan fill="#397bc0">1</tspan> · <tspan fill="#338260">10 µM</tspan></text>','</svg>']

from pathlib import Path
Path("competition.svg").write_text("".join(svg))
for c in (0, 1, 10, 20):
    print(c, *(round(occupancy(c, k), 4) for k in KCS))
