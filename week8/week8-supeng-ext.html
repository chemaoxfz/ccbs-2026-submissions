Week 8 Extension: Two relaxation experiments identify binding rates without equilibrium data

Author: Peng Su · Lectures 15-16 · CCBS 2026

One sentence: Equilibrium measurements are not always required to identify a designed binder's kinetics: measuring relaxation at two known ligand concentrations makes 1/τ=konS+koff a straight line whose slope is kon and intercept is koff, so changing the input can remove an identifiability problem that repeated snapshots cannot.

From hidden binding rates to an identifiable experiment

Lecture 15 gives the one site binding model, its solution θ(t)=θ∞(1−e−t/τ), and the relaxation time τ=1/(konS+koff). It also shows that equal Kd=koff/kon can hide different response speeds and therefore motivates time-resolved measurements. These were taken as the backgroud.

Meanwhile, lecture 16 identifies parameter unidentifiability as an obstacle to a mechanistic virtual cell. Therefore, one experimental design claim is that, instead of combining equilibrium occupancy with one transient, vary the known input concentration and use relaxation alone. For this module, two input conditions are sufficient to identify both microscopic rates.

Changing ligand concentration converts kinetics into a line

Define the measured relaxation rate r=1/τ. At each clamped free-ligand concentration S, the Lecture 15 relation becomes

r(S) = 1/τ(S) = konS + koff.

This is a linear experiment, not merely an algebraic rewrite. The ligand concentration is the controlled input, the fitted relaxation rate is the output, and the two mechanistic parameters occupy different geometric roles. Association determines how strongly the rate changes with ligand concentration; dissociation remains when the ligand concentration is extrapolated to zero.

For measurements (S1,r1) and (S2,r2), with S1≠S2,

kon = (r2−r1)/ (S2−S1),    koff = (S2r1−S1r2)/ (S2−S1).

No equilibrium occupancy appears. The input variation breaks the parameter symmetry because changing S changes only the association contribution.

Two concentrations recover the rates; a third tests the model

Consider code-generated relaxation measurements from a binder with kon=0.307 μM−1s−1 and koff=0.154 s−1. The time to reach 90% of equilibrium is t90=τln(10).

The first two rows give a slope of (0.768−0.307)/(2.0−0.5)=0.307 μM−1s−1 and an intercept of 0.154 s−1. The third measurement was not needed to determine the two parameters. Instead, it checks the one-site mechanism: its point should fall on the same line. Systematic curvature would be evidence for a missing state, ligand depletion, cooperativity or another concentration-dependent process.

What changes when ligand concentration is unknown

The observation design determines which parameters exist separately in the data. If θ∞ and τ are measured but S is unknown, define A=konS and B=koff. Then A=θ∞/τ and B=(1−θ∞)/τ. Therefore koff remains identifiable; what cannot be separated is kon from S. The indistinguishable family is

{(kon/a, koff, aS) : a>0}.

This is more precise than saying that “only konS is observed”: the dissociation rate is still recoverable. An independent concentration calibration, or controlled experiments at two known concentrations, removes the remaining scale ambiguity.

Where the straight-line identification breaks

Two relaxation measurements should not be treated as a universal solution to whole-cell unidentifiability. The result assumes a clamped and accurately known free concentration, one independent binding state, a known perturbation time and a single-exponential relaxation that can be estimated without bias. With noisy and sparsely sampled data, the parameters may be structurally identifiable yet remain practically uncertain.

More importantly, curvature in the 1/τ-versus-S plot should not be forced into a line. Such curvature may indicate that the proposed mechanism is incomplete. Larger cellular models can also generate new combinations of unidentifiable parameters even when each isolated module is identifiable. The result should therefore be interpreted as an experimental-design principle rather than a universal theorem: before collecting more measurements of the same output under the same condition, vary an input that gives competing parameters distinguishable mathematical signatures.
