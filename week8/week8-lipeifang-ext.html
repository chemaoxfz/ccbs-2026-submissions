Week 9 — Extension: dynamics is a tax, not a subsidy

Author: fanglipei (20261203497) · Week 9 (2026-10-29) · CCBS 2026 · Lecture 15

One sentence: in the Eyring-plus-κ ledger a classical dynamical effect enters as a prefactor that cannot exceed one, so it can only be a tax; the ledger is worth having not because it bounds that tax — my six-friction scan gives 5.8×, but κ ≤ 1 supplies no lower bound and a more viscous system can be far worse — but because it forces any claim to name its size in kcal/mol (1.37 per decade at 300 K), the channel it acts through, and its timescale.

The point I am adding

The lecture is careful about dynamics by omission, and the notes say why: AlphaFold2 returns a point estimate, recycling and random seeds change the topology (§3.6), "multiple random seeds are not a thermodynamic ensemble", and pLDDT and PAE describe confidence rather than motion (§9). The measurement side has since moved a long way: NMR relaxation dispersion resolves µs–ms exchange, time-resolved serial crystallography catches intermediates from ps to ms, cryo-EM now yields multi-conformer ensembles, and generative emulators produce equilibrium ensembles at a small fraction of molecular-dynamics cost (bioEmu, 2025). "We cannot see the ensemble" is no longer the obstacle.

What is still contested is whether the motions cause catalysis, and that argument is usually conducted in mismatched currencies — kcal/mol of electrostatic preorganisation on one side, promoting vibrations and hydrogen tunnelling on the other. A rate has only one currency: k = κ (kBT/h) exp(−ΔG‡/RT). In classical transition-state theory κ ≤ 1 by construction, because TST already counts every trajectory that crosses the dividing surface once and dynamics can only add recrossings. That much is a sign, not a size: no classical dynamical mechanism can be a subsidy. It is also not the whole ledger — if κ is defined relative to classical TST and allowed to go above one, tunnelling enters the same slot, which is exactly how a quantum effect can be a subsidy. What the ledger cannot tell you is how large either term is.

So I measured the exchange in one model rather than asserting it. A one-dimensional Langevin barrier crossing: V(x) = B(1−x²)² with B = 6 kBT, friction γ from 0.1 to 30 in reduced units, BAOAB integration, and a flux estimator so that unfinished trajectories contribute instead of being dropped. The transmission coefficient runs from 0.79 down to 0.14 over those six frictions — a factor of 5.8, which is 1.05 kcal mol⁻¹, or 4.5% of the 23.3 kcal mol⁻¹ that a 1017-proficient enzyme buys. That last ratio is a comparison of energy scales in this model. It is not an estimate of any enzyme's dynamical share, and the arithmetic below shows why: κ has no universal positive lower bound, so −RT ln κ has no universal finite upper bound, and another system can be far worse.

The exchange rate itself is exact arithmetic and does not depend on the model: one decade of rate is RT ln 10 = 1.37 kcal mol⁻¹ at 300 K. What the simulation adds is a sense of scale, and a demonstration that the scale is not universal. κ ≤ 1 is a ceiling, not a floor; the analytic branch that tracks my two highest-friction points keeps falling, to κ ≈ 0.016 at γ = 300 and 0.0016 at γ = 3000 — a tax of 2.5 and 3.8 kcal mol⁻¹ respectively, and still falling. A glassy active site and a sloppy one can therefore differ by far more than the 5.8× measured here, and nothing in the ceiling stops the difference from growing. Any claim of the form "dynamics costs only a few percent" is a claim about a particular system, and has to be measured on that system.

That leaves the question of which channel a motion acts through, because the two channels obey different bookkeeping. Crossing the barrier is the first: friction and recrossing act while the system is on top of the barrier, which takes picoseconds, and their effect is what κ measures. The fact that a full cycle waits for milliseconds does not exclude this channel — a reaction can wait a millisecond and cross in a picosecond, and the crossing is where the dynamics lives. The second channel is populations: a conformational step that must precede chemistry changes how often the system is in the reactive state, and there the two-step result knet = kconfkchem/(kconf + kchem) applies. It has to be read in both directions: when kconf ≫ kchem the ratio tends to 1 and the motion is pre-equilibrated and invisible; when kconf ≪ kchem the ratio tends to kconf/kchem and the slow mandatory step does not vanish — it becomes the rate, and what you measure as kcat is its barrier, not the chemical one.

So the convention I would adopt is three items per claim: an equivalent barrier change in kcal mol⁻¹, the channel (crossing versus population), and the timescale at which it acts. With that, "dynamics matters" becomes a question with a threshold — does the proposed motion shift ΔG‡, or the population of the reactive state, by more than its error bar? For most systems the honest answer is "unknown", and that is a better position than "missing link".

Work it

The script is one file of Python that needs numpy and matplotlib (and nothing else) and runs in about three minutes. Three parts: a BAOAB integrator for the Langevin equation, a flux estimator k = (arrivals)/(total time at risk) that handles censored trajectories, and arithmetic that converts κ into kcal mol⁻¹. The velocity Ornstein–Uhlenbeck step is integrated exactly, which matters at high friction where a plain Euler step drifts.

The frequency bookkeeping is the part that bit me. For V = B(1−x²)² the curvature is +8B at the minima and −4B at the top, so the prefactor needs ω0 = √(8B) while Grote–Hynes needs ωb = √(4B). A first version used the well frequency in both places; the analytic curve then sat 20–60% above the simulation, and I attributed that residual to the shallow barrier. It was not — it was a factor √2 in a frequency. After the correction the two highest-friction points are within 2% and 15% of the analytic value, and the growing excess of the analytic curve at low friction is the expected failure of the spatial-diffusion formula, not an artefact. That episode is the honest argument for reporting checks rather than only numbers.

Two further checks. The measured κ never exceeds 1 in any run, as the classical ledger requires. And three seeds at fixed friction give κ = 0.381, 0.407, 0.434 — a 13% spread that sets the resolution of the whole measurement, which is why nothing here is quoted to better than a factor.

"""Dynamics in the ledger of the barrier: what the exchange rate can and cannot say.

Three computations, all reported in the essay:
  1. a 1-D Langevin barrier crossing  -> transmission coefficient kappa(friction)
  2. the Eyring exchange rate         -> rate factor <-> kcal/mol at 300 K
  3. a two-step scheme                -> which motional rates gate turnover

Reduced units in the simulation: kT = 1, mass = 1, barrier B = 6 kT,
potential V(x) = B (1 - x^2)^2, minima at x = +-1, barrier at x = 0.
For this potential V''(0) = -4B and V''(+-1) = 8B, so with mass 1
    omega_b = sqrt(4B) = 4.90   (barrier top, used by Grote-Hynes)
    omega_0 = sqrt(8B) = 6.93   (well bottom, used by the TST prefactor)
The two frequencies are not interchangeable; an earlier version of this file
used omega_0 in both places.

Needs numpy and matplotlib.  Writes fig1_kappa.svg and fig2_currency.svg.
"""
import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

B = 6.0                                  # barrier height, in kT
W0 = math.sqrt(8.0*B)                    # well-bottom frequency   (6.93)
WB = math.sqrt(4.0*B)                    # barrier-top frequency   (4.90)
K_TST = W0/(2*math.pi)*math.exp(-B)      # harmonic transition-state rate


def run(gamma, dt=0.005, tmax=4000.0, ntraj=200, seed=1):
    """Flux estimator k = arrivals / total time at risk, so censored runs still count.
    BAOAB splitting; the velocity Ornstein-Uhlenbeck part is integrated exactly."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = -np.ones(ntraj)
    v = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, ntraj)
    t = np.zeros(ntraj)
    done = np.zeros(ntraj, bool)
    c1 = math.exp(-gamma*dt)
    c2 = math.sqrt(1.0 - math.exp(-2*gamma*dt))
    for _ in range(int(tmax/dt)):
        a = ~done
        if not a.any():
            break
        xa, va = x[a], v[a]
        va += 0.5*dt*4*B*xa*(1 - xa*xa)                 # B: half kick
        xa += 0.5*dt*va                                 # A: half drift
        va = c1*va + c2*rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, xa.size)   # O: exact OU step
        xa += 0.5*dt*va                                 # A
        va += 0.5*dt*4*B*xa*(1 - xa*xa)                 # B
        x[a], v[a] = xa, va
        t[a] += dt
        hit = xa > 1.0                                  # first passage into the product well
        idx = np.flatnonzero(a)[hit]
        done[idx] = True
        x[idx] = 1.0
    return done.sum()/t.sum(), int(done.sum())


def kappa_gh(gamma):
    """Grote-Hynes transmission coefficient; the frequency must be the one at the
    barrier top, omega_b, not the well frequency omega_0"""
    return (math.sqrt(gamma*gamma/4 + WB*WB) - gamma/2)/WB


def main():
    gammas = [0.1, 0.3, 1.0, 3.0, 10.0, 30.0]
    print(f"k_TST = {K_TST:.4e}; omega_0 (well) = {W0:.3f}; "
          f"omega_b (barrier) = {WB:.3f}; barrier {B} kT")
    gs, kap = [], []
    for g in gammas:
        k, n = run(g)
        gs.append(g)
        kap.append(k/K_TST)
        print(f"  gamma={g:6.1f}  k={k:.3e}  kappa={k/K_TST:5.3f}"
              f"  (Grote-Hynes {kappa_gh(g):5.3f})  {n}/200 arrived")
    kap = np.array(kap)

    # ---- seed replication at one friction
    rep = [run(10.0, seed=s, ntraj=120)[0]/K_TST for s in (1, 2, 3)]
    print("  gamma=10, three seeds: kappa = " + ", ".join(f"{r:.3f}" for r in rep)
          + f"   (spread {100*(max(rep)-min(rep))/np.mean(rep):.0f}%)")

    # ---- exchange rate between a rate factor and a barrier change
    RT = 1.987e-3*300.0
    per_decade = RT*math.log(10)
    prof = 17*math.log(10)*RT
    span = -RT*math.log(kap.min()/kap.max())
    print(f"\nRT(300 K) = {RT:.4f} kcal/mol;  10x rate = {per_decade:.3f} kcal/mol")
    print(f"this model: kappa {kap.min():.3f}..{kap.max():.3f} over six frictions "
          f"= a factor {kap.max()/kap.min():.1f} = {span:.2f} kcal/mol")
    print("  kappa has no universal positive lower bound, so -RT ln kappa has no")
    print("  universal upper bound; here the analytic branch keeps falling:")
    for g in (300.0, 3000.0):
        print(f"    gamma={g:7.0f} -> Grote-Hynes kappa = {kappa_gh(g):.4f}")
    print(f"10^17 rate enhancement = {prof:.1f} kcal/mol, so the span above is "
          f"{100*span/prof:.1f}% of that scale")

    # ---- two-step scheme
    xr = np.logspace(-2, 3.5, 200)
    ratio = xr/(1 + xr)
    print("\ntwo-step: k_net/k_chem = x/(1+x) with x = k_conf/k_chem")
    for xx in (9, 99, 1000):
        print(f"  x = {xx:6.0f} -> {100*xx/(1+xx):5.1f}%")
    print("  and as k_conf/k_chem -> 0 the ratio -> 0: a slow mandatory step "
          "takes over the rate")

    INK, ACC, MUT, CORAL, GRID = "#5d6e7b", "#2f6f9f", "#8e9ca7", "#b8544c", "#c9d2d9"
    plt.rcParams.update({"svg.fonttype": "path", "font.size": 10, "font.family": "DejaVu Sans"})

    # ---- figure 1: measured coefficient, with the analytic branch
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.6, 3.05))
    gg = np.logspace(0, 1.7, 120)
    ax.plot(gg, [kappa_gh(g) for g in gg], "-", color=MUT, lw=1.3,
            label="Grote-Hynes, $\\omega_b$ = 4.90")
    ax.plot(gs, kap, "o", color=CORAL, ms=6.5, label="Langevin simulation")
    ax.axhline(1.0, color=INK, lw=0.8, ls=(0, (5, 3)))
    ax.text(0.105, 1.03, r"$\kappa = 1$: the classical ceiling", fontsize=9,
            color=INK, va="bottom")
    ax.annotate("largest $\\kappa$ in\nthis six-point scan", xy=(1.0, 0.789),
                xytext=(0.115, 0.33), fontsize=9, color=CORAL,
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color=CORAL, lw=0.9))
    ax.set_xscale("log")
    ax.set_yscale("log")
    ax.set_xlabel(r"friction $\gamma$ (reduced units, $m = k_BT = 1$)", color=INK, fontsize=10)
    ax.set_ylabel(r"transmission coefficient $\kappa$", color=INK, fontsize=10)
    ax.set_xlim(0.08, 55)
    ax.set_ylim(0.09, 1.5)
    ax.grid(True, color=GRID, lw=0.5, alpha=0.6)
    ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9, loc="lower left", labelcolor=INK)
    for sp in ("top", "right"):
        ax.spines[sp].set_visible(False)
    for sp in ("bottom", "left"):
        ax.spines[sp].set_color(GRID)
    ax.tick_params(colors=INK, labelsize=9, length=3)
    fig.subplots_adjust(left=0.10, right=0.98, top=0.97, bottom=0.17)
    fig.savefig("fig1_kappa.svg", transparent=True)
    plt.close(fig)

    # ---- figure 2: the currency, and the two-step window
    fig, (a1, a2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(7.6, 3.05))
    dg = np.linspace(0, 25, 300)
    a1.plot(dg, dg/per_decade, color=ACC, lw=1.6)
    a1.plot([span], [span/per_decade], "o", color=CORAL, ms=6.5)
    a1.annotate("this model:\n%.1f kcal/mol, %.0f\u00d7" % (span, kap.max()/kap.min()),
                xy=(span, span/per_decade), xytext=(7.2, 1.3), fontsize=9, color=CORAL,
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color=CORAL, lw=0.9))
    a1.plot([prof], [17], "s", color=INK, ms=6.5)
    a1.annotate("10$^{17}$ proficiency\n= %.0f kcal/mol" % prof,
                xy=(prof, 17), xytext=(4.6, 18.8), fontsize=9, color=INK,
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color=INK, lw=0.9))
    a1.set_xlabel(r"barrier change $\Delta\Delta G^\ddagger$ / kcal mol$^{-1}$",
                  color=INK, fontsize=10)
    a1.set_ylabel(r"rate factor $\log_{10}$", color=INK, fontsize=10)
    a1.set_xlim(0, 25)
    a1.set_ylim(0, 22.5)
    a1.grid(True, color=GRID, lw=0.5, alpha=0.6)

    a2.plot(xr, 100*ratio, color=ACC, lw=1.6)
    a2.axvspan(1e-2, 9, color=CORAL, alpha=0.10, lw=0)
    a2.text(0.014, 46, "gates", color=CORAL, fontsize=9)
    a2.text(0.011, 26, "slow mandatory\nstep takes over", color=CORAL, fontsize=9,
            ha="left", va="top")
    a2.plot([1e3], [100*1e3/(1+1e3)], "o", color=CORAL, ms=6.5)
    a2.text(780, 90, r"$\mu$s vs ms", fontsize=9, color=CORAL, ha="right")
    a2.set_xscale("log")
    a2.set_xlabel(r"$k_\mathrm{conf}/k_\mathrm{chem}$", color=INK, fontsize=10)
    a2.set_ylabel(r"$k_\mathrm{net}/k_\mathrm{chem}$ / %", color=INK, fontsize=10)

    a2.text(60, 13, "pre-equilibrated:\ninvisible", color=MUT, fontsize=9, ha="center")

    for ax in (a1, a2):
        for sp in ("top", "right"):

What I would argue against / where this breaks

There is no universal size for the tax. This is the strongest objection to the essay's first draft, and it is fatal to the strong reading. Six frictions in one toy potential cannot bound what dynamics does in general; κ ≤ 1 gives a ceiling but no floor, and the same formula that fits my data predicts κ ≈ 0.002 at a hundredfold higher friction. The 1.05 kcal mol⁻¹ and the 4.5% are properties of this example. What survives is the exchange rate and the sign.

Turnover time is not crossing time. I used to slide from "the cycle takes a millisecond" to "femtosecond motions cannot matter". That does not follow: the waiting time can be milliseconds while the barrier crossing takes picoseconds, and friction, recrossing and barrier-shape effects act during the crossing. The timescale argument is valid only for the population channel. For the crossing channel the relevant comparison is with the barrier-relaxation and recrossing times, not with kcat.

Slow motions are not "invisible" either. The two-step scheme is asymmetric and my earlier sentence — that motions much slower than turnover cannot rate-limit — contradicted the formula it was based on. As kconf/kchem → 0 the ratio goes to zero: the motion becomes the rate. What is true is narrower and still useful: for a mandatory step, a slow motion replaces the chemical barrier rather than modifying it, so it appears as a different transition state, not as a correction to kchem. The formula says nothing about a step that is not mandatory, and I should not have implied it does: a non-mandatory motion can still shift the equilibrium population of the active state, open a parallel path, or — on longer times — push the enzyme into an inactive form.

Tunnelling fits the ledger after all. I wrote that Eyring-plus-κ does not apply to hydrogen tunnelling. Better: define κ relative to classical TST and let it be a quantum correction; for a parabolic barrier the tunnelling correction grows above one as the barrier narrows. Then the tunnelling literature and my bookkeeping are the same bookkeeping, with the sign of the correction flipped. That is a stronger claim than the one I made, and a more useful one for the reader.

Barrier and friction are not separable. The same electrostatic environment that lowers ΔG‡ changes the friction along the coordinate, so splitting a rate into "barrier" and "dynamics" is bookkeeping no experiment performs. That is why it is worth doing: it tells you the size of the term being argued about. But a reader should not mistake the split for two independent physical mechanisms.

The model is one-dimensional and the barrier is shallow. Six kBT keeps the run affordable and makes the harmonic reference shaky; a real enzyme has a rough landscape and a diffusive coordinate, and κ there could be much smaller than 0.14. Smaller κ strengthens the "tax" reading but also means these numbers demonstrate an exchange rate, not an enzyme. Equilibrium averaging likewise presupposes that the coordinates in question are sampled on the timescale of the measurement, which for a glassy site they may not be.

I am arguing about rates, not about importance. Dynamics is central to specificity, allostery, evolution and drug binding, and none of that is in this essay. A reader could accept every number here and still be right to study motions — just not as a universal explanation of catalytic power.
