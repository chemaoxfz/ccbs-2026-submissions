The hidden load on a designed regulator

Author: Li Changkai· Lectures 15 & 16 · 9 November 2026

One sentence: Two designed transcription factors can work identically at their intended promoter yet respond at different speeds in cells, because off-target DNA sites exert a retroactive load that is strongest when their dissociation constant matches the operating concentration.

The point I am adding

Lecture 15 separates target binding from cellular function; Lecture 16 asks whether separately characterized parts compose into a predictive virtual cell.[1] A specific reason composition can fail is retroactivity: DNA sites downstream of a transcription factor (TF) can absorb and release it, thereby changing the upstream TF dynamics even if those sites do not directly control a gene.[2] Decoy-site effects have been seen in yeast and in the bacterial PhoB system.[3], [4]

Here is a deliberately sharper prediction: the strongest possible decoy binding does not necessarily give the longest delay. For a TF with a fixed free concentration, maximum dynamic loading occurs at an intermediate dissociation constant. This is a toy-model prediction, not a measurement of any designed protein.

Work it: binding adds a hidden storage pool

Let x be free TF, C be TF bound to Dtot decoy sites, s the TF production rate and γ the loss rate of free TF. Assume a fixed decoy pool, fast reversible 1:1 binding, and that decoy-bound TF is protected from degradation over the modeled interval. With Kd = koff/kon, fast equilibrium gives

C(x) = Dtotx / (Kd + x)Decoy occupancy under the fast-binding approximation

Production and free-protein loss change the total TF, not just the free pool: d(x + C)/dt = s − γx. Thus, near the steady state x* = s/γ,

(1 + DtotKd / (Kd + x)2) dx/dt = s − γx
τeff = [1 + DtotKd / (Kd + x*)2] / γLinearized response time for small perturbations around the same free-TF set point

The extra multiplier is a binding capacitance. At fixed x*, its derivative with respect to Kd changes sign at Kd = x*, exactly where half the decoy sites are occupied. Very weak binding stores almost nothing; extremely tight binding saturates sites and makes their occupancy insensitive to a small change in free TF.

One reproducible result

Choose illustrative, not experimentally fitted, values x* = 100 nM, Dtot = 1,000 nM and γ = 0.1 min−1. For a small change in TF production, the calculated response times are:

10 minTime constant without decoys
35 minAt decoy Kd = 100 nM
3.5×Total delay vs. unloaded response

The implication for a virtual-cell model is practical: matching only the intended promoter's binding affinity is not enough. To rank two protein designs for cellular performance, we also need their off-target binding spectrum, site abundances, on/off timescales and bound-state turnover. Those measurements determine whether their molecular scores survive composition.

What I would argue against / where this breaks

I would argue against the unconditional claim that “decoys always slow the cell, so weaker binding is always better.” The conclusion above requires fast equilibration, small perturbations, protected bound protein and a fixed number of sites. If bound and free TF instead decay at the same first-order rate, then d(x + C)/dt = s − γ(x + C): the total-pool relaxation time becomes 1/γ, although its steady-state free concentration changes. Indeed, Lee and Maheshri infer approximately equal loss rates for their tested yeast construct,[3] so my 35-minute curve must not be presented as their observation. Without kinetic and turnover measurements, this curve is a falsifiable hypothesis rather than a design guarantee.

"""Recreate the illustration in the CCBS Week 8 mini-essay.
Python 3 standard library only. All parameters are illustrative.
Run: python3 reproduce_figure.py
Output: week8-figure.svg
"""
import math
from xml.sax.saxutils import escape

D = 1000.0       # nM: total decoy-site concentration
x_star = 100.0  # nM: operating free regulator concentration
gamma = 0.1     # min^-1: degradation rate of free regulator

def tau(kd):
    # Fast equilibrium; bound regulator protected from degradation.
    return (1.0 + D * kd / (kd + x_star)**2) / gamma

def fraction(t, time_constant):
    # Fraction of a small production-step response completed at time t.
    return 1.0 - math.exp(-t / time_constant)

assert abs(tau(100) - 35.0) < 1e-10
assert abs(tau(10) - 18.264462809917354) < 1e-10
assert abs(fraction(20, 35) - 0.4352818779922407) < 1e-10
assert abs(fraction(20, 10) - 0.8646647167633873) < 1e-10

W, H = 780, 360
parts = [f'<svg class="fig" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" viewBox="0 0 {W} {H}" '
         'role="img" aria-labelledby="fig-title fig-desc">',
         '<title id="fig-title">Binding-site load slows a regulator</title>',
         '<desc id="fig-desc">Left: time constant peaks at Kd of 100 nM. '
         'Right: step response without decoys is faster than with matching-affinity decoys.</desc>',
         '<rect width="780" height="360" rx="14" fill="#fbfdff"/>']

def line(x1,y1,x2,y2,color='#dce5ec',stroke=1,dash=''):
    d = f' stroke-dasharray="{dash}"' if dash else ''
    parts.append(f'<line x1="{x1:.2f}" y1="{y1:.2f}" x2="{x2:.2f}" y2="{y2:.2f}" stroke="{color}" stroke-width="{stroke}"{d}/>')

def txt(x,y,string,size=12,color='#53677b',anchor='start',weight=400):
    parts.append(f'<text x="{x:.2f}" y="{y:.2f}" font-family="Arial,Helvetica,sans-serif" '
                 f'font-size="{size}" font-weight="{weight}" fill="{color}" text-anchor="{anchor}">'
                 f'{escape(str(string))}</text>')

def circle(x,y,r,fill,stroke='#ffffff'):
    parts.append(f'<circle cx="{x:.2f}" cy="{y:.2f}" r="{r}" fill="{fill}" stroke="{stroke}" stroke-width="2"/>')

def path(points,color,width=3,dash=''):
    d = 'M' + ' L'.join(f'{x:.2f},{y:.2f}' for x,y in points)
    dashattr = f' stroke-dasharray="{dash}"' if dash else ''
    parts.append(f'<path d="{d}" fill="none" stroke="{color}" stroke-width="{width}" '
                 f'stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round"{dashattr}/>')

blue, orange, green = '#17617c', '#c7682c', '#087f70'
txt(38,36,'A  Affinity-dependent delay',16,'#163651',weight=700)
txt(410,36,'B  Response to a small step',16,'#163651',weight=700)

# Panel A: logarithmic decoy Kd from 1 to 10000 nM.
left, right, top, bottom = 54, 361, 68, 283
ax = lambda kd: left + (math.log10(kd) / 4.0) * (right-left)
ay = lambda t: bottom - (t-10.0) / 26.0 * (bottom-top)
for val in (10,20,30,35):
    y=ay(val); line(left,y,right,y)
    txt(left-9,y+4,str(val),11,anchor='end')
for val in (1,10,100,1000,10000):
    x=ax(val); line(x,top,x,bottom,'#edf1f5')
    txt(x,bottom+21,str(val),10,anchor='middle')
line(left,top,left,bottom,'#8393a3',1.2)
line(left,bottom,right,bottom,'#8393a3',1.2)
line(ax(100),top,ax(100),bottom,'#c7682c',1.3,'5 5')
points=[(ax(10**(4*i/300)),ay(tau(10**(4*i/300)))) for i in range(301)]
path(points,blue,3.5)
circle(ax(100),ay(35),5.8,orange)
txt(ax(100)+12,ay(35)-9,'35 min',12,orange,weight=700)
txt((left+right)/2,bottom+46,'Decoy Kd (nM; log scale)',12,'#374b61',anchor='middle')

    y=by(val); line(left2,y,right2,y)
    txt(left2-8,y+4,f'{int(val*100)}%',10,anchor='end')
for val in (0,20,40,60,80):
